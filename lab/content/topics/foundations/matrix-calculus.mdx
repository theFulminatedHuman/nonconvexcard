Optimisation theory is calculus with inequalities attached. The assumptions that appear in every
convergence theorem later — $L$-smoothness, $\mu$-strong convexity, bounded gradients — are all
statements about the objects defined here, and the descent lemma proved below is used in essentially
every proof on the <TopicRef id="optimization/gradient-descent-convergence">gradient descent</TopicRef>
page.

## Derivatives as linear maps

<Definition id="differentiable" title="Differentiability">
$f : \R^d \to \R^m$ is **differentiable** at $x$ if there is a linear map
$Df(x) : \R^d \to \R^m$ with
$$
f(x+h) = f(x) + Df(x)h + o(\norm{h}) \quad \text{as } h \to 0 .
$$
In coordinates $Df(x)$ is the **Jacobian** $J \in \R^{m \times d}$ with
$J_{ij} = \partial f_i/\partial x_j$. When $m=1$ we write the derivative as a column vector, the
**gradient** $\nabla f(x) \in \R^d$, so that $Df(x)h = \ip{\nabla f(x)}{h}$.
</Definition>

<Intuition>
The gradient is not "the direction of steepest ascent" by definition — it is the unique vector that
*represents* the derivative as an inner product, and steepest ascent is then a one-line consequence
of Cauchy–Schwarz: among unit $h$, $\ip{\nabla f(x)}{h}$ is largest when $h$ points along
$\nabla f(x)$. The distinction matters because the representation depends on the inner product, and
changing the inner product changes the "steepest" direction. That observation is the whole idea
behind mirror descent and natural gradient methods.
</Intuition>

<Definition id="directional" title="Directional derivative">
The derivative of $f$ at $x$ along $v$ is
$$
D_v f(x) = \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \frac{f(x+tv) - f(x)}{t} = \ip{\nabla f(x)}{v},
$$
the last equality holding when $f$ is differentiable at $x$. A function can have all directional
derivatives at a point without being differentiable there; $f(x_1,x_2) = x_1^3/(x_1^2+x_2^2)$ at the
origin is the standard example.
</Definition>

<Definition id="hessian" title="Hessian">
If $\nabla f$ is itself differentiable at $x$, its Jacobian is the **Hessian**
$\nabla^2 f(x) \in \R^{d\times d}$ with $[\nabla^2 f(x)]_{ij} = \partial^2 f/\partial x_i \partial x_j$.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="symmetry-hessian" title="Symmetry of the Hessian">
If the second partial derivatives of $f$ exist and are continuous in a neighbourhood of $x$, then
$\nabla^2 f(x)$ is symmetric.
</Theorem>

This is Clairaut–Schwarz; the proof is in any analysis text and is not reproduced here. The
consequence we use constantly is that the spectral theorem applies to Hessians, so "curvature" is a
list of real eigenvalues, and the second-order conditions for a minimum become eigenvalue conditions.

## Taylor expansion and the descent lemma

<Theorem id="taylor-quadratic" title="Taylor's theorem with integral remainder">
Let $f : \R^d \to \R$ be twice continuously differentiable. For all $x, y$,
$$
f(y) = f(x) + \ip{\nabla f(x)}{y - x} + \int_0^1 (1-t)\,(y-x)^\top \nabla^2 f\big(x + t(y-x)\big)(y-x)\,dt .
$$
In particular there is $\xi$ on the segment $[x,y]$ with
$f(y) = f(x) + \ip{\nabla f(x)}{y-x} + \tfrac12 (y-x)^\top\nabla^2 f(\xi)(y-x)$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Fix $x,y$ and set $g(t) = f(x + t(y-x))$ for $t \in [0,1]$, so $g$ is twice continuously
differentiable with
$$
g'(t) = \ip{\nabla f(x+t(y-x))}{y-x}, \qquad
g''(t) = (y-x)^\top \nabla^2 f(x+t(y-x))(y-x)
$$
by the chain rule. Integrating by parts,
$$
g(1) - g(0) - g'(0) = \int_0^1 \big(g'(t) - g'(0)\big)dt = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^t g''(s)\,ds\,dt
= \int_0^1 (1-s) g''(s)\,ds,
$$
where the last step swaps the order of integration over the triangle $\{0 \le s \le t \le 1\}$.
Substituting the expressions for $g$, $g'$, $g''$ gives the integral form. The mean-value form
follows because $\int_0^1(1-t)\,dt = \tfrac12$ and $t \mapsto g''(t)$ is continuous, so it attains its
average value.
</Proof>

<Definition id="smooth-def" title="L-smoothness">
$f$ is **$L$-smooth** if $\nabla f$ is $L$-Lipschitz:
$\norm{\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)}_2 \le L\norm{x-y}_2$ for all $x,y$. For twice-differentiable $f$
this is equivalent to $-L I \preceq \nabla^2 f(x) \preceq L I$ for all $x$.
</Definition>

<Lemma id="descent-lemma" title="Descent lemma">
If $f$ is $L$-smooth then for all $x,y$,
$$
\big| f(y) - f(x) - \ip{\nabla f(x)}{y-x} \big| \le \frac{L}{2}\norm{y-x}_2^2 .
$$
In particular $f(y) \le f(x) + \ip{\nabla f(x)}{y-x} + \frac{L}{2}\norm{y-x}_2^2$.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
Write $f(y) - f(x) = \int_0^1 \ip{\nabla f(x + t(y-x))}{y-x}\,dt$ (the fundamental theorem of
calculus applied to $g$ as above; this needs only differentiability). Subtract the linear model:
$$
f(y) - f(x) - \ip{\nabla f(x)}{y-x} = \int_0^1 \ip{\nabla f(x+t(y-x)) - \nabla f(x)}{y-x}\,dt .
$$
Bound the integrand with Cauchy–Schwarz and then with Lipschitzness of the gradient:
$$
\big|\ip{\nabla f(x+t(y-x)) - \nabla f(x)}{y-x}\big|
\le L\,\norm{t(y-x)}_2\norm{y-x}_2 = Lt\norm{y-x}_2^2 .
$$
Integrating $Lt$ over $t\in[0,1]$ gives $L/2$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The descent lemma converts a *global* assumption about the gradient into a *usable quadratic upper
bound* on the function. Every proof of a gradient-descent rate in this laboratory begins by
minimising that upper bound, which is why the step size $1/L$ appears everywhere: it is the exact
minimiser of $t \mapsto f(x) - t\norm{\nabla f(x)}^2 + \frac{L t^2}{2}\norm{\nabla f(x)}^2$.
</KeyIdea>

<Warning title="Common mistake">
$L$-smoothness is a condition on the *gradient*, not on the function values, and it is not implied by
differentiability. $f(x) = |x|^{3/2}$ is differentiable everywhere but not $L$-smooth near 0 for any
finite $L$; $f(x) = \norm{x}_2$ is not differentiable at 0 at all. Neural network losses with ReLU
activations are not $L$-smooth either, which is why the theorems on the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">deep learning theory</TopicRef> page have to say
what they assume instead.
</Warning>

## The chain rule and reverse-mode differentiation

<Theorem id="chain-rule" title="Chain rule">
If $g : \R^d \to \R^m$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f : \R^m \to \R^k$ is differentiable at $g(x)$,
then $f \circ g$ is differentiable at $x$ with
$$
D(f\circ g)(x) = Df(g(x)) \cdot Dg(x) .
$$
</Theorem>

Consider a composition of $n$ maps, as in a feedforward network:
$$
f = f_n \circ f_{n-1} \circ \dots \circ f_1, \qquad
x_0 = x,\quad x_i = f_i(x_{i-1}) .
$$
The chain rule gives $Df(x) = J_n J_{n-1}\cdots J_1$ with $J_i = Df_i(x_{i-1})$. Matrix
multiplication is associative, so this product may be evaluated in any order — and the order is
exactly the difference between forward and reverse mode.

<Algorithm title="Reverse-mode differentiation (backpropagation)">
<ol>
<li><strong>Forward pass.</strong> Compute and store $x_1, \dots, x_n$.</li>
<li><strong>Seed.</strong> Set $\bar{x}_n = \nabla_{x_n} \mathcal{L}$, the gradient of the scalar objective with respect to the output.</li>
<li><strong>Backward pass.</strong> For $i = n, n-1, \dots, 1$: set $\bar{x}_{i-1} = J_i^\top \bar{x}_i$ and accumulate the gradient with respect to the parameters of layer $i$ as $(\partial x_i/\partial \theta_i)^\top \bar{x}_i$.</li>
<li><strong>Return</strong> $\bar{x}_0$ and the accumulated parameter gradients.</li>
</ol>
</Algorithm>

<Proposition id="reverse-cost" title="Cost of reverse mode">
For a scalar-valued $f$ built from $n$ elementary operations, reverse mode computes the full gradient
at a cost within a constant factor of one function evaluation, using memory proportional to the number
of stored intermediates. Forward mode costs one function evaluation *per input coordinate*.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Associate the product $J_n \cdots J_1$ from the left. Each step multiplies a row vector (the
transposed adjoint, of length $m_i$) by a Jacobian, which for an elementary operation costs $O(1)$
because elementary Jacobians are sparse and structured. There are $n$ such steps, so the total is
$O(n)$ — the same order as the forward evaluation. Associating from the right instead propagates a
$d$-column matrix, costing $O(nd)$.

The memory statement is immediate: the backward pass needs $x_{i-1}$ to evaluate $J_i$, so the
intermediates must be retained (or recomputed, which is the gradient-checkpointing trade).
</Proof>

<Note title="Why this is the reason deep learning is practical">
A modern network has $d \sim 10^9$ parameters and one scalar loss. Forward mode would need $10^9$
passes; reverse mode needs one, at the price of storing the activations. The asymmetry is entirely
about the shape of the problem — many inputs, one output — and it is a statement about matrix
multiplication order, not about neural networks.
</Note>

## Matrix derivative identities

Throughout, $x \in \R^d$, $A \in \R^{d\times d}$, $X \in \R^{n \times p}$, and all gradients are with
respect to the indicated variable.

| Function | Derivative | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $a^\top x$ | $a$ | |
| $x^\top A x$ | $(A + A^\top)x$ | $2Ax$ if $A$ symmetric |
| $\tfrac12\norm{Ax - b}_2^2$ | $A^\top(Ax-b)$ | normal equations set this to zero |
| $\norm{x}_2$ | $x/\norm{x}_2$ | undefined at $x=0$ |
| $\log\det X$ | $X^{-\top}$ | $X \succ 0$ |
| $\tr(AX)$ | $A^\top$ | with respect to $X$ |
| $\tr(X^\top A X)$ | $(A + A^\top)X$ | |
| $\norm{X}_F^2$ | $2X$ | |
| $X^{-1}$ | $-X^{-1}(dX)X^{-1}$ | as a differential |
| $\sigma(x)$, $\sigma(t)=\tfrac{1}{1+e^{-t}}$ | $\diag\!\big(\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))\big)$ | elementwise |
| $\operatorname{softmax}(z)$ | $\diag(p) - pp^\top$, $p = \operatorname{softmax}(z)$ | PSD, singular on $\one$ |

<Derivation title="The softmax Jacobian, since it is used in every LLM derivation">
<Step why="Definition.">
$p_i = e^{z_i}/\sum_k e^{z_k}$.
</Step>
<Step why="Differentiate the quotient, separating the i = j and i ≠ j cases.">
$$
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j}
= \frac{\delta_{ij}e^{z_i}\sum_k e^{z_k} - e^{z_i}e^{z_j}}{\left(\sum_k e^{z_k}\right)^2}
= \delta_{ij}p_i - p_ip_j .
$$
</Step>
<Step why="Collect into matrix form.">
$\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial z} = \diag(p) - pp^\top$.
</Step>
<Step why="The Jacobian is PSD and annihilates the all-ones vector, because shifting all logits by a constant does not change the distribution.">
For any $v$, $v^\top(\diag(p) - pp^\top)v = \E_{i \sim p}[v_i^2] - (\E_{i\sim p}[v_i])^2 = \Var_{i\sim p}(v_i) \ge 0$, and the expression vanishes for $v = \one$.
</Step>
</Derivation>

That last line is worth keeping: the softmax Jacobian *is* a variance under the predicted
distribution. It is why gradients vanish when the model is confident — a confident $p$ has small
variance — and it reappears as the Fisher information of the categorical family in the
<TopicRef id="llm/language-modelling-and-cross-entropy">language modelling</TopicRef> derivations.
