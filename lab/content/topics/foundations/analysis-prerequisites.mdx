This page is a short reference, not a course. It states the analysis facts that later proofs use
without comment, so that a reader who wants to check a step has somewhere to look. Where a result is
standard and long, it is stated with a citation rather than proved — and marked as such.

## Suprema and infima

<Definition id="sup-def" title="Supremum">
For $S \subseteq \R$ non-empty and bounded above, $\sup S$ is the least upper bound: $\sup S \ge s$
for all $s \in S$, and no smaller number has that property. It need not belong to $S$. By convention
$\sup \emptyset = -\infty$ and $\sup S = +\infty$ when $S$ is unbounded above.
</Definition>

Two facts are used repeatedly in learning theory, where the object of interest is
$\sup_{f \in \Fcal} \big(R(f) - \hat R_n(f)\big)$ over a function class $\Fcal$:

$$
\sup_{x}\big(g(x) + h(x)\big) \le \sup_x g(x) + \sup_x h(x), \qquad
\Big|\sup_x g(x) - \sup_x h(x)\Big| \le \sup_x |g(x) - h(x)| .
$$

The first is subadditivity; the second says the supremum is a $1$-Lipschitz functional in the uniform
norm, and it is what makes the bounded-differences argument behind
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-inequalities">McDiarmid's inequality</TopicRef>
apply to suprema at all.

## Continuity and Lipschitz continuity

<Definition id="lipschitz" title="Lipschitz continuity">
$f : \Xcal \to \R$ on a metric space $(\Xcal, \rho)$ is **$K$-Lipschitz** if
$|f(x) - f(y)| \le K\rho(x,y)$ for all $x,y$. The smallest such $K$ is $\norm{f}_{\mathrm{Lip}}$.
</Definition>

Lipschitz continuity is the regularity assumption of choice in high-dimensional probability, for a
specific reason: it is exactly what is needed for a function of many independent inputs to
concentrate, and the resulting bounds do not degrade with dimension. Compare:

- $f(x) = \max_i x_i$ is $1$-Lipschitz in $\norm{\cdot}_2$ on $\R^d$ — uniformly in $d$.
- $f(x) = \sum_i x_i$ is $\sqrt d$-Lipschitz.
- $f(x) = \norm{x}_2$ is $1$-Lipschitz, by the triangle inequality.

For differentiable $f$, $\norm{f}_{\mathrm{Lip}} = \sup_x \norm{\nabla f(x)}_2$, which is the bridge
between the analytic condition and the calculus.

<Note title="Lipschitz in which metric?">
The constant depends on the metric, and sloppiness here produces wrong dimension dependence. A
function that is $1$-Lipschitz in $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$ can be $\sqrt d$-Lipschitz in
$\norm{\cdot}_2$. Every Lipschitz assumption on this site names its norm.
</Note>

## Convexity inequalities

<Theorem id="jensen" title="Jensen's inequality">
Let $\varphi : \R \to \R$ be convex and $X$ an integrable random variable with
$\E|\varphi(X)| < \infty$. Then
$$
\varphi(\E X) \le \E[\varphi(X)] .
$$
If $\varphi$ is strictly convex, equality holds if and only if $X$ is almost surely constant.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Let $m = \E X$. Convexity gives a supporting line at $m$: there is $c \in \R$ (any subgradient of
$\varphi$ at $m$) with
$$
\varphi(t) \ge \varphi(m) + c(t - m) \quad \text{for all } t \in \R .
$$
Substitute $t = X$ and take expectations. The linear term has expectation
$c(\E X - m) = 0$, leaving $\E[\varphi(X)] \ge \varphi(m)$.

For the equality case with $\varphi$ strictly convex, the supporting line touches $\varphi$ only at
$m$, so $\varphi(X) - \varphi(m) - c(X-m)$ is a non-negative random variable that is strictly
positive whenever $X \ne m$. Its expectation is zero exactly when $X = m$ almost surely.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The supporting-line trick — replace a convex function by a linear lower bound, then take
expectations — is one of the two or three most reused moves in this laboratory. It proves Jensen
here, it proves the variational characterisation of the KL divergence, and it is the first step of
the Chernoff method.
</KeyIdea>

<Theorem id="holder" title="Hölder's inequality">
For $p, q \in [1,\infty]$ with $1/p + 1/q = 1$,
$$
\E|XY| \le \norm{X}_{L^p}\,\norm{Y}_{L^q}, \qquad \norm{X}_{L^p} = \big(\E|X|^p\big)^{1/p} .
$$
The case $p = q = 2$ is Cauchy–Schwarz.
</Theorem>

Hölder is quoted, not proved here; see the references. The consequence used most often is the
monotonicity of $L^p$ norms, $\norm{X}_{L^p} \le \norm{X}_{L^q}$ for $p \le q$, which is why
convergence in $L^2$ implies convergence in $L^1$ but not conversely.

## Limits and integrals

<Theorem id="fatou-mct" title="Monotone and dominated convergence">
Let $X_n \to X$ pointwise almost surely.

- **Monotone convergence.** If $0 \le X_1 \le X_2 \le \cdots$ then $\E X_n \to \E X$ (both sides possibly $+\infty$).
- **Dominated convergence.** If $|X_n| \le Y$ for all $n$ with $\E Y < \infty$, then $\E X_n \to \E X$.
- **Fatou.** If $X_n \ge 0$ then $\E[\liminf_n X_n] \le \liminf_n \E X_n$.
</Theorem>

These are quoted from measure theory. They appear whenever a proof differentiates under an
expectation — for instance when showing that a moment generating function is smooth inside its
domain, a step used silently in the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/sub-gaussian-random-variables">sub-Gaussian</TopicRef>
computations.

<Warning title="Common mistake">
Exchanging a limit and an expectation without checking a hypothesis is the single most common gap in
otherwise correct probability arguments. $X_n = n\one\{U < 1/n\}$ with $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$ satisfies
$X_n \to 0$ almost surely while $\E X_n = 1$ for every $n$: pointwise convergence alone gives nothing.
</Warning>

## Compactness and existence of minimisers

<Theorem id="extreme-value" title="Extreme value theorem">
A continuous real-valued function on a compact subset of $\R^d$ attains its supremum and infimum.
</Theorem>

In $\R^d$, compact means closed and bounded (Heine–Borel). This theorem is what the proof of the
<TopicRef id="foundations/linear-algebra">spectral theorem</TopicRef> uses to produce the top
eigenvector, and it is the reason optimisation theory cares about **coercivity**: if
$f(x) \to \infty$ as $\norm{x} \to \infty$, then the sublevel set $\{f \le f(x_0)\}$ is bounded,
closed by continuity, hence compact, so a minimiser exists. Without coercivity a continuous convex
function need not have a minimiser at all — $f(x) = e^{x}$ on $\R$ is the one-line counterexample.
