Everything later in this laboratory is written in the language of this page. A concentration
inequality for random matrices is a statement about $\|A\|$; a convergence rate for gradient
descent is a statement about $\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2 f)$ and $\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^2 f)$; the
kernel trick is a statement about Gram matrices; attention is a statement about the geometry of
inner products. This page fixes notation and proves the five or six facts that get used without
comment from here on.

## Inner products, norms and orthogonality

We work in $\R^d$ with the standard inner product $\ip{x}{y} = \sum_{i=1}^d x_i y_i = x^\top y$ and
the Euclidean norm $\norm{x}_2 = \sqrt{\ip{x}{x}}$.

<Definition id="norm-defs" title="Norms on vectors">
For $p \in [1,\infty)$ the $\ell^p$ norm is $\norm{x}_p = \left(\sum_i |x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}$, and
$\norm{x}_\infty = \max_i |x_i|$. Each satisfies

- $\norm{x} \ge 0$, with $\norm{x} = 0$ only for $x = 0$ (positivity),
- $\norm{cx} = |c|\,\norm{x}$ (homogeneity),
- $\norm{x+y} \le \norm{x} + \norm{y}$ (triangle inequality).

Only $p=2$ comes from an inner product, which is why the Euclidean norm is the one with a geometry —
angles, projections, orthogonality — attached to it.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="cauchy-schwarz" title="Cauchy–Schwarz inequality">
For all $x, y \in \R^d$,
$$
|\ip{x}{y}| \le \norm{x}_2 \norm{y}_2 ,
$$
with equality if and only if $x$ and $y$ are linearly dependent.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
If $y = 0$ both sides vanish. Otherwise, for every $t \in \R$,
$$
0 \le \norm{x - ty}_2^2 = \norm{x}_2^2 - 2t\ip{x}{y} + t^2\norm{y}_2^2 .
$$
The right-hand side is a quadratic in $t$ that is non-negative everywhere, so its discriminant is
non-positive:
$$
4\ip{x}{y}^2 - 4\norm{x}_2^2\norm{y}_2^2 \le 0 ,
$$
which is the claim. Equality forces the discriminant to vanish, so the quadratic has a double root
$t_\star$ with $\norm{x - t_\star y}_2 = 0$, i.e. $x = t_\star y$. Conversely if $x = cy$ both sides
equal $|c|\norm{y}_2^2$.
</Proof>

<Note title="Why this proof is the right one">
The argument is "complete the square and read off the discriminant", and it generalises verbatim to
any inner-product space, including the reproducing-kernel Hilbert spaces of the
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/kernel-methods-and-rkhs">kernel methods</TopicRef> page. It uses
nothing about $\R^d$ beyond bilinearity and positivity.
</Note>

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is what lets us define the **angle** $\theta$ between non-zero vectors
through $\cos\theta = \ip{x}{y}/(\norm{x}_2\norm{y}_2) \in [-1,1]$. Two vectors are **orthogonal**
when $\ip{x}{y}=0$. A set $\{u_1,\dots,u_k\}$ is **orthonormal** when $\ip{u_i}{u_j} = \delta_{ij}$.

### Orthogonal projection

<Theorem id="projection-theorem" title="Orthogonal projection onto a subspace">
Let $V \subseteq \R^d$ be a subspace with orthonormal basis $u_1,\dots,u_k$, and let
$P = \sum_{i=1}^k u_i u_i^\top$. Then for every $x \in \R^d$:

1. $Px \in V$ and $x - Px \perp V$;
2. the decomposition $x = Px + (x - Px)$ into a $V$-component and a $V^\perp$-component is unique;
3. $Px = \argmin_{v \in V} \norm{x - v}_2$, and $\norm{x}_2^2 = \norm{Px}_2^2 + \norm{x-Px}_2^2$;
4. $P = P^\top = P^2$, and $\norm{P} = 1$ unless $V = \{0\}$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
(1) $Px = \sum_i \ip{u_i}{x} u_i$ is a combination of the $u_i$, hence in $V$. For each $j$,
$\ip{u_j}{x - Px} = \ip{u_j}{x} - \sum_i \ip{u_i}{x}\ip{u_j}{u_i} = \ip{u_j}{x} - \ip{u_j}{x} = 0$,
and the $u_j$ span $V$.

(2) If $x = v_1 + w_1 = v_2 + w_2$ with $v_i \in V$, $w_i \in V^\perp$, then
$v_1 - v_2 = w_2 - w_1 \in V \cap V^\perp = \{0\}$, because a vector orthogonal to itself has norm
zero.

(3) For any $v \in V$ write $x - v = (x - Px) + (Px - v)$. The first term is in $V^\perp$ and the
second in $V$, so they are orthogonal and
$$
\norm{x-v}_2^2 = \norm{x - Px}_2^2 + \norm{Px - v}_2^2 \ge \norm{x-Px}_2^2 ,
$$
with equality exactly when $v = Px$. Taking $v=0$ gives the Pythagorean identity.

(4) Symmetry and idempotence are immediate from orthonormality:
$P^2 = \sum_{i,j} u_i (u_i^\top u_j) u_j^\top = \sum_i u_i u_i^\top = P$. By (3),
$\norm{Px}_2 \le \norm{x}_2$ always, with equality for $x \in V$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Least squares *is* orthogonal projection. The normal equations of
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/linear-and-ridge-regression">linear regression</TopicRef> say exactly
that the residual is orthogonal to the column space of the design matrix; the geometry of this
theorem is the whole derivation.
</KeyIdea>

## The spectral theorem

Write $\mathbb{S}^d$ for the set of real symmetric $d \times d$ matrices.

<Theorem id="spectral-theorem" title="Spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices">
Let $A \in \mathbb{S}^d$. Then there exist real numbers
$\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d$ and an orthonormal basis $u_1,\dots,u_d$ of
$\R^d$ with $Au_i = \lambda_i u_i$. Equivalently $A = U\Lambda U^\top$ with $U$ orthogonal and
$\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d)$, equivalently
$A = \sum_{i=1}^d \lambda_i u_i u_i^\top$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Induct on $d$. For $d=1$ there is nothing to prove.

The function $x \mapsto x^\top A x$ is continuous on the unit sphere $S^{d-1}$, which is compact, so
it attains a maximum at some $u_1$ with $\norm{u_1}_2 = 1$; set $\lambda_1 = u_1^\top A u_1$.

We claim $Au_1 = \lambda_1 u_1$. Let $v \perp u_1$ with $\norm{v}_2 = 1$ and consider
$x(t) = (u_1 + tv)/\norm{u_1+tv}_2$, a curve on the sphere with $x(0)=u_1$. Since
$\norm{u_1+tv}_2^2 = 1+t^2$,
$$
g(t) := x(t)^\top A x(t) = \frac{\lambda_1 + 2t\,v^\top A u_1 + t^2 v^\top A v}{1+t^2},
$$
using symmetry to combine the two cross terms. As $t=0$ is a maximum of $g$ we need $g'(0)=0$, and
differentiating gives $g'(0) = 2\, v^\top A u_1$. So $Au_1$ is orthogonal to every $v \perp u_1$,
which forces $Au_1 \in \operatorname{span}(u_1)$, and pairing with $u_1$ identifies the scalar as
$\lambda_1$.

Now let $W = u_1^\perp$, a subspace of dimension $d-1$. If $w \in W$ then
$\ip{u_1}{Aw} = \ip{Au_1}{w} = \lambda_1\ip{u_1}{w} = 0$, so $A$ maps $W$ into $W$. The restriction
$A|_W$ is symmetric as a bilinear form on $W$, so by induction $W$ has an orthonormal eigenbasis
$u_2,\dots,u_d$ with real eigenvalues. Together with $u_1$ these form an orthonormal eigenbasis of
$\R^d$; sorting the eigenvalues gives the stated ordering.

Reality of the eigenvalues is part of the construction: each $\lambda_i$ arises as a value of the
real quadratic form $x^\top A x$.
</Proof>

<Proposition id="rayleigh" title="Rayleigh quotient characterisation of eigenvalues">
For $A \in \mathbb{S}^d$ and $x \ne 0$ define the Rayleigh quotient
$R_A(x) = \dfrac{x^\top A x}{x^\top x}$. Then
$$
\lambda_{\max}(A) = \max_{x \ne 0} R_A(x), \qquad \lambda_{\min}(A) = \min_{x \ne 0} R_A(x),
$$
and more generally $\lambda_k(A) = \max_{\dim V = k}\ \min_{0 \ne x \in V} R_A(x)$ (the Courant–Fischer
min–max characterisation).
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Expand $x = \sum_i c_i u_i$ in the eigenbasis. Then $x^\top A x = \sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2$ and
$x^\top x = \sum_i c_i^2$, so $R_A(x)$ is a weighted average of the eigenvalues with weights
$c_i^2 / \sum_j c_j^2$. A weighted average lies between the smallest and largest value averaged, and
attains each at the corresponding $c$ concentrated on one coordinate. The min–max statement follows
by the same expansion together with a dimension count: any $k$-dimensional $V$ meets
$\operatorname{span}(u_k,\dots,u_d)$ non-trivially, which bounds the inner minimum above by
$\lambda_k$, and $V = \operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_k)$ achieves it.
</Proof>

<Note title="Where this gets used">
The Rayleigh quotient is the bridge between "eigenvalue" and "optimisation problem", and it is the
reason spectral quantities show up in convergence rates at all. It is also the tool that turns the
$\eps$-net arguments of <TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/nets-covering-and-packing">nets and
covering numbers</TopicRef> into bounds on $\norm{A}$.
</Note>

## Positive semidefinite matrices and quadratic forms

<Definition id="psd-def" title="Positive semidefinite and positive definite">
$A \in \mathbb{S}^d$ is **positive semidefinite** (PSD), written $A \succeq 0$, if
$x^\top A x \ge 0$ for all $x$, and **positive definite** (PD), written $A \succ 0$, if
$x^\top A x > 0$ for all $x \ne 0$. We write $A \succeq B$ for $A - B \succeq 0$; this is the
**Loewner order**, a partial order — not every pair of symmetric matrices is comparable.
</Definition>

<Proposition id="psd-characterisation" title="Characterisations of positive semidefiniteness">
For $A \in \mathbb{S}^d$ the following are equivalent:

1. $x^\top A x \ge 0$ for all $x \in \R^d$;
2. every eigenvalue of $A$ is non-negative;
3. $A = B^\top B$ for some matrix $B$;
4. $A = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i v_i v_i^\top$ for some $r \le d$, $\sigma_i > 0$ and orthonormal $v_i$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
(1) $\Leftrightarrow$ (2): by the Rayleigh quotient, $\min_{x\ne0} R_A(x) = \lambda_{\min}(A)$, and
$x^\top A x \ge 0$ for all $x$ is precisely $\lambda_{\min}(A) \ge 0$.

(2) $\Rightarrow$ (4): the spectral theorem gives $A = \sum_i \lambda_i u_i u_i^\top$; drop the zero
eigenvalues.

(4) $\Rightarrow$ (3): take $B = \sum_i \sqrt{\sigma_i}\, v_i e_i^\top$, so that
$B^\top B = \sum_i \sigma_i v_i v_i^\top$.

(3) $\Rightarrow$ (1): $x^\top B^\top B x = \norm{Bx}_2^2 \ge 0$.
</Proof>

Statement (3) is why PSD matrices are unavoidable in learning theory: every **Gram matrix**
$K_{ij} = \ip{\phi(x_i)}{\phi(x_j)}$ is of the form $B^\top B$ and therefore PSD, and conversely every
PSD matrix is a Gram matrix of *some* feature map. That equivalence is Mercer's condition in
miniature.

<Definition id="matrix-sqrt" title="Square root and Cholesky factor">
For $A \succeq 0$ with spectral decomposition $U\Lambda U^\top$, the matrix
$A^{1/2} := U \Lambda^{1/2} U^\top$ is the unique PSD matrix with $(A^{1/2})^2 = A$. If $A \succ 0$
it also has a unique factorisation $A = LL^\top$ with $L$ lower triangular and positive diagonal
(the Cholesky factor), computable in $\tfrac13 d^3$ flops — which is how every "solve
$(X^\top X + \lambda I)\beta = X^\top y$" in this laboratory is actually executed.
</Definition>

## The singular value decomposition

<Theorem id="svd" title="Singular value decomposition">
Every $A \in \R^{n \times p}$ can be written
$$
A = U \Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top ,
$$
where $r = \rank A$, $U \in \R^{n\times n}$ and $V \in \R^{p \times p}$ are orthogonal, and
$\Sigma \in \R^{n \times p}$ is zero off the diagonal with
$\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_{\min(n,p)} \ge 0$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Apply the spectral theorem to $A^\top A \in \mathbb{S}^p$, which is PSD because
$x^\top A^\top A x = \norm{Ax}_2^2 \ge 0$. Write $A^\top A = V \Lambda V^\top$ with
$\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_p \ge 0$, and set $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$. Let $r$ be the number
of strictly positive $\sigma_i$.

For $i \le r$ define $u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i$. These are orthonormal:
$$
\ip{u_i}{u_j} = \frac{v_i^\top A^\top A v_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}
= \frac{\lambda_j\, \ip{v_i}{v_j}}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} = \delta_{ij}.
$$
Extend $u_1,\dots,u_r$ to an orthonormal basis of $\R^n$. For $i > r$ we have
$\norm{Av_i}_2^2 = \lambda_i = 0$, so $Av_i = 0$. Hence $Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$ for all $i$, which is
exactly $AV = U\Sigma$, and $V$ orthogonal gives $A = U\Sigma V^\top$. Finally
$\rank A = \rank \Sigma = r$.
</Proof>

<Note title="Reading the SVD">
$A$ acts by rotating with $V^\top$, scaling the $i$-th coordinate by $\sigma_i$, and rotating with
$U$. The right singular vectors $v_i$ diagonalise $A^\top A$; the left singular vectors $u_i$
diagonalise $AA^\top$; the two matrices share the non-zero eigenvalues $\sigma_i^2$. This is why the
spectral experiments on this site can compute singular values by forming whichever of the two
products is smaller.
</Note>

### Matrix norms

<Definition id="matrix-norms" title="Operator, Frobenius and nuclear norms">
$$
\norm{A} = \norm{A}_{2\to2} = \max_{\norm{x}_2 = 1}\norm{Ax}_2 = \sigma_1, \qquad
\norm{A}_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} A_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\sum_i \sigma_i^2}, \qquad
\norm{A}_* = \sum_i \sigma_i .
$$
The first is the **spectral** (operator) norm, the second the **Frobenius** norm, the third the
**nuclear** norm. All three are functions of the singular values alone, hence invariant under
$A \mapsto QAR$ for orthogonal $Q,R$.
</Definition>

The inequalities
$$
\norm{A} \le \norm{A}_F \le \sqrt{r}\,\norm{A}, \qquad \norm{A}_F \le \norm{A}_* \le \sqrt{r}\,\norm{A}_F
$$
follow at once from $\sigma_1^2 \le \sum_i \sigma_i^2 \le r\sigma_1^2$ and Cauchy–Schwarz on the vector
of singular values. They matter because the *statistically* natural norm is often $\norm{\cdot}_F$
while the *analytically* tractable one is $\norm{\cdot}$, and the conversion costs a $\sqrt{r}$ —
a factor that is harmless for low-rank objects and fatal for full-rank ones in high dimension.

<Theorem id="eckart-young" title="Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem">
Let $A_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$ be the rank-$k$ truncation of the SVD. Then for every
matrix $B$ of rank at most $k$,
$$
\norm{A - A_k} = \sigma_{k+1} \le \norm{A-B}, \qquad
\norm{A-A_k}_F = \Big(\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2\Big)^{1/2} \le \norm{A-B}_F .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof sketch>
The value at $A_k$ is a computation from the SVD. For optimality in the spectral norm: if
$\rank B \le k$ then $\ker B$ has dimension at least $p-k$, so it meets
$\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{k+1})$ in some unit vector $x$, and then
$\norm{A-B} \ge \norm{(A-B)x}_2 = \norm{Ax}_2 \ge \sigma_{k+1}$ since $x$ lies in the span of the top
$k+1$ right singular vectors. The Frobenius statement follows from Weyl's inequalities for singular
values, $\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y) \le \sigma_i(X) + \sigma_j(Y)$, applied to $A = (A-B) + B$; the details
of that step are the part omitted here.
</Proof>

This theorem is the reason principal component analysis is what it is: PCA is the Frobenius-optimal
low-rank approximation of the centred data matrix, and nothing more.

## Matrix calculus in one line each

The derivative conventions used throughout the optimisation pages are collected on the
<TopicRef id="foundations/matrix-calculus">matrix calculus</TopicRef> page. The three identities used
most often are
$$
\nabla_x (a^\top x) = a, \qquad
\nabla_x (x^\top A x) = (A + A^\top)x, \qquad
\nabla_x \tfrac12\norm{Ax-b}_2^2 = A^\top(Ax-b).
$$

<Warning title="Common mistake">
$\nabla_x (x^\top A x) = 2Ax$ **only** when $A$ is symmetric. For general $A$ the answer is
$(A+A^\top)x$. Most of the time in this laboratory $A$ is a Hessian or a covariance and therefore
symmetric, which makes the shortcut safe — but not always, and the asymmetric case is exactly where
sign errors in backpropagation derivations come from.
</Warning>
