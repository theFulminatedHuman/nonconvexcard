The algorithm is
$$
x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\,\nabla f(x_t),
$$
and the question is how fast $f(x_t) - f^\star$ goes to zero. The answer depends entirely on which
assumptions hold, and the four theorems below give four qualitatively different answers. The table is
the summary.

| Assumptions | Quantity bounded | Rate | Steps for $\eps$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| convex, $L$-smooth | $f(x_t)-f^\star$ | $\dfrac{L\norm{x_0-x^\star}^2}{2t}$ | $O(1/\eps)$ |
| $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth | $f(x_t)-f^\star$ | $\left(1-\tfrac1\kappa\right)^{t}\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big)$ | $O(\kappa\log(1/\eps))$ |
| convex, $G$-Lipschitz (non-smooth) | $f(\bar x_t)-f^\star$ | $\dfrac{G\norm{x_0-x^\star}}{\sqrt t}$ | $O(1/\eps^2)$ |
| $L$-smooth only | $\min_{s\le t}\norm{\nabla f(x_s)}$ | $\sqrt{\dfrac{2L(f(x_0)-f^\star)}{t}}$ | $O(1/\eps^2)$ |

## The descent lemma applied

<Lemma id="gd-descent" title="One step decreases the objective">
Let $f$ be $L$-smooth and let $x^+ = x - \eta\nabla f(x)$ with $0 < \eta \le 1/L$. Then
$$
f(x^+) \le f(x) - \frac{\eta}{2}\norm{\nabla f(x)}_2^2 .
$$
</Lemma>

<Proof>
By the <TopicRef id="foundations/matrix-calculus">descent lemma</TopicRef> with $y = x^+$ and
$y - x = -\eta\nabla f(x)$,
$$
f(x^+) \le f(x) - \eta\norm{\nabla f(x)}_2^2 + \frac{L\eta^2}{2}\norm{\nabla f(x)}_2^2
= f(x) - \eta\left(1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}\right)\norm{\nabla f(x)}_2^2 .
$$
For $\eta\le1/L$ we have $L\eta/2 \le 1/2$, so the bracket is at least $1/2$.
</Proof>

<Assumptions>
$L$-smoothness only. No convexity is used, which is why this lemma is the starting point of the
non-convex analysis as well. The step-size restriction $\eta \le 1/L$ is essential and is not a
technical convenience — see the divergence proposition at the end of the page.
</Assumptions>

## Convex and smooth: the $O(1/t)$ rate

<Theorem id="gd-convex" title="Gradient descent on convex smooth functions">
Let $f$ be convex, $L$-smooth, with a minimiser $x^\star$. Run gradient descent with $\eta = 1/L$.
Then for all $t\ge1$,
$$
f(x_t) - f^\star \le \frac{L\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2}{2t}.
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
**Step 1: the distance to the optimum is non-increasing, quantitatively.** Expand
$$
\norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2 = \norm{x_t - x^\star}_2^2 - 2\eta\ip{\nabla f(x_t)}{x_t-x^\star} + \eta^2\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 .
$$
By convexity (the tangent-plane inequality at $x_t$ evaluated at $x^\star$),
$$
\ip{\nabla f(x_t)}{x_t - x^\star} \ge f(x_t) - f^\star .
$$
By the descent lemma above, $\tfrac\eta2\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 \le f(x_t) - f(x_{t+1})$, so
$\eta^2\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 \le 2\eta\big(f(x_t)-f(x_{t+1})\big)$. Substituting both,
$$
\norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2 \le \norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 - 2\eta\big(f(x_t)-f^\star\big) + 2\eta\big(f(x_t)-f(x_{t+1})\big),
$$
that is,
$$
2\eta\big(f(x_{t+1}) - f^\star\big) \le \norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 - \norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2 .
$$

**Step 2: telescope.** Summing for $s = 0,\dots,t-1$, the right side telescopes:
$$
2\eta\sum_{s=1}^{t}\big(f(x_s)-f^\star\big) \le \norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2 - \norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 \le \norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2 .
$$

**Step 3: use monotonicity.** The descent lemma makes $f(x_s)$ non-increasing, so
$f(x_t)-f^\star \le \frac1t\sum_{s=1}^t(f(x_s)-f^\star)$. Combining with Step 2 and $\eta = 1/L$,
$$
f(x_t) - f^\star \le \frac{\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2}{2\eta t} = \frac{L\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2}{2t}.
$$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Step 1 is the move to internalise: the squared distance to the optimum acts as a **potential
function** (a Lyapunov function), and each step decreases it by an amount proportional to the
suboptimality. Telescoping then converts a per-step decrease into a rate. Every convergence proof in
this section — including the momentum and stochastic ones — is an instance of the same template with
a cleverer potential.
</KeyIdea>

<NotSaid>
The $O(1/t)$ rate is sublinear: reaching $\eps$ takes $\Theta(1/\eps)$ steps, so three more digits of
accuracy costs a thousand times more work. There is no contradiction with the linear rate below; the
difference is entirely the presence of strong convexity. Also note that the bound depends on
$\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2$, so it is not scale-free, and it says nothing about $\norm{x_t-x^\star}$ — a
convex smooth function can have a flat valley of near-minimisers far apart from each other.
</NotSaid>

## Strongly convex and smooth: the linear rate

<Theorem id="gd-strongly-convex" title="Gradient descent on smooth strongly convex functions">
Let $f$ be $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth, $\kappa = L/\mu$, and run gradient descent with
$\eta = 1/L$. Then
$$
f(x_t) - f^\star \le \left(1-\frac1\kappa\right)^t\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big),
$$
and consequently, by quadratic growth,
$\norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 \le \frac2\mu\left(1-\frac1\kappa\right)^t\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big)$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Combine the descent lemma with the
<TopicRef id="optimization/convex-sets-and-functions">PL inequality</TopicRef>. Let
$\Delta_t = f(x_t) - f^\star$. The descent lemma with $\eta = 1/L$ gives
$$
\Delta_{t+1} \le \Delta_t - \frac{1}{2L}\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 ,
$$
and PL gives $\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 \ge 2\mu\Delta_t$. Therefore
$$
\Delta_{t+1} \le \Delta_t - \frac{2\mu\Delta_t}{2L} = \left(1-\frac\mu L\right)\Delta_t
= \left(1-\frac1\kappa\right)\Delta_t .
$$
Iterating gives the claim. The distance bound follows from quadratic growth,
$\tfrac\mu2\norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 \le \Delta_t$.
</Proof>

<Note title="Reading the rate">
$(1-1/\kappa)^t \le e^{-t/\kappa}$, so $O(\kappa\log(1/\eps))$ steps suffice. The dependence on
accuracy is logarithmic — excellent — and the dependence on conditioning is linear — the problem. For
$\kappa = 10^4$, which is unremarkable for a real quadratic, that is $10^4$ iterations per digit.
<TopicRef id="advanced-optimization/acceleration">Acceleration</TopicRef> improves the $\kappa$ to
$\sqrt\kappa$, and the lower bound below says that is the best possible for a first-order method.
</Note>

## Non-convex and smooth: stationarity only

<Theorem id="gd-nonconvex" title="Gradient descent on smooth non-convex functions">
Let $f$ be $L$-smooth and bounded below by $f^\star$, and run gradient descent with $\eta = 1/L$.
Then
$$
\min_{0\le s < t}\norm{\nabla f(x_s)}_2 \le \sqrt{\frac{2L\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big)}{t}} .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Sum the descent lemma over $s = 0,\dots,t-1$ with $\eta = 1/L$:
$$
\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\norm{\nabla f(x_s)}_2^2 \le \sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\big(f(x_s)-f(x_{s+1})\big)
= f(x_0)-f(x_t) \le f(x_0)-f^\star .
$$
The minimum of the summands is at most their average, so
$$
\min_{s<t}\norm{\nabla f(x_s)}_2^2 \le \frac1t\sum_{s<t}\norm{\nabla f(x_s)}_2^2
\le \frac{2L\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big)}{t} .
$$
Taking square roots completes the proof.
</Proof>

<NotSaid>
Read the conclusion very carefully. It says: **some** iterate among the first $t$ has small gradient.
It does **not** say

- that the last iterate has small gradient (though the monotonicity of $f$ makes it plausible),
- that any iterate is near a local minimum — a saddle point also has zero gradient,
- that $f(x_t)$ is close to $\inf f$ — the iterates may converge to a strictly suboptimal stationary point,
- that anything converges at all, in the sense of the iterates having a limit.

This is the strongest general statement available for smooth non-convex optimisation with a
first-order method, and the gap between it and what practitioners want is the subject of the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">deep learning theory</TopicRef> page. A concrete
example of the gap: $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on $\R$ has a local minimum at $x=1$ and a local maximum at
$x=-1$; started at $x_0 = -1$, gradient descent stays there forever, satisfying the theorem exactly
and finding nothing.
</NotSaid>

## Non-smooth convex: the subgradient method

<Definition id="subgradient" title="Subgradient">
$g$ is a **subgradient** of a convex $f$ at $x$ if $f(y)\ge f(x)+\ip{g}{y-x}$ for all $y$. The set of
subgradients is the **subdifferential** $\partial f(x)$, which is non-empty for convex $f$ on the
interior of its domain and equals $\{\nabla f(x)\}$ where $f$ is differentiable.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="subgradient-method" title="Subgradient method">
Let $f$ be convex and $G$-Lipschitz (equivalently, $\norm{g}_2\le G$ for all $g\in\partial f(x)$ and
all $x$), with minimiser $x^\star$ and $\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2\le R$. Run
$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta g_t$ with $g_t\in\partial f(x_t)$ and fixed $\eta = \frac{R}{G\sqrt T}$ for a
horizon $T$. Then the average iterate $\bar x_T = \frac1T\sum_{t<T}x_t$ satisfies
$$
f(\bar x_T) - f^\star \le \frac{GR}{\sqrt T}.
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Expand as before, now with a subgradient in place of the gradient:
$$
\norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2 = \norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 - 2\eta\ip{g_t}{x_t-x^\star}+\eta^2\norm{g_t}_2^2 .
$$
The defining property of a subgradient gives $\ip{g_t}{x_t - x^\star}\ge f(x_t)-f^\star$, and
$\norm{g_t}_2\le G$, so
$$
2\eta\big(f(x_t)-f^\star\big) \le \norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 - \norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2 + \eta^2G^2 .
$$
Sum over $t = 0,\dots,T-1$ and telescope:
$$
2\eta\sum_{t<T}\big(f(x_t)-f^\star\big) \le R^2 + T\eta^2G^2 .
$$
By convexity $f(\bar x_T)\le\frac1T\sum_{t<T}f(x_t)$ (Jensen), so
$$
f(\bar x_T)-f^\star \le \frac{R^2}{2\eta T} + \frac{\eta G^2}{2} .
$$
This is minimised over $\eta$ at $\eta = R/(G\sqrt T)$, where its value is $GR/\sqrt T$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Common mistake">
The subgradient method has **no descent lemma**: $f(x_{t+1})$ can exceed $f(x_t)$, so the last iterate
carries no guarantee and the averaging is not cosmetic. Also note the step size depends on the horizon
$T$: this is a *fixed-horizon* algorithm, and a diminishing schedule $\eta_t \propto 1/\sqrt t$ is
needed for an anytime guarantee. Both features reappear in
<TopicRef id="stochastic-optimization/sgd-convergence">SGD</TopicRef>, for exactly the same reason —
in both cases the search direction is not a descent direction.
</Warning>

## Step size and lower bounds

<Proposition id="gd-step-threshold" title="Divergence above the threshold">
Let $f(x) = \tfrac{L}{2}x^2$ on $\R$, so $f$ is $L$-smooth and $L$-strongly convex. Gradient descent
with step $\eta$ satisfies $x_{t} = (1-\eta L)^tx_0$, hence converges if and only if
$0<\eta<2/L$, is stationary if $\eta = 2/L$, and diverges if $\eta>2/L$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta L x_t = (1-\eta L)x_t$, so $x_t = (1-\eta L)^t x_0$. This tends to $0$ iff
$|1-\eta L|<1$, i.e. $0<\eta<2/L$; equals $\pm x_0$ alternately when $\eta = 2/L$; and grows
geometrically when $\eta>2/L$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The threshold $2/L$ is exact, not an artefact. It is also the reason "learning rate too high" produces
*divergence* rather than slow progress: past the threshold the dynamics are unstable, and the failure
is qualitative. In deep learning the empirical counterpart is the "edge of stability" phenomenon,
where training settles at a sharpness $L \approx 2/\eta$ — the largest value compatible with
stability. That is an empirical observation, discussed as such on the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">deep learning theory</TopicRef> page.
</KeyIdea>

<Theorem id="gd-lower-bound" title="Lower bound for first-order methods">
Consider any method that, at step $t$, outputs a point in the affine span of
$x_0,\nabla f(x_0),\dots,\nabla f(x_{t-1})$.

- For convex $L$-smooth $f$ in dimension $d > t$, there is an instance with $f(x_t)-f^\star \gtrsim \dfrac{L\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2}{(t+1)^2}$.
- For $\mu$-strongly convex $L$-smooth $f$, there is an instance with $f(x_t)-f^\star \gtrsim \left(\dfrac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}\right)^{2t}\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big)$.
</Theorem>

Quoted from Nesterov's lectures; the instances are quadratics built from a tridiagonal matrix that
"reveals" only one new coordinate per gradient query. The consequences are important:

- Plain gradient descent's $O(1/t)$ is **not optimal**; $O(1/t^2)$ is achievable and is attained by <TopicRef id="advanced-optimization/acceleration">Nesterov acceleration</TopicRef>.
- The $\sqrt\kappa$ in the strongly convex lower bound is also achieved by acceleration, so the gap between $\kappa$ and $\sqrt\kappa$ closes exactly.
- No first-order method can do better. Beating these rates requires a stronger oracle — second-order information, as in <TopicRef id="advanced-optimization/second-order-methods">Newton's method</TopicRef>, or structure beyond smoothness and convexity.
