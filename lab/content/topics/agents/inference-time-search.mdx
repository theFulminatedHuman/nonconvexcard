<Intuition>
If one attempt succeeds with probability $p$, then $n$ independent attempts contain a success with
probability $1 - (1-p)^n$. That is exponential improvement for linear cost — the most favourable
trade in the whole subject, and the reason "just sample more and pick the best" works.

The catch is in "pick the best". You need something that can tell which attempt succeeded. How good
that thing is determines whether the exponential is real or an illusion.
</Intuition>

## With a perfect verifier

<Claim kind="proposition" id="bon-perfect-verifier" title="Best-of-n with a perfect verifier">
Let each of $n$ independent samples be correct with probability $p$, and suppose a verifier
identifies a correct sample whenever one is present. Then
$$
\P(\text{success}) = 1 - (1-p)^n,
$$
and the number of samples needed to reach target $T$ is
$$
n \ \ge\ \frac{\log(1-T)}{\log(1-p)} .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Failure requires all $n$ samples to be incorrect, which by independence has probability $(1-p)^n$;
the success probability is its complement. For the second claim,
$1-(1-p)^n \ge T \iff (1-p)^n \le 1-T$, and taking logarithms — noting $\log(1-p) &lt; 0$, which
reverses the inequality — gives $n \ge \log(1-T)/\log(1-p)$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Details summary="Reading the exponential honestly">
At $p = 0.3$: $n=1$ gives 0.30, $n=5$ gives 0.83, $n=10$ gives 0.97, $n=20$ gives 0.999.

But invert it. To go from 0.97 to 0.999 took an extra 10 samples; the next factor of ten in failure
probability costs another 20. Failure decays like $e^{-n\log\frac{1}{1-p}}$, so **success improves
logarithmically in compute**: each fixed increment of quality costs a constant *multiple* of the
samples. That is the same shape as scaling laws for training, and it is why test-time search is
powerful in the middle of the curve and expensive at the end of it.
</Details>

## Estimating pass@k honestly

Evaluating $\mathrm{pass}@k$ by drawing exactly $k$ samples is high-variance. The standard estimator
draws $n \ge k$ and computes a combinatorial correction.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="passk-estimator" title="The unbiased pass@k estimator">
Draw $n$ i.i.d. samples of which $c$ are correct. Then
$$
\widehat{\mathrm{pass}@k} = 1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}
$$
is an unbiased estimator of the probability that a uniformly random subset of $k$ of those $n$
samples contains at least one correct sample.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Condition on $c$. Choosing $k$ of the $n$ samples uniformly without replacement, the subset contains
no correct sample exactly when all $k$ come from the $n-c$ incorrect ones. The number of such subsets
is $\binom{n-c}{k}$ out of $\binom{n}{k}$ total, so
$$
\P(\text{no correct sample in the subset} \mid c) = \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}},
$$
with the convention $\binom{m}{k} = 0$ for $m &lt; k$. The estimator is one minus this quantity, which
is therefore exactly the conditional probability of interest — unbiased by construction, being the
quantity itself rather than an approximation to it. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Note>
Computing this directly overflows for realistic $n$. The numerically sound form uses the telescoping
product
$$
\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}} = \prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{n-c-i}{n-i},
$$
evaluated term by term, which is also immediate from writing out the factorials and cancelling.
</Note>

## What best-of-n costs in distribution

Selecting the best of $n$ samples changes the distribution you are sampling from. That shift has an
exact closed form, and it is the quantity that governs reward hacking.

<Claim kind="theorem" id="bon-kl" title="The KL divergence of best-of-n">
Let $\pi$ be a base policy and $r$ a reward whose distribution under $\pi$ is continuous (so ties have
probability zero). Let $\pi_n$ be the distribution of the highest-reward sample among $n$ i.i.d.
draws from $\pi$. Then
$$
\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_n \,\|\, \pi\big) \;=\; \log n - \frac{n-1}{n}.
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Let $F$ be the CDF of $r(X)$ for $X\sim\pi$, continuous by assumption. The best-of-$n$ sample is the
one attaining the maximum reward, so its density with respect to $\pi$ is
$$
\frac{d\pi_n}{d\pi}(x) = n\,F\big(r(x)\big)^{\,n-1},
$$
because $x$ is selected when it is drawn (density $\pi$), one of $n$ positions ($n$ ways), and the
other $n-1$ draws all have smaller reward (probability $F(r(x))^{n-1}$ each, independently).

Therefore
$$
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_n\|\pi) = \E_{X\sim\pi_n}\left[\log \frac{d\pi_n}{d\pi}(X)\right]
= \log n + (n-1)\,\E_{X\sim\pi_n}\big[\log F(r(X))\big].
$$

Set $U = F(r(X))$. Under $\pi$, $U$ is uniform on $[0,1]$ by the probability integral transform
(valid since $F$ is continuous). Under $\pi_n$, $U$ is the maximum of $n$ i.i.d. uniforms, whose CDF
is $u^n$ and density $nu^{n-1}$ on $[0,1]$. Hence
$$
\E_{\pi_n}[\log U] = \int_0^1 (\log u)\, n u^{n-1}\,du .
$$
Integrate by parts with $dv = nu^{n-1}du$, $v = u^n$:
$$
\int_0^1 (\log u)\,nu^{n-1}du = \Big[u^n\log u\Big]_0^1 - \int_0^1 u^n\cdot\frac{1}{u}\,du
= 0 - \int_0^1 u^{n-1}du = -\frac1n,
$$
the boundary term vanishing at both ends. Substituting,
$$
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_n\|\pi) = \log n + (n-1)\left(-\frac1n\right) = \log n - \frac{n-1}{n}. \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The divergence grows like $\log n$ — slowly, but without bound, and it depends on **nothing about the
reward except that it is continuous**. So best-of-$n$ gives a principled exchange rate between
compute and distribution shift, and lets you compare test-time selection against a KL-regularised
fine-tune on the same axis: $n = 8$ costs about $1.20$ nats, $n = 64$ about $3.17$.

This matters because distribution shift away from the base policy is exactly the regime where a
learned reward model stops being trustworthy — it was fit on samples from the base policy, and
best-of-$n$ is deliberately sampling where those were rare.
</KeyIdea>

## Imperfect verifiers

<Claim kind="proposition" id="imperfect-verifier" title="An imperfect verifier caps the gain">
Suppose each sample is correct with probability $p$, the verifier accepts a correct sample with
probability $1$, and accepts an incorrect sample with probability $\varphi &gt; 0$. If the system
returns a uniformly chosen accepted sample, then as $n\to\infty$ the probability that the returned
answer is correct tends to
$$
\frac{p}{p + (1-p)\varphi} \;&lt;\; 1 .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
By the law of large numbers the accepted pool contains approximately $np$ correct samples and
$n(1-p)\varphi$ incorrect ones. Choosing uniformly from the pool, the probability of a correct answer
tends to the ratio of correct to total accepted, which is the stated expression. It is strictly below
$1$ because $(1-p)\varphi &gt; 0$ whenever $p &lt; 1$ and $\varphi &gt; 0$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Warning>
Sampling more does not overcome a verifier's false positives — it *recruits* them. With $p = 0.3$ and
$\varphi = 0.05$ the ceiling is $0.3/(0.3 + 0.035) = 0.896$, and no amount of extra compute passes it.
Worse, when the selector is a learned reward model rather than a checker, large $n$ actively searches
for its errors: the best-of-$n$ distribution concentrates precisely on the inputs the reward model
scores highest, which include the ones it scores wrongly. This is reward hacking stated as a sampling
phenomenon, and it is why measured best-of-$n$ curves against learned reward models typically rise,
peak, and then *decline* with $n$ — while the same curves against an executable checker keep rising.
</Warning>

<NotSaid>
Independence across samples is assumed throughout. Samples from one model at one temperature are not
independent in the relevant sense — they share the model's systematic errors — so $(1-p)^n$
overstates the benefit exactly as it did for majority voting. And nothing here addresses *which*
search to run: best-of-$n$ over complete solutions, beam search over steps, or tree search with
backtracking all spend the same compute differently, and the comparison between them is empirical.
</NotSaid>
