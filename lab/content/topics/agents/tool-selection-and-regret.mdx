<Intuition>
An agent with several tools — a search index, a calculator, a code interpreter, a database — must
pick one at each step without knowing in advance which works best for the query at hand. Picking the
one that has worked best so far exploits what you know and risks never discovering a better option.
Picking at random explores but wastes calls. The bandit literature settles this trade with a precise
answer: be optimistic in proportion to your uncertainty, and your total loss grows only
logarithmically in the number of decisions.
</Intuition>

## Setup

<Claim kind="definition" id="regret-def" title="Cumulative regret">
There are $K$ arms (tools). Arm $i$ yields i.i.d. rewards in $[0,1]$ with unknown mean $\mu_i$. Let
$\mu^\star = \max_i \mu_i$ and $\Delta_i = \mu^\star - \mu_i$ be arm $i$'s **gap**. An algorithm
choosing arm $I_t$ at round $t$ has cumulative regret
$$
R_T = T\mu^\star - \E\left[\sum_{t=1}^T \mu_{I_t}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^K \Delta_i\,\E\big[n_i(T)\big],
$$
where $n_i(T)$ counts pulls of arm $i$ in $T$ rounds.
</Claim>

The second equality is just bookkeeping: each pull of arm $i$ costs exactly $\Delta_i$ against the
benchmark, so total regret is the gap-weighted pull count. **Bounding regret therefore means bounding
how often a suboptimal arm is pulled.**

<Algorithm title="UCB1">
1. Pull each arm once.
2. For $t &gt; K$, pull the arm maximising
   $$
   \mathrm{UCB}_i(t) = \hat\mu_i(t) + \sqrt{\frac{2\log t}{n_i(t)}},
   $$
   where $\hat\mu_i(t)$ is the empirical mean of arm $i$'s rewards so far.
3. Update $\hat\mu$ and $n$ and repeat.
</Algorithm>

The second term is a confidence radius: wide for rarely-pulled arms, shrinking like $1/\sqrt{n_i}$.
Acting on the *upper* end of the interval is "optimism in the face of uncertainty" — an arm is played
either because it looks good or because you do not yet know that it is not.

## The pull bound

<Claim kind="theorem" id="ucb-pulls" title="UCB1 pulls a suboptimal arm O(log T / Δ²) times">
For rewards in $[0,1]$ and any arm $i$ with $\Delta_i &gt; 0$, UCB1 satisfies
$$
\E\big[n_i(T)\big] \ \le\ \frac{8\log T}{\Delta_i^2} \;+\; 1 + \frac{\pi^2}{3}.
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
Rewards i.i.d. per arm, bounded in $[0,1]$, independent across arms and rounds, and **stationary**.
The last is the assumption most often violated in agent settings and is discussed at the end.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Let $\ell = \lceil 8\log T/\Delta_i^2\rceil$. We show that once arm $i$ has been pulled $\ell$ times,
further pulls are rare.

Suppose UCB1 pulls arm $i$ at round $t$ while $n_i(t) \ge \ell$. Then
$\mathrm{UCB}_i(t) \ge \mathrm{UCB}_{i^\star}(t)$ for the optimal arm $i^\star$, which forces at least
one of the following three events:

**(a)** $\hat\mu_{i^\star}(t) + \sqrt{2\log t / n_{i^\star}(t)} \le \mu^\star$ — the optimal arm's
confidence interval fails to cover its mean;

**(b)** $\hat\mu_i(t) &gt; \mu_i + \sqrt{2\log t/n_i(t)}$ — arm $i$'s empirical mean is far above its
true mean;

**(c)** $\mu^\star &lt; \mu_i + 2\sqrt{2\log t/n_i(t)}$ — the confidence radius is still wide enough to
straddle the gap.

Indeed, if none of (a)–(c) holds then
$$
\mathrm{UCB}_{i^\star}(t) &gt; \mu^\star \ \ge\ \mu_i + 2\sqrt{\frac{2\log t}{n_i(t)}}
\ \ge\ \hat\mu_i(t) + \sqrt{\frac{2\log t}{n_i(t)}} = \mathrm{UCB}_i(t),
$$
contradicting the assumption that $i$ was selected.

**(c) is impossible once $n_i(t) \ge \ell$.** Rearranged, (c) says
$\Delta_i &lt; 2\sqrt{2\log t/n_i(t)}$, i.e. $n_i(t) &lt; 8\log t/\Delta_i^2 \le 8\log T/\Delta_i^2 \le \ell$.

**(a) and (b) are rare.** Each is a deviation of an empirical mean of $s$ bounded rewards from its
expectation by $\sqrt{2\log t/s}$. By Hoeffding's inequality, for a fixed count $s$,
$$
\P\left(\hat\mu_{i,s} &gt; \mu_i + \sqrt{\frac{2\log t}{s}}\right) \le \exp\left(-2s\cdot\frac{2\log t}{s}\right) = t^{-4},
$$
and likewise for (a). Union bounding over the at most $t$ possible values of each arm's pull count,
$$
\P\big((a)\big) + \P\big((b)\big) \ \le\ 2\,t\cdot t^{-4} = 2t^{-3}.
$$

Summing over rounds,
$$
\E\big[n_i(T)\big] \ \le\ \ell + \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} 2t^{-3} \ \le\ \frac{8\log T}{\Delta_i^2} + 1 + \frac{\pi^2}{3},
$$
using $\sum_{t\ge1} t^{-3} \le \sum_{t\ge1}t^{-2} = \pi^2/6$ and $\lceil x\rceil \le x+1$.
$\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Claim kind="corollary" id="ucb-regret" title="Logarithmic regret for UCB1">
$$
R_T \ \le\ \sum_{i:\Delta_i&gt;0}\left(\frac{8\log T}{\Delta_i} + \left(1+\frac{\pi^2}{3}\right)\Delta_i\right)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
R_T = O\big(\sqrt{KT\log T}\big).
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
The first bound is the theorem multiplied by $\Delta_i$ and summed, using
$R_T = \sum_i \Delta_i\E[n_i(T)]$.

For the gap-free bound, split at a threshold $\Delta &gt; 0$. Arms with $\Delta_i \le \Delta$
contribute at most $T\Delta$ in total, since every round costs at most $\Delta$. Arms with
$\Delta_i &gt; \Delta$ contribute at most $\sum_i 8\log T/\Delta_i \le 8K\log T/\Delta$ plus a constant.
Hence
$$
R_T \ \lesssim\ T\Delta + \frac{8K\log T}{\Delta},
$$
and optimising over $\Delta$ — the two terms balance at $\Delta = \sqrt{8K\log T/T}$ — gives
$R_T = O(\sqrt{KT\log T})$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Two readings of the same result:

- **Per-instance:** regret grows like $\log T$, not $T$. The *fraction* of rounds spent on the wrong
  tool tends to zero. Exploration is nearly free in the long run.
- **Small gaps are expensive.** The $1/\Delta_i^2$ in the pull bound says arms that are *nearly* as
  good as the best get pulled a lot — which is fine, because each such pull costs only $\Delta_i$.
  That is exactly why the regret bound carries $1/\Delta_i$ rather than $1/\Delta_i^2$.
</KeyIdea>

<Claim kind="theorem" id="bandit-lower-bound" title="No algorithm beats logarithmic regret">
For any algorithm whose regret is $o(T^a)$ for every $a &gt; 0$ on every instance, and for a broad class
of reward families, there are instances on which
$$
\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{R_T}{\log T} \ \ge\ \sum_{i:\Delta_i&gt;0}\frac{\Delta_i}{\mathrm{KL}(\nu_i\,\|\,\nu^\star)},
$$
where $\mathrm{KL}(\nu_i\|\nu^\star)$ is the divergence between arm $i$'s reward distribution and the
optimal arm's.
</Claim>

<Warning>
The proof is not given here; it is a change-of-measure argument showing that distinguishing two
nearby instances requires a number of samples inversely proportional to their KL divergence. What
matters for use: **UCB1's $\log T$ rate is optimal in order**, so no cleverer exploration rule
improves the shape of the bound — only its constants. Stated without that lower bound, "UCB achieves
logarithmic regret" would sound like an achievement that might be beaten; it cannot be.
</Warning>

<NotSaid>
The gap between this theory and an actual agent is wide, and each gap is a genuine modelling failure
rather than a technicality:

- **Tool quality is context-dependent.** The right tool depends on the query, which makes this a
  *contextual* bandit; the analysis above is for a single fixed distribution per arm. Contextual
  algorithms exist with their own guarantees and different assumptions.
- **Rewards are not stationary.** Tools change, indexes go stale, APIs degrade. UCB's confidence
  radius shrinks monotonically, so it becomes progressively *less* willing to re-explore an arm that
  has gone bad — the wrong behaviour under drift. Discounted or sliding-window variants exist.
- **Calls are not free or equal.** Arms have different latencies and costs; the reward-per-round
  model prices them identically.
- **Feedback is delayed and partial.** Whether a tool call helped is often only apparent at the end
  of a trajectory, if at all — the credit-assignment problem the bandit formulation assumes away.

Use the bandit framing to reason about exploration budgets and to recognise that a small persistent
exploration rate is cheap. Do not read the regret bound as a guarantee about a deployed agent.
</NotSaid>
