<Intuition>
An agent that calls a tool, reads the result, decides, and repeats is running a chain. Chains break at
their weakest link and, worse, they break *cumulatively*: each step is another chance to fail. The
arithmetic is unforgiving and it is the first thing to compute before designing anything else.
</Intuition>

## The compounding law

<Claim kind="proposition" id="trajectory-success" title="Trajectory success decays geometrically in the horizon">
Let an agent take $n$ steps, each succeeding independently with probability $p$, where the trajectory
succeeds only if every step does. Then
$$
\P(\text{trajectory succeeds}) = p^{\,n},
$$
and achieving a target success rate $T$ requires per-step reliability
$$
p \ \ge\ T^{1/n}.
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
Independence across steps, and a strict conjunction — no step is recoverable. Both are idealisations,
and the second is relaxed below. Independence is the more suspect: a mistaken early step often makes
later steps harder, which correlates failures *positively* and makes the true number worse than
$p^n$, not better.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
The success events are independent, so the probability of their intersection is the product,
$\prod_{i=1}^n p = p^n$. For the second claim, $p^n \ge T \iff n\log p \ge \log T \iff
p \ge T^{1/n}$, using that $\log$ is increasing and $p \in (0,1]$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Details summary="The table every agent designer should have seen">
Trajectory success $p^n$:

| $p$ \\ $n$ | 5 | 10 | 20 | 50 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.90 | 0.59 | 0.35 | 0.12 | 0.005 |
| 0.95 | 0.77 | 0.60 | 0.36 | 0.08 |
| 0.99 | 0.95 | 0.90 | 0.82 | 0.61 |
| 0.999 | 0.995 | 0.990 | 0.980 | 0.951 |

And the bar to hit 90% end-to-end, $p = 0.9^{1/n}$:

| $n$ | 5 | 10 | 20 | 50 | 100 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| required $p$ | 0.979 | 0.990 | 0.9947 | 0.9979 | 0.9989 |

**Reading these.** A 95%-reliable step — which sounds strong, and is roughly where careful
single-shot LLM behaviour sits on many tasks — gives a 36% success rate over 20 steps. To run a
50-step agent at 90% end to end you need 99.8% per step, which is two orders of magnitude fewer
errors than "very good".

The design consequence is that **shortening the horizon beats improving the step**, because $n$ sits
in the exponent. Halving $n$ squares the trajectory success; a few points of per-step accuracy moves
it linearly. Collapsing five tool calls into one composite call is usually worth more than any
prompt engineering on the five.
</Details>

## Voting, and when it works

Sampling a step several times and taking the majority is the standard repair. Its guarantee is
classical.

<Claim kind="theorem" id="condorcet-hoeffding" title="Majority voting with independent votes">
Let $V_1,\dots,V_k$ be independent indicators of a correct vote, each with $\P(V_i = 1) = p &gt; 1/2$,
and let the decision be the majority. Then
$$
\P(\text{majority incorrect}) \ \le\ \exp\!\left(-2k\left(p-\tfrac12\right)^2\right),
$$
which tends to $0$ as $k\to\infty$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Let $S = \sum_{i=1}^k V_i$, so $\E S = kp$. The majority is incorrect only if $S \le k/2$. Since the
$V_i$ are independent and bounded in $[0,1]$, Hoeffding's inequality gives, for any $t &gt; 0$,
$$
\P\big(S - \E S \le -t\big) \ \le\ \exp\!\left(-\frac{2t^2}{k}\right).
$$
Take $t = kp - k/2 = k(p-\tfrac12) &gt; 0$, which is positive exactly because $p &gt; 1/2$. Then
$$
\P\big(S \le k/2\big) = \P\big(S - \E S \le -t\big) \le \exp\!\left(-\frac{2k^2(p-\tfrac12)^2}{k}\right)
= \exp\!\left(-2k\big(p-\tfrac12\big)^2\right). \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The exponent contains $(p - \tfrac12)^2$. At $p = 0.8$ the bound decays like $e^{-0.18k}$ — about 26
votes to reach 99%. At $p = 0.55$ it decays like $e^{-0.005k}$, needing over 900. Voting rescues a
step that is *reliably better than chance*; it barely touches one that is marginal, and for
$p &lt; 1/2$ it drives confidently to the wrong answer.
</KeyIdea>

## Why it usually helps less than that

<Claim kind="proposition" id="correlated-votes" title="Correlated votes destroy the guarantee">
Suppose the votes are generated as $V_i = Z$ for a single Bernoulli$(p)$ variable $Z$ — perfectly
correlated. Then for every odd $k$,
$$
\P(\text{majority correct}) = p ,
$$
independent of $k$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
All votes equal $Z$, so the majority equals $Z$ and is correct precisely when $Z = 1$, which has
probability $p$ regardless of $k$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Warning>
This degenerate case is not far from reality. Repeated samples from one model at moderate temperature
share the model's systematic misconceptions: if the model has misread the problem, all $k$ samples
misread it the same way. Independence is the assumption the theorem needs and the assumption
sampling does not supply.

What the evidence shows is that self-consistency *does* help on tasks with many distinct valid
reasoning paths converging on one answer — arithmetic and multi-step reasoning — and helps much less
where the failure is a shared misunderstanding. The effective number of independent votes is smaller
than $k$, sometimes very much smaller, and the honest way to use the theorem is as an upper bound on
the benefit. Diversity across *different* models or prompts recovers some genuine independence, which
is why ensembles of unlike models outperform repeated samples of one.
</Warning>

## Recovery changes the exponent

The compounding law assumed a step failure is terminal. Agents that can notice a failure and retry
are playing a different game.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="retry-improvement" title="Detect-and-retry raises the effective step reliability">
Suppose a step succeeds with probability $p$; on failure it is detected with probability $d$; and a
detected failure is retried, independently, up to $m$ times. Then the effective per-step success
probability is
$$
p_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1 - (1-p)\,(1-dp)^{m}.
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
The step ultimately fails only if the initial attempt fails, which has probability $1-p$, and then
each of the $m$ retry opportunities fails to rescue it. A given opportunity rescues the step when the
failure is detected *and* the retry succeeds, with probability $dp$; it fails to rescue with
probability $1 - dp$. By independence across opportunities,
$$
\P(\text{step ultimately fails}) = (1-p)(1-dp)^m,
$$
and $p_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is its complement. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Details summary="Detection is worth more than raw accuracy">
Take $p = 0.9$ and $m = 2$ retries.

| detection $d$ | $p_{\mathrm{eff}}$ | 20-step trajectory |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0 | 0.900 | 0.12 |
| 0.5 | 0.970 | 0.54 |
| 0.9 | 0.994 | 0.89 |
| 0.99 | 0.9992 | 0.98 |

Raising per-step accuracy from 0.90 to 0.994 directly would be a major modelling achievement. Getting
detection to 0.9 with two retries achieves the same trajectory success and is an *engineering*
problem: type checks, schema validation, unit tests on generated code, assertions on tool outputs,
a second model asked only to check.

This is the strongest structural argument for verifiable intermediate steps. It is far easier to
build a checker for a step than to make the step reliable, and the checker's probability enters the
same exponent.
</Details>

<NotSaid>
Every model on this page assumes independence somewhere — across steps, across votes, across retries.
Real agent trajectories violate all three, generally in the unfavourable direction: a confused agent
stays confused, and its self-check is performed by the same confused model. Use these formulas to set
expectations and to compare designs, not to predict a deployment's success rate. The measured
quantity that matters, and that none of this derives, is the *correlation structure* of an agent's
errors along a trajectory.
</NotSaid>

<OpenProblem>
No accepted model predicts an agent's end-to-end success rate from measurable properties of its base
model and its tool environment. The compounding law gives the right qualitative shape and is
quantitatively wrong because of the correlations it ignores; there is no established way to estimate
an effective horizon or an effective independent-vote count in advance.
</OpenProblem>
