<Intuition>
Deep learning works. That is an empirical fact, established by enormous and repeatable evidence. It
is also true that no theorem currently explains it. Those two statements are compatible, and holding
both at once is the honest position.

This page is organised as a ledger. For each topic: what is actually proved, under what hypotheses,
what the theorem does *not* say, what the empirical evidence shows, and what remains open. The
distinction between the columns is the entire point of the page.
</Intuition>

## The starting point: it should not work

<Theorem id="nonconvex-hardness" title="Training a neural network is NP-hard in general">
Deciding whether a given dataset can be fit exactly by a network with two hidden threshold units and
one output unit is NP-complete.
</Theorem>

Quoted from Blum and Rivest. Related results establish hardness for ReLU networks and for
approximate fitting. So there is no algorithm that trains all networks efficiently, and any
explanation of why training works in practice must be an explanation about the *instances* that arise
in practice, not about the problem class.

<Theorem id="what-gd-gives" title="What gradient methods provably give on any smooth loss">
For any $L$-smooth loss bounded below, $T$ steps of gradient descent with $\eta = 1/L$ give
$\min_{t<T}\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2 \le \sqrt{2L\Delta_0/T}$, and $T$ steps of SGD with tuned constant
step give $\frac1T\sum_t\E\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 \lesssim \sqrt{L\sigma^2\Delta_0/T}$.
</Theorem>

These are the theorems on the
<TopicRef id="optimization/gradient-descent-convergence">gradient descent</TopicRef> and
<TopicRef id="stochastic-optimization/sgd-convergence">SGD</TopicRef> pages, and they are the strongest
assumption-free guarantees available.

<NotSaid>
They deliver an **approximate stationary point**. They do not say that point is a local minimum, that
it has low loss, or that it generalises. A neural network loss has exponentially many stationary
points, most of them useless. So the assumption-free theory is compatible with training producing
nothing of value — and the fact that it does not is precisely what needs explaining.

Note also that neural network losses are typically **not** $L$-smooth globally (ReLU has unbounded
curvature at the kink locus in a suitable sense; the loss surface has regions of very large
curvature), so even these theorems apply only under a hypothesis that is not verified in practice.
</NotSaid>

## What overparameterisation buys: the NTK regime

<Definition id="ntk-def" title="Neural tangent kernel">
For a network $f(x;\theta)$, the **tangent features** are $\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta)$ and the
**neural tangent kernel** is
$$
\Theta(x,x') = \ip{\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta)}{\nabla_\theta f(x';\theta)} .
$$
</Definition>

<Theorem id="ntk-convergence" title="Convergence for sufficiently wide networks">
Consider a two-layer ReLU network of width $m$ with standard initialisation, trained by gradient
descent on the squared loss over $n$ data points with $\norm{x_i}_2=1$ and no two inputs parallel.
Let $\lambda_0 = \lambda_{\min}(\Theta^\infty)>0$ be the smallest eigenvalue of the infinite-width
kernel matrix. If
$$
m = \Omega\!\left(\frac{n^6}{\lambda_0^4\delta^3}\right)
\quad\text{and}\quad \eta = O\!\left(\frac{\lambda_0}{n^2}\right),
$$
then with probability at least $1-\delta$ over the initialisation,
$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta_t) \le \left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda_0}{2}\right)^t\mathcal{L}(\theta_0),
$$
so the training loss converges to zero at a linear rate.
</Theorem>

Quoted from Du et al.; Jacot, Gabriel and Hongler introduced the kernel and its infinite-width limit.

<Proposition id="ntk-mechanism" title="Why the NTK argument works — a PL inequality in disguise">
Let $u_t \in\R^n$ be the vector of network outputs on the training set and
$J_t = \partial u_t/\partial\theta$. Gradient flow on
$\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\norm{u - y}_2^2$ gives
$$
\dot u_t = -J_tJ_t^\top(u_t-y) = -\Theta_t\,(u_t-y),
$$
so
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{L}(\theta_t) = -(u_t-y)^\top\Theta_t(u_t-y) \le -\lambda_{\min}(\Theta_t)\,\norm{u_t-y}_2^2
= -2\lambda_{\min}(\Theta_t)\,\mathcal{L}(\theta_t) .
$$
If $\lambda_{\min}(\Theta_t)\ge\lambda_0/2$ for all $t$ along the trajectory, then
$\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)\le e^{-\lambda_0t}\mathcal{L}(\theta_0)$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Gradient flow is $\dot\theta = -\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L} = -J_t^\top(u_t-y)$, so by the chain rule
$\dot u_t = J_t\dot\theta = -J_tJ_t^\top(u_t-y)$. The displayed inequality is the Rayleigh-quotient
bound $v^\top\Theta v\ge\lambda_{\min}(\Theta)\norm v_2^2$. Integrating
$\dot{\mathcal{L}}\le-\lambda_0\mathcal{L}$ gives the exponential decay.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The condition $\dot{\mathcal{L}}\le-2\lambda_{\min}\mathcal{L}$ is exactly a
<TopicRef id="optimization/convex-sets-and-functions">Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality</TopicRef>. So the
entire NTK convergence result is: *width makes the tangent kernel stay near its initialisation, which
makes $\lambda_{\min}(\Theta_t)$ stay bounded away from zero, which gives a PL inequality, which gives
a linear rate*. The non-convexity is not overcome — it is bypassed, by showing the trajectory stays
in a region where the problem behaves like a strongly convex one.

The technical heart is the second step: bounding $\norm{\Theta_t-\Theta_0}$ over the trajectory. That
is what forces the enormous width requirement, since one needs the parameters to move so little that
the Jacobian barely changes.
</KeyIdea>

<Claim kind="open-problem" id="ntk-limits" title="The NTK regime does not describe practical training">
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
Four separate problems with reading the NTK theorem as an explanation of deep learning:

1. **The width requirement is astronomical.** $m = \Omega(n^6/\lambda_0^4)$ with $n = 10^6$ is beyond any conceivable model. Sharper analyses have reduced the exponent, but not to the regime of real networks.
2. **In the NTK regime the network does not learn features.** By construction the parameters barely move, so the tangent features are those at initialisation: the model is a *linear* model in random features, and the "deep" part is inert. Chizat, Oyallon and Bach call this **lazy training** and show it is a consequence of the scaling, not of depth.
3. **NTK-regime networks generalise worse than real ones.** Empirically, the NTK-limit kernel machine underperforms the trained finite network on standard benchmarks, often substantially. So whatever the finite network is doing, it is not what the NTK describes.
4. **The kernel demonstrably drifts.** Measurements of $\Theta_t$ during real training show large changes, especially early. The hypothesis of the theorem is observably false in practice.

The mean-field / $\mu$P line of work studies alternative scalings under which features *do* move in
the infinite-width limit, and is the more promising direction. It has produced genuine results for
two-layer networks and useful hyperparameter-transfer prescriptions; a comparable theory for deep
networks is open.
</NotSaid>

## Implicit bias

Overparameterised networks have infinitely many zero-loss parameter settings, most of which
generalise badly. So the question is not "does training reach zero loss" but "which zero-loss
solution does it reach".

<Theorem id="implicit-bias-linear" title="Implicit bias — the cases that are proved">
- **Linear regression, underdetermined.** Gradient descent from $\beta_0=0$ on $\tfrac12\norm{y-X\beta}_2^2$ converges to the **minimum $\ell^2$-norm** interpolator.
- **Linear classification, separable data.** Gradient descent on the logistic loss has $\theta_t/\norm{\theta_t}_2$ converging to the **maximum-margin** ($\ell^2$) direction, at rate $O(1/\log t)$.
- **Diagonal linear networks.** Parameterising $\beta = u\odot v$ and running gradient descent from small initialisation converges towards the **minimum $\ell^1$-norm** interpolator, so the parameterisation changes the induced norm.
- **Deep matrix factorisation.** Gradient flow on $\norm{Y - W_L\cdots W_1}_F^2$ from small initialisation biases towards low **nuclear norm**, with depth sharpening the effect.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
We prove the first, which is short and shows the mechanism. Gradient descent gives
$$
\beta_{t+1} = \beta_t - \eta X^\top(X\beta_t - y),
$$
so every increment lies in $\operatorname{row}(X) = \operatorname{col}(X^\top)$. Starting from
$\beta_0 = 0$, therefore $\beta_t\in\operatorname{row}(X)$ for all $t$.

If $\beta_\infty$ is any limit point with $X\beta_\infty = y$, then for any other interpolator
$\beta'$ we have $X(\beta'-\beta_\infty) = 0$, i.e. $\beta'-\beta_\infty\in\ker X = \operatorname{row}(X)^\perp$.
Hence $\beta_\infty\perp(\beta'-\beta_\infty)$ and
$$
\norm{\beta'}_2^2 = \norm{\beta_\infty}_2^2 + \norm{\beta'-\beta_\infty}_2^2 \ge \norm{\beta_\infty}_2^2 .
$$
So $\beta_\infty$ is the minimum-norm interpolator.

The other three are quoted from the literature (Soudry et al. for the second; the third and fourth
have their own substantial literature).
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The first proof is one paragraph and it identifies the mechanism precisely: *the algorithm cannot
leave the row space, so the component of the solution in the kernel is whatever the initialisation
put there — namely zero*. The implicit bias is a consequence of the update's algebraic form, not of
anything subtle. That is encouraging, and it is also why the deep case is hard: for a nonlinear
network the increments do not live in a fixed subspace.
</KeyIdea>

<Claim kind="open-problem" id="implicit-bias-open" title="Implicit bias of SGD on deep networks">
There is no characterisation of the solution selected by SGD on a realistic deep network. Partial
results exist for specific architectures, losses, initialisation scales and (usually) gradient flow
rather than discrete stochastic steps, and they do not compose into a general statement.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
This is arguably *the* central technical gap. If the implicit bias were characterised, the
generalisation question would reduce to asking whether the induced complexity measure is small for
the data — a question of the kind learning theory knows how to answer. Without it, we do not even
know what function class the algorithm is effectively searching.

Note also that the discrete/continuous distinction is not cosmetic: gradient descent with a finite
step has a different implicit bias from gradient flow (there are extra terms of order $\eta$ that
penalise gradient norm), and the SGD noise adds further terms. Results proved for flow do not
transfer automatically.
</NotSaid>

## Saddle points and the loss landscape

<Theorem id="saddle-escape" title="Perturbed gradient descent escapes strict saddles">
Let $f$ be $L$-smooth with $\rho$-Lipschitz Hessian. Perturbed gradient descent — gradient descent
with small isotropic noise added when the gradient is small — finds an $\eps$-second-order stationary
point, i.e. a point with
$$
\norm{\nabla f(x)}_2\le\eps \quad\text{and}\quad \lambda_{\min}\big(\nabla^2f(x)\big)\ge-\sqrt{\rho\eps},
$$
in $\tilde O(1/\eps^2)$ iterations, with only polylogarithmic dependence on the dimension.
</Theorem>

Quoted from Jin et al. The intuition is that a strict saddle has a descent direction, and isotropic
noise has a component along it with high probability, so the iterate is pushed off the saddle's
stable manifold. This is a genuine and satisfying result.

<NotSaid>
It gives an *approximate second-order* stationary point, which rules out strict saddles but not:
degenerate saddles with $\lambda_{\min}=0$, poor local minima, or plateaus. And deep network losses
are known to have degenerate critical points in abundance — every permutation of hidden units gives a
symmetry, and every dead ReLU gives a flat direction. The theorem is progress on one obstruction, not
on all of them.
</NotSaid>

## Flatness, sharpness and the edge of stability

<Claim kind="empirical" id="flat-minima" title="Flat minima and generalisation">
Across many experiments, solutions with smaller Hessian eigenvalues at the minimum generalise better,
and algorithms that explicitly seek flat minima (SAM and relatives) improve test accuracy.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The correlation is real and reproducible. The causal claim is not established, and there is a sharp
objection. Dinh et al. showed that for a ReLU network the map
$(W_1,W_2)\mapsto(\alpha W_1,\alpha^{-1}W_2)$ leaves the function — hence the test error — exactly
unchanged while changing the Hessian spectrum by factors of $\alpha^{\pm2}$. So **"sharpness" is not
a property of the learned function**; it is a property of the parameterisation, and can be made
arbitrarily large or small without touching generalisation.

Responses have been to define reparameterisation-invariant sharpness measures (adaptive sharpness,
information-theoretic or PAC-Bayes flatness), and some of these correlate with generalisation more
robustly. But no theorem currently shows that flatness in any such sense implies generalisation for
realistic networks. Treat "flat minima generalise" as a well-supported empirical heuristic with a
known counterexample to its naive form.
</NotSaid>

<Claim kind="empirical" id="edge-of-stability" title="Edge of stability">
Cohen et al. document that full-batch gradient descent on neural networks typically has the sharpness
$\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2\mathcal{L})$ *rise* during training until it reaches approximately $2/\eta$,
and then hover just above it, with the loss decreasing non-monotonically.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The observation is striking because $2/\eta$ is exactly the classical stability threshold of the
<TopicRef id="optimization/gradient-descent-convergence">quadratic analysis</TopicRef>: gradient
descent on a quadratic with curvature above $2/\eta$ diverges. So training operates at the boundary of
where the standard theory applies, and *beyond* the regime where descent-lemma arguments are valid.

This has a direct consequence: convergence proofs assuming $\eta \le 1/L$ with $L$ the true
smoothness constant do not describe what happens, because the effective $L$ adapts to $\eta$ rather
than the reverse. Theory for the edge-of-stability regime exists for specific simplified models and
is an active area; there is no general account.
</NotSaid>

## Generalisation, memorisation and grokking

The foundational empirical result here is Zhang et al.: standard architectures can fit **random
labels** on standard datasets to zero training error. So the hypothesis class is rich enough to
memorise, and any explanation of generalisation must be about the algorithm and the data, not the
architecture's capacity. This is also why
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/erm-and-uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</TopicRef> bounds over
the architecture's function class are vacuous here.

<Claim kind="empirical" id="grokking-status" title="Grokking">
On small algorithmic tasks (modular arithmetic, group operations), networks reach $100\%$ training
accuracy quickly while remaining at chance on held-out data, and then — after many thousands of
further steps with no visible change in training loss — jump to $100\%$ test accuracy.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
Several mechanisms have been proposed: a slow transition from a memorising to a generalising circuit
driven by weight decay; a competition between a fast-to-find memorisation solution and a
slower-to-find but lower-norm generalising one; a delayed alignment of representations. Each has
supporting evidence on specific tasks, and mechanistic-interpretability work has reverse-engineered
the learned circuit in the modular-addition case in convincing detail.

What is not established is a general theory: which tasks grok, how long the delay is, or whether the
phenomenon occurs in large-scale training at all (the evidence is largely from small models on
algorithmic data). It is an unusually clean experimental handle on the memorisation-to-generalisation
transition, and that is its main value.
</NotSaid>

<Claim kind="empirical" id="scaling-laws-status" title="Scaling laws">
Test loss follows
$$
\mathcal{L}(N,D) \approx \mathcal{L}_\infty + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}
$$
in parameters $N$ and tokens $D$, with fitted exponents, over several orders of magnitude. Hoffmann
et al. (Chinchilla) fit this form and conclude that compute-optimal training scales $N$ and $D$
roughly in proportion, revising the earlier Kaplan et al. prescription.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
This is among the most useful empirical regularities in the field — it is what makes large training
runs planable — and it is *not derived*. There are suggestive arguments connecting power-law loss
curves to power-law data spectra, to intrinsic dimension of the data manifold, and to
nonparametric-rate calculations of the kind on the
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</TopicRef> page. None of them
predicts the observed exponents from properties of the data, and none explains the functional form's
remarkable stability across modalities and architectures.

Two further cautions. The fitted form is an extrapolation and has been revised when refit with better
methodology — the Kaplan-to-Chinchilla correction moved the recommended data-to-parameter ratio by
more than an order of magnitude, from a fit to the same functional family. And the laws describe
*loss*, not capability; the relationship between loss and downstream behaviour is itself not a
smooth function and is much less well characterised.
</NotSaid>

## The ledger

| Question | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Does gradient descent reach a stationary point? | **Proved**, with rates, under smoothness |
| Does it reach zero training loss for wide enough nets? | **Proved** in the NTK regime; width requirement unrealistic |
| Is the NTK regime what happens in practice? | **No** — kernel drifts, features are learned |
| Which interpolating solution does GD select? | **Proved** for linear models and a few simple architectures; **open** in general |
| Can first-order methods escape saddles? | **Proved** for strict saddles with noise |
| Do flat minima generalise better? | **Correlated**; naive sharpness is reparameterisation-dependent; causal claim **open** |
| Why does training operate at the edge of stability? | **Open**; documented empirically |
| Why do overparameterised nets generalise? | **Open** — the central question |
| Why do scaling laws hold, with those exponents? | **Open**; empirically very robust |
| Is there a non-vacuous generalisation bound for a state-of-the-art model? | **No** |

<Claim kind="open-problem" id="the-central-open-problem" title="The central open problem">
Given an architecture, a dataset and a training procedure, there is no theory that predicts in
advance that training will find a solution which generalises — nor a non-vacuous bound on its test
error. Every quantitative guarantee available today is either vacuous at realistic scale or holds
under hypotheses that practical training violates.
</Claim>

<Note title="How to read this page">
None of the above is an argument that theory is useless. The theorems on this site explain why the
$1/\sqrt{d_k}$ scaling is there, why residual connections help, why AdamW differs from Adam with
$L^2$, why batch size and learning rate interact the way they do, and why the sample covariance
cannot be trusted when $p\approx n$. Theory that explains *components* is doing real work even when
theory that explains *the whole* does not exist.

What the page argues against is the habit of citing a theorem whose hypotheses fail as though it were
an explanation. "Deep learning works because neural networks are universal approximators", "because
SGD finds flat minima", "because of the NTK" — each of these attaches a real theorem to a real
phenomenon across a gap that the theorem does not bridge.
</Note>
