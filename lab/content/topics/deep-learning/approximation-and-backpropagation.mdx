## From the perceptron to a hidden layer

<Proposition id="perceptron-limits" title="A single threshold unit cannot represent XOR">
There is no $(w,b)\in\R^2\times\R$ with
$\sign(\ip w{x}+b)$ equal to $x_1\oplus x_2$ on $\{0,1\}^2$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Suppose such $(w,b)$ existed. XOR requires
$$
\ip w{(0,0)}+b<0,\quad \ip w{(1,0)}+b>0,\quad \ip w{(0,1)}+b>0,\quad \ip w{(1,1)}+b<0,
$$
i.e. $b<0$, $w_1+b>0$, $w_2+b>0$, $w_1+w_2+b<0$. Adding the second and third gives
$w_1+w_2+2b>0$, so $w_1+w_2 > -2b$. The fourth gives $w_1+w_2<-b$. Hence $-2b<-b$, i.e. $b>0$,
contradicting $b<0$.
</Proof>

This is the observation that halted the first wave of neural network research, and the fix — compose
two layers — is what the next theorem generalises.

## Universal approximation

<Theorem id="universal-approximation" title="Universal approximation theorem">
Let $\rho:\R\to\R$ be continuous and **not a polynomial**. Then for every compact $K\subseteq\R^d$,
every continuous $f:K\to\R$ and every $\eps>0$, there exist $m\in\N$, $w_j\in\R^d$, $b_j\in\R$ and
$c_j\in\R$ with
$$
\sup_{x\in K}\left|f(x) - \sum_{j=1}^m c_j\,\rho\big(\ip{w_j}{x}+b_j\big)\right| < \eps .
$$
</Theorem>

Quoted from Leshno et al., which gives the sharp hypothesis; Cybenko and Hornik proved earlier
versions for sigmoidal and for bounded non-constant activations. The proof is a functional-analysis
argument (the set of such sums is a subalgebra-like family whose closure is shown to be all of
$C(K)$, via Stone–Weierstrass or via a Hahn–Banach separation and a Fourier/Radon-transform
computation).

<Note title="Why 'not a polynomial' is exactly right">
If $\rho$ is a polynomial of degree $k$ then every $\rho(\ip wx+b)$ is a polynomial of degree $\le k$,
so finite sums of them lie in the finite-dimensional space of degree-$\le k$ polynomials — which is
not dense in $C(K)$. Conversely non-polynomiality is enough. Note that this makes the theorem apply
to ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, GELU and essentially every activation in use, and it shows the theorem cannot
be the reason one is better than another.
</Note>

<Proposition id="ua-says-nothing" title="What universal approximation does not give">
The theorem is a statement about the **closure** of a set of functions. It provides:

- no bound on $m$ — the width may need to grow exponentially in $d$ or in $1/\eps$;
- no algorithm for finding the parameters;
- no statement about generalisation from finite data;
- no distinction between activations, architectures or depths.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Each is a matter of what the statement quantifies over. The width bound: the theorem asserts
existence of *some* $m$; and there are lower bounds showing $m = \Omega(\eps^{-d})$ is necessary for
the class of Lipschitz $f$ on $[0,1]^d$, matching the nonparametric rate on the
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</TopicRef> page. The algorithmic
gap: training a network with even two hidden units to a given accuracy is NP-hard in general, so
existence of an approximator does not imply it is findable. The statistical gap: the theorem is about
approximating a *known* $f$ uniformly, whereas learning has access only to samples, and the
approximation error is only one of the two terms in the
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/erm-and-uniform-convergence">excess risk decomposition</TopicRef>.
</Proof>

<Warning title="A very common over-claim">
"Neural networks can approximate any function, which is why deep learning works." The first clause is
a theorem; the second does not follow from it. Polynomials, splines, kernel machines with a universal
kernel and Fourier series are all universal approximators too, and none of them works as well as deep
networks on image or language data. Universality is necessary and very far from sufficient; whatever
explains deep learning's success is a statement about *which* functions are easy to represent and
*which* are easy to find, not about what is representable in principle.
</Warning>

<Theorem id="depth-separation" title="Depth separation">
There is a function $g:\R^d\to\R$, expressible exactly by a network with three layers and
$O(\mathrm{poly}(d))$ units, such that any two-layer network approximating $g$ to constant accuracy in
$L^2$ over a natural measure requires $e^{\Omega(d)}$ units.
</Theorem>

Quoted from Eldan and Shamir; Telgarsky gives a complementary one-dimensional construction separating
depth $k$ from depth $O(k^{1/3})$ using a sawtooth function whose oscillation count grows
exponentially with depth. These are the strongest rigorous statements available about the value of
depth, and it is worth being clear about their scope: they show depth *can* buy exponential
efficiency on specially constructed functions. They do not show that the functions arising in
practice have this character.

## Backpropagation

Represent the network as a computational graph: nodes are intermediate values, edges are elementary
operations. For a feedforward net,
$$
a^{(0)} = x, \qquad
z^{(\ell)} = W^{(\ell)}a^{(\ell-1)}+b^{(\ell)}, \qquad
a^{(\ell)} = \rho\big(z^{(\ell)}\big),
\qquad \ell = 1,\dots,L,
$$
with scalar loss $\mathcal{J} = \ell\big(a^{(L)},y\big)$.

<Theorem id="backprop-correct" title="Backpropagation computes the exact gradient">
Define the adjoints $\delta^{(\ell)} = \partial\mathcal{J}/\partial z^{(\ell)}$. Then
$$
\delta^{(L)} = \nabla_{a^{(L)}}\ell \odot \rho'\big(z^{(L)}\big),
\qquad
\delta^{(\ell)} = \Big(\big(W^{(\ell+1)}\big)^\top\delta^{(\ell+1)}\Big)\odot\rho'\big(z^{(\ell)}\big),
$$
and
$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial W^{(\ell)}} = \delta^{(\ell)}\big(a^{(\ell-1)}\big)^\top,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial b^{(\ell)}} = \delta^{(\ell)} .
$$
These are exact derivatives (no finite differences), computed in time $O(\text{forward pass})$ and
memory $O(\sum_\ell\dim a^{(\ell)})$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Apply the <TopicRef id="foundations/matrix-calculus">chain rule</TopicRef> to the graph. For the last
layer, $\mathcal{J}$ depends on $z^{(L)}$ through $a^{(L)} = \rho(z^{(L)})$, and $\rho$ acts
coordinatewise, so its Jacobian is $\diag(\rho'(z^{(L)}))$, giving the Hadamard product.

For a general layer, $z^{(\ell)}$ influences $\mathcal{J}$ only through $a^{(\ell)}$ and then
$z^{(\ell+1)} = W^{(\ell+1)}a^{(\ell)}+b^{(\ell+1)}$. Hence
$$
\delta^{(\ell)} = \left(\frac{\partial a^{(\ell)}}{\partial z^{(\ell)}}\right)^\top
\left(\frac{\partial z^{(\ell+1)}}{\partial a^{(\ell)}}\right)^\top\delta^{(\ell+1)}
= \diag\big(\rho'(z^{(\ell)})\big)\big(W^{(\ell+1)}\big)^\top\delta^{(\ell+1)} ,
$$
which is the stated recursion. For the parameters, $z^{(\ell)}_p = \sum_q W^{(\ell)}_{pq}a^{(\ell-1)}_q + b^{(\ell)}_p$,
so $\partial z^{(\ell)}_p/\partial W^{(\ell)}_{pq} = a^{(\ell-1)}_q$ and the chain rule gives the outer
product.

Exactness is just the chain rule being an identity. The cost statement is the general
<TopicRef id="foundations/matrix-calculus">reverse-mode</TopicRef> result: each layer's backward step
is one matrix–vector product with $(W^{(\ell)})^\top$, the same cost as the forward matrix–vector
product with $W^{(\ell)}$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Backpropagation is not an algorithm specific to neural networks and it is not an approximation. It is
reverse-mode automatic differentiation applied to a particular graph, and the reason it is cheap is
the one on the <TopicRef id="foundations/matrix-calculus">matrix calculus</TopicRef> page: many
inputs, one output, so accumulate the Jacobian product from the left. The "many parameters, one loss"
shape of deep learning is what makes it affordable, and the memory cost of storing activations is the
price.
</KeyIdea>

<Proposition id="chain-rule-vanishing" title="Gradient magnitude through a deep chain">
With $J^{(\ell)} = \diag(\rho'(z^{(\ell)}))\big(W^{(\ell)}\big)^\top$, the input-layer adjoint is
$$
\delta^{(1)} = J^{(1)}J^{(2)}\cdots J^{(L-1)}\,\delta^{(L)},
$$
so
$$
\norm{\delta^{(1)}}_2 \le \left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\norm{J^{(\ell)}}\right)\norm{\delta^{(L)}}_2,
$$
and generically $\norm{\delta^{(1)}}_2 \approx c^{L}$ for $c$ the typical singular value. The gradient
is therefore **exponential in depth**, vanishing for $c<1$ and exploding for $c>1$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The recursion of the previous theorem composed over layers gives the product form; the norm bound is
submultiplicativity of the operator norm. For the generic statement: if the $J^{(\ell)}$ are
independent with typical spectral behaviour, $\log\norm{\prod_\ell J^{(\ell)}}$ is a sum of $L$ roughly
i.i.d. terms, so by the law of large numbers it grows linearly in $L$ with slope
$\E\log\norm{J}$ — hence exponential growth or decay unless that slope is exactly zero.
</Proof>

<Note title="Why this proposition explains four architectural choices">
Everything in modern deep learning that looks like a trick is an attempt to keep
$\prod_\ell\norm{J^{(\ell)}}$ near $1$:

- **Careful initialisation** (He, Glorot) sets $\Var(W_{ij}) = 2/n_{\text{in}}$ so that $\E\norm{J^{(\ell)}}\approx1$ at the start of training.
- **Residual connections** replace $J^{(\ell)}$ with $I + \tilde J^{(\ell)}$, whose product stays near $I$ when the $\tilde J$ are small — this is the mathematical content of ResNet.
- **Normalisation layers** rescale $z^{(\ell)}$ so that $\rho'$ operates in its non-saturated range, keeping $\diag(\rho'(z))$ away from zero.
- **Gating** in LSTMs and GRUs creates paths where the effective Jacobian is exactly $I$, which is the subject of the <TopicRef id="deep-learning/convolution-recurrence-normalisation">recurrence</TopicRef> page.

None of these is a heuristic in need of an explanation; each targets this specific product.
</Note>
