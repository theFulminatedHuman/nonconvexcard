## Convolution is forced by equivariance

<Definition id="conv-def" title="Discrete convolution">
For signals on $\Z_n$ (cyclically indexed),
$$
(k * x)[i] = \sum_{j\in\Z_n} k[j]\,x[i-j] .
$$
Let $T_s$ denote the shift $(T_sx)[i] = x[i-s]$.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="conv-equivariance" title="Translation-equivariant linear maps are exactly convolutions">
Let $A:\R^{\Z_n}\to\R^{\Z_n}$ be linear. Then
$$
A T_s = T_s A \quad\text{for all } s\in\Z_n
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
Ax = k*x \ \text{ for some } k .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
($\Leftarrow$) Compute: $(k*(T_sx))[i] = \sum_jk[j]x[i-s-j] = (k*x)[i-s] = (T_s(k*x))[i]$.

($\Rightarrow$) Let $e_0$ be the indicator of index $0$ and set $k = Ae_0$. Since $e_m = T_me_0$,
equivariance gives $Ae_m = AT_me_0 = T_mAe_0 = T_mk$. Any $x = \sum_mx[m]e_m$, so by linearity
$$
(Ax)[i] = \sum_m x[m]\,(T_mk)[i] = \sum_m x[m]\,k[i-m] = (k*x)[i].
$$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Convolution is not a clever choice of architecture; it is the *only* linear map commuting with
translation. So a designer who wants translation equivariance has no freedom at all in the linear
part — the weight sharing in a CNN is a theorem, not a heuristic. This is the template for the whole
geometric-deep-learning programme: fix a symmetry group, and the equivariant linear maps are forced.
Cohen and Welling carry this out for rotations and reflections.
</KeyIdea>

<Theorem id="conv-theorem" title="Convolution theorem">
Let $\hat x[\omega] = \sum_i x[i]e^{-2\pi\i\omega i/n}$ be the discrete Fourier transform. Then
$$
\widehat{k*x}[\omega] = \hat k[\omega]\,\hat x[\omega].
$$
Equivalently: the Fourier basis diagonalises every convolution operator simultaneously, with
eigenvalues $\hat k[\omega]$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
$$
\widehat{k*x}[\omega] = \sum_i\sum_j k[j]x[i-j]e^{-2\pi\i\omega i/n}.
$$
Substitute $i = j+m$ and split the exponential:
$$
= \sum_jk[j]e^{-2\pi\i\omega j/n}\sum_m x[m]e^{-2\pi\i\omega m/n} = \hat k[\omega]\hat x[\omega].
$$
For the diagonalisation statement: the character $\chi_\omega[i] = e^{2\pi\i\omega i/n}$ satisfies
$k*\chi_\omega = \hat k[\omega]\,\chi_\omega$ by the same computation, so each $\chi_\omega$ is an
eigenvector with the stated eigenvalue.
</Proof>

<Note title="What the Fourier view buys">
Three things. It makes the $O(n\log n)$ FFT-based convolution possible. It explains what a learned
filter *is* — a frequency response, which is why visualising early CNN filters shows oriented
band-pass structures. And it makes the effect of stacking transparent: composing convolutions
multiplies frequency responses, so a deep stack of small filters can realise a sharp frequency
selectivity that no single small filter can.
</Note>

<Proposition id="receptive-field" title="Receptive field growth">
Stacking $L$ convolutions of kernel size $k$ with stride $1$ gives receptive field
$1 + L(k-1)$ — linear in depth. Using dilation $2^{\ell}$ at layer $\ell$ gives receptive field
$1+(k-1)(2^L-1)$ — exponential in depth.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Each layer of kernel size $k$ and dilation $d$ extends the receptive field by $d(k-1)$. Summing with
$d = 1$ gives $L(k-1)$; summing with $d = 2^{\ell-1}$ for $\ell=1,\dots,L$ gives
$(k-1)\sum_{\ell<L}2^\ell = (k-1)(2^L-1)$.
</Proof>

Pooling and stride have the same effect by a different mechanism: they reduce spatial resolution so
that a fixed kernel covers a larger fraction of the original input. Note that pooling also destroys
equivariance and replaces it with approximate **invariance** — a max over a window is unchanged by
small shifts within the window, which is what is wanted at the output but not in the middle.

## Recurrence and the vanishing gradient

<Definition id="rnn-def" title="Vanilla RNN">
$$
h_t = \rho\big(W h_{t-1} + Ux_t + b\big),\qquad t=1,\dots,T,
$$
with output $\hat y = g(h_T)$ and loss $\mathcal{J}$.
</Definition>

<Proposition id="rnn-jacobian" title="The RNN gradient is a product of recurrent Jacobians">
$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial h_1}
= \left(\prod_{t=2}^{T} \diag\big(\rho'(z_t)\big)W^\top\right)\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial h_T},
$$
where $z_t = Wh_{t-1}+Ux_t+b$. Consequently
$$
\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial h_1}\right\|_2
\le \big(\norm{\rho'}_\infty\norm{W}\big)^{T-1}\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial h_T}\right\|_2 .
$$
If $\norm{\rho'}_\infty\norm W<1$ the gradient vanishes exponentially in $T$; if
$\sigma_{\min}(W)\norm{\rho'}>1$ it explodes.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The chain rule through the recurrence gives
$\partial h_t/\partial h_{t-1} = \diag(\rho'(z_t))W$, and composing over $t$ gives the product; the
transpose appears because the adjoint propagates backwards. The bound is submultiplicativity. Note
that **the same matrix $W$ appears at every step**, so unlike the feedforward case in the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning/approximation-and-backpropagation">previous page</TopicRef> there is no
averaging over independent layers: the growth rate is determined by the single spectral quantity
$\norm{\rho'}_\infty\norm W$, with no possibility of the fluctuations cancelling.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
For $\tanh$, $\norm{\rho'}_\infty = 1$, so stability requires $\norm W \approx 1$ exactly — a measure-zero
condition. Either the gradient dies and the network cannot learn dependencies beyond a few dozen
steps, or it explodes and training diverges. Gradient clipping addresses the explosion (it bounds the
step, at the cost of biasing the direction); nothing about a vanilla RNN addresses the vanishing.
</KeyIdea>

<Proposition id="lstm-gating" title="The LSTM cell state has an additive path">
The LSTM equations are
$$
\begin{aligned}
f_t &= \sigma(W_f[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_f) &&\text{(forget)}\\
i_t &= \sigma(W_i[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_i) &&\text{(input)}\\
o_t &= \sigma(W_o[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_o) &&\text{(output)}\\
\tilde c_t &= \tanh(W_c[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_c) &&\text{(candidate)}\\
c_t &= f_t\odot c_{t-1} + i_t\odot\tilde c_t &&\text{(cell state)}\\
h_t &= o_t\odot\tanh(c_t). &&\text{(hidden state)}
\end{aligned}
$$
Along the cell-state path,
$$
\frac{\partial c_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} = \diag(f_t),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\frac{\partial c_T}{\partial c_1} = \diag\left(\prod_{t=2}^{T}f_t\right)
$$
(ignoring the indirect dependence through $h_{t-1}$). With $f_t \approx 1$ this product is
$\approx1$ for any $T$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Differentiate $c_t = f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde c_t$ with respect to $c_{t-1}$, treating
$f_t,i_t,\tilde c_t$ as functions of $h_{t-1}$ and $x_t$ only. The first term contributes
$\diag(f_t)$ and the second contributes nothing. Composing over $t$ multiplies the diagonals.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Compare the two propositions. In a vanilla RNN the gradient is a product of **matrices** $W^\top$,
whose norm is generically $\ne1$ and cannot be controlled per time step. In an LSTM the cell-state
gradient is a product of **diagonal matrices with entries in $(0,1)$**, and each entry is *learned*.
Setting $f_t\approx1$ gives an identity path — the "constant error carousel" of the original paper —
so the network can learn which information to preserve and for how long. The gating is not a
heuristic addition; it is the minimal modification that turns an uncontrollable matrix product into a
controllable scalar one.

The GRU makes the same move with fewer parameters, coupling the input and forget gates:
$$
h_t = (1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1} + z_t\odot\tilde h_t ,
$$
so the Jacobian along the state path is $\diag(1-z_t)$. Residual connections in a
<TopicRef id="deep-learning/transformers">transformer</TopicRef> are the same idea again with the gate
fixed at $1$: $\partial(x+F(x))/\partial x = I + \partial F/\partial x$.
</KeyIdea>

## Normalisation

<Definition id="norm-def" title="Batch and layer normalisation">
For a pre-activation $z$, both compute $\hat z = \gamma\odot\frac{z-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\eps}}+\beta$;
they differ in what the statistics are computed over:

- **Batch norm**: $\mu,\sigma^2$ per feature, averaged over the batch. Depends on other examples; requires running statistics at inference.
- **Layer norm**: $\mu,\sigma^2$ per example, averaged over features. Independent of the batch, which is why it is used in transformers and in any setting with variable-length sequences or batch size one.
- **RMS norm**: layer norm without mean subtraction, $\hat z = \gamma\odot z/\sqrt{\overline{z^2}+\eps}$.
</Definition>

<Proposition id="norm-scale-invariance" title="Normalisation makes the layer scale-invariant in its weights">
If $\hat z = (Wx - \mu)/\sigma$ with $\mu,\sigma$ the empirical mean and standard deviation of $Wx$,
then replacing $W$ by $cW$ for $c>0$ leaves $\hat z$ unchanged. Consequently the gradient with
respect to $W$ satisfies
$$
\nabla_{cW}\mathcal{J} = \frac1c\nabla_W\mathcal{J}\Big|_{W} ,
$$
so the effective step size scales as $1/\norm W^2$ relative to the direction of $W$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Both $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are homogeneous of degree one in $W$, so the ratio is homogeneous of degree
zero: $\hat z(cW) = \hat z(W)$. Differentiating the identity $\mathcal{J}(cW) = \mathcal{J}(W)$ in
$W$ gives $c\,\nabla\mathcal{J}(cW) = \nabla\mathcal{J}(W)$.
</Proof>

This is a genuine and often-overlooked consequence: with normalisation, the *norm* of a weight matrix
has no effect on the function, only on the effective learning rate. Weight decay in a normalised
network therefore acts purely as a learning-rate schedule, which explains several otherwise puzzling
empirical observations about the interaction of weight decay and learning rate.

<Claim kind="empirical" id="batchnorm-mechanism" title="Why batch normalisation helps is not settled">
Batch norm reliably accelerates training and permits larger learning rates. The original explanation
— that it reduces "internal covariate shift" — has been directly challenged.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
Santurkar et al. ran the decisive experiment: they injected non-zero-mean, time-varying noise
*after* batch norm layers, deliberately restoring covariate shift, and the training speed-up
persisted. So covariate shift reduction is not the mechanism, or at least not the whole one.

Their proposed alternative is that batch norm makes the loss landscape smoother, with smaller and
more predictable gradient and Hessian norms along the training path, which permits larger stable
steps. They provide supporting measurements and some analysis in restricted settings. Other proposed
mechanisms include the scale-invariance effect above and an implicit regularisation from batch noise.

The state of knowledge: batch norm works, the original explanation is wrong, and there are several
partial explanations with supporting evidence and no consensus. This is a good example of a case
where the engineering is far ahead of the theory, and of why it is worth labelling such claims as
empirical rather than presenting a plausible story as established.
</NotSaid>
