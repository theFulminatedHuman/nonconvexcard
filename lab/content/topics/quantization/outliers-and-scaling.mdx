<Intuition>
Quantizing the weights of a large language model is comparatively easy. Quantizing its *activations*
is not, and the reason is concentrated in a few channels. In a large transformer, a small number of
hidden dimensions carry values perhaps fifty times larger than everything else — consistently, across
inputs, in the same dimensions. A single scale covering the whole tensor must span that range, and
almost all of the grid is then spent on values that never occur.
</Intuition>

## The observation

<Claim kind="empirical" id="outlier-emergence" title="Systematic outlier features in large transformers">
In transformer language models above roughly 6–7 billion parameters, a small set of hidden dimensions
— on the order of tens out of thousands — carries activation magnitudes one to two orders of
magnitude above the median channel. The affected dimensions are largely consistent across inputs and
across layers, and zeroing them degrades the model sharply.
</Claim>

<Warning>
This is a measurement, reproduced across model families, and it is **not** explained by any theory.
There is no derivation predicting which dimensions become outliers, why the phenomenon has a scale
threshold, or whether it is intrinsic to the architecture or an artefact of the optimiser and
normalisation choices. Papers that describe outlier features as "emergent" are naming the
observation, not accounting for it. Do not carry an explanation into this that the evidence does not
support.
</Warning>

## Why a few large values are so expensive

<Claim kind="proposition" id="bit-loss" title="Outliers cost bits directly">
Consider a symmetric $b$-bit quantizer whose range is set by the tensor maximum, $A = \max_i|x_i|$.
Let $\tau$ be a typical magnitude, say the median of $|x_i|$, and $R = A/\tau$ the outlier ratio.
Then the number of quantization levels covering $[-\tau,\tau]$ is
$$
2^{b}/R,
$$
so ordinary values are represented as if with
$$
b_{\text{eff}} = b - \log_2 R
$$
bits.
</Claim>

<Proof>
The quantizer places $2^b$ levels uniformly across $[-A, A]$, i.e. with spacing
$\Delta = 2A/2^b$. The subinterval $[-\tau, \tau]$ has width $2\tau$ and therefore contains
$2\tau/\Delta = 2^{b}\tau/A = 2^b/R$ levels. A $b_{\text{eff}}$-bit quantizer covering $[-\tau,\tau]$
would supply $2^{b_{\text{eff}}}$ levels; equating gives
$b_{\text{eff}} = b - \log_2 R$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

At $R = 50$ this costs $\log_2 50 \approx 5.6$ bits. An 8-bit quantizer is then behaving like a
2.4-bit one for the values that make up almost the whole tensor — which is why naive per-tensor INT8
activation quantization of a large model collapses, while the same scheme on a small model is fine.

<KeyIdea>
Finer granularity attacks $R$ directly. Per-channel or per-group scales mean an outlier inflates $R$
only within *its own* group, leaving every other group at full effective precision. For weights this
is straightforward. For activations it is awkward: the reduction axis of the matrix multiply is the
one you would need to scale along, and a per-token scale must be computed at run time.
</KeyIdea>

## Moving the problem

<Claim kind="proposition" id="diagonal-invariance" title="Diagonal scale migration is exact">
Let $X \in \R^{n\times d}$, $W \in \R^{d \times m}$, and let $S = \operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_d)$
with all $s_j \ne 0$. Then
$$
XW \;=\; \big(XS^{-1}\big)\big(SW\big).
$$
The transformation rescales column $j$ of $X$ by $1/s_j$ and row $j$ of $W$ by $s_j$, and changes the
layer's output not at all.
</Claim>

<Proof>
$S$ is invertible, so $S^{-1}S = I$ and $(XS^{-1})(SW) = X(S^{-1}S)W = XW$. The entrywise description
follows from $\big(XS^{-1}\big)_{ij} = X_{ij}/s_j$ and $\big(SW\big)_{jk} = s_jW_{jk}$.
$\blacksquare$
</Proof>

Trivial as mathematics, and the basis of the main practical technique: the *function* is invariant,
but the *quantization error* is not, because the two factors are quantized after the rescaling. Pick
$s_j$ large for channels where activations are large, and the activation outlier is damped while the
corresponding weight row grows.

<Details summary="Choosing the migration strength">
SmoothQuant parameterises the choice as
$$
s_j = \frac{\big(\max_i |X_{ij}|\big)^{\alpha}}{\big(\max_k |W_{jk}|\big)^{1-\alpha}},
\qquad \alpha \in [0,1].
$$
At $\alpha = 0$ nothing moves; at $\alpha = 1$ all the activation range is flattened and pushed into
the weights. Intermediate $\alpha$ — around $0.5$ for many models — equalises the difficulty, and the
right value depends on how much headroom the weight quantizer has.

The reason a migration helps at all is that the two operands are *not* symmetric in difficulty.
Weights are static: their range is known exactly, offline, and can be handled per output channel at
no run-time cost. Activations are input-dependent, must be handled with a scale computed per token or
estimated from calibration, and carry the outliers. Trading a hard problem for an easier one of the
same total size is a real gain, and the invariance above is what makes the trade free.

Note also that $S$ can usually be folded into the preceding layer norm or linear layer's parameters,
so the rescaling of $X$ costs nothing at run time.
</Details>

## Protecting the channels that matter

A second use of the same invariance: rather than equalising ranges, scale up the weight channels that
the activations actually excite.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="awq-error-scaling" title="Scaling up a salient channel reduces its relative quantization error">
Consider group-wise symmetric quantization with step $\Delta = 2A/2^b$ where $A$ is the maximum
magnitude in the group. Multiply one channel's weights by $s &gt; 1$ and divide the matching
activations by $s$, per the invariance. If the group maximum is unchanged — that is, the scaled
channel is still not the largest in its group, so the step stays $\Delta$ — then the contribution of
that channel to the layer output has quantization error scaled by exactly $1/s$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Write the channel's contribution as $x w$. Unscaled, the quantized weight is $w + e$ with
$|e| \le \Delta/2$, giving output error $|x||e| \le |x|\Delta/2$.

After migration the channel computes $(x/s)\cdot Q(sw)$. By assumption the group's step is still
$\Delta$, so $Q(sw) = sw + e'$ with $|e'| \le \Delta/2$, and
$$
\frac{x}{s}\,Q(sw) = \frac{x}{s}\big(sw + e'\big) = xw + \frac{x e'}{s}.
$$
The output error is now at most $|x|\Delta/(2s)$, smaller by the factor $s$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Assumptions>
The hypothesis "the group maximum is unchanged" is doing all the work and is the reason the technique
is applied to a *small fraction* of channels. Scaling many channels, or scaling one past the group
maximum, raises $A$ and hence $\Delta$ for the whole group — which increases the error on every other
channel in it. The gain is a redistribution, not a free lunch: it is worth taking only when the
protected channels matter disproportionately to the output, which is what "activation-aware" means
in practice.
</Assumptions>

<NotSaid>
None of this bounds end-to-end degradation. The propositions above control error in one channel's
contribution to one layer's pre-activation, under stated conditions. They say nothing about how that
perturbation propagates through the remaining layers, nor about generation quality. As in the
Hessian-aware setting, the case that these methods preserve model quality is empirical.
</NotSaid>

<OpenProblem>
Why outlier features exist at all is open. Candidate accounts — that they implement a soft
attention-sink or no-op mechanism, that they are a consequence of layer normalisation interacting
with softmax saturation, that they are an artefact of Adam's per-coordinate scaling — each explain
part of the evidence and none predicts which dimensions will be affected in a model not yet trained.
An account that did would likely also suggest how to train models that do not develop them, which
would make most of this page unnecessary.
</OpenProblem>
