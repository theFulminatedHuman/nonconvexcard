<Intuition>
Quantization replaces a real number with the nearest point of a finite grid. Two things can go wrong:
the grid may be too coarse, so you lose precision; or too narrow, so extreme values fall off the end
and get clipped. Widening the grid fixes clipping and worsens resolution. Almost all of quantization
engineering is choosing where to sit on that trade-off, and doing it separately for groups of numbers
that have different scales.
</Intuition>

## The affine quantizer

<Definition id="affine-quantizer" title="Affine quantizer">
For a target integer range $\lbrace 0,1,\dots,2^n-1 \rbrace$ and a clipping interval
$[\alpha,\beta]$, define the **scale** and **zero point**
$$
\Delta = \frac{\beta-\alpha}{2^n-1},
\qquad
z = -\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{\Delta}\right),
$$
and the quantize / dequantize pair
$$
Q(x) = \operatorname{clamp}\left(\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right) + z,\ 0,\ 2^n-1\right),
\qquad
\widehat{x} = \Delta\,\big(Q(x) - z\big).
$$
The quantizer is **symmetric** when $z=0$, which requires $\alpha = -\beta$.
</Definition>

<Claim kind="proposition" id="affine-roundtrip" title="Affine quantization round-trip error">
If $x \in [\alpha,\beta]$, so that no clamping occurs, then
$$
\big|\widehat{x} - x\big| \ \le\ \frac{\Delta}{2}.
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Without clamping, $Q(x) = \operatorname{round}(x/\Delta) + z$, so
$\widehat x = \Delta \operatorname{round}(x/\Delta)$. Rounding to the nearest integer moves its
argument by at most $\tfrac12$:
$$
\left|\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right) - \frac{x}{\Delta}\right| \le \frac12 .
$$
Multiplying by $\Delta &gt; 0$ gives the claim. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

This is a *deterministic* bound — it holds for every input, with no probabilistic assumption. Keep it
separate in your mind from what follows.

## Error as a random variable

<Claim kind="proposition" id="uniform-cell" title="Exact error law on a single cell">
Let $X \sim \mathrm{Unif}\big([(k-\tfrac12)\Delta,\ (k+\tfrac12)\Delta]\big)$ for an integer $k$, so
that $X$ lies in the cell rounding to $k\Delta$. Then the error $E = \widehat X - X$ satisfies
$$
E \sim \mathrm{Unif}\left[-\frac{\Delta}{2}, \frac{\Delta}{2}\right],
\qquad \E[E] = 0, \qquad \Var(E) = \frac{\Delta^2}{12}.
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
On this cell $\widehat X = k\Delta$ identically, so $E = k\Delta - X$ is an affine, orientation-
reversing image of a uniform variable and is therefore uniform on
$[-\Delta/2, \Delta/2]$. For a uniform variable on $[-a,a]$ the mean is $0$ by symmetry and
$$
\Var = \E[E^2] = \frac{1}{2a}\int_{-a}^{a} u^2\,du = \frac{a^2}{3},
$$
which at $a = \Delta/2$ gives $\Delta^2/12$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Claim kind="heuristic" id="additive-noise-model" title="The additive white-noise model">
For a general input distribution, one models quantization as
$$
\widehat X = X + E, \qquad \E[E]=0,\quad \Var(E) = \frac{\Delta^2}{12},\quad E \perp X,
$$
with $E$ uncorrelated across coordinates.
</Claim>

<Warning>
This is **not** a theorem, and the distinction matters. The error is a deterministic function of the
input, $E = \Delta\operatorname{round}(X/\Delta) - X$, so it cannot be independent of $X$ — for
instance, $X$ already on the grid forces $E = 0$. The model is accurate in the *high-resolution*
regime, where $\Delta$ is small relative to the scale over which the density of $X$ varies, so that
the density is nearly constant across each cell and the previous proposition applies cell by cell.
There is an exact version (Widrow's quantizing theorem) requiring the characteristic function of $X$
to vanish outside $(-\pi/\Delta, \pi/\Delta)$, a condition no real distribution satisfies.

At the bit widths that matter for LLMs — 4 bits, sometimes 3 — the high-resolution regime is
plainly not in force: there are 16 levels covering the whole range of a weight group. Predictions
derived from this model should be treated as orientation, not as bounds. The deterministic
$|E| \le \Delta/2$ remains valid throughout.
</Warning>

## Signal-to-quantization-noise ratio

<Claim kind="proposition" id="six-db-rule" title="Six decibels per bit">
Assume the noise model, a symmetric $b$-bit quantizer with clipping range $[-A, A]$ so that
$\Delta = 2A/2^{b}$, and a signal with variance $\sigma^2$ and negligible clipping. Then
$$
\mathrm{SQNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sigma^2}{\Var(E)} = 6.02\,b + 4.77 - 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{A}{\sigma}\right).
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Substituting $\Delta = 2A/2^b$ into $\Var(E) = \Delta^2/12$ gives
$\Var(E) = 4A^2/(12\cdot 2^{2b}) = A^2/(3\cdot 2^{2b})$. Hence
$$
\frac{\sigma^2}{\Var(E)} = \frac{3 \cdot 2^{2b}\,\sigma^2}{A^2},
$$
and taking $10\log_{10}$,
$$
\mathrm{SQNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10\log_{10} 3 + 20 b \log_{10} 2 - 20\log_{10}\frac{A}{\sigma}
= 4.77 + 6.02\,b - 20\log_{10}\frac{A}{\sigma}. \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Two readings, both useful:

1. **Each bit buys about 6 dB**, a factor of 4 in noise power. Going from 8-bit to 4-bit costs 24 dB
   — a 250× increase in quantization noise power, which is why 4-bit needs machinery that 8-bit does
   not.
2. **The loading factor $A/\sigma$ enters as a penalty.** If one outlier forces $A$ to be $50\sigma$,
   the term $20\log_{10}50 \approx 34$ dB eats nearly six bits of precision. The grid is being spent
   on a range almost no value occupies.
</KeyIdea>

That second reading is the central practical fact about quantizing transformers, and the reason
per-tensor scaling is inadequate for activations.

## Clipping versus resolution

<Claim kind="proposition" id="clip-tradeoff" title="Clipping and resolution trade off">
Let $X$ have density $f$, symmetric about $0$, and quantize with $b$ bits over $[-A,A]$. Under the
noise model for the unclipped part, the mean squared error decomposes as
$$
\mathrm{MSE}(A) = \underbrace{\frac{A^2}{3\cdot 2^{2b}}\,\P(|X| \le A)}_{\text{rounding}}
\;+\; \underbrace{2\int_A^\infty (x-A)^2 f(x)\,dx}_{\text{clipping}} .
$$
The first term is increasing in $A$ and the second decreasing, so if $X$ is unbounded the minimiser
is attained at some finite $A^\star &gt; 0$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Condition on whether $|X| \le A$. Inside the range the quantizer contributes rounding error of mean
square $\Delta^2/12 = A^2/(3\cdot2^{2b})$ under the model, weighted by $\P(|X|\le A)$. Outside, the
value is clamped to $\pm A$, so the error is exactly $|X| - A$ in magnitude; by symmetry the two
tails contribute equally, giving the stated integral.

Monotonicity: the rounding term is $A^2$ times a factor increasing in $A$ (the probability), hence
increasing. Differentiating the clipping term in $A$ gives
$-4\int_A^\infty (x-A) f(x)\,dx \le 0$, so it is non-increasing.

Existence of an interior minimiser: as $A \to 0$ the clipping term tends to $2\E[X^2] &gt; 0$ while
rounding tends to $0$; as $A\to\infty$ rounding grows like $A^2/(3\cdot2^{2b}) \to \infty$ while
clipping tends to $0$. Since $\mathrm{MSE}$ is continuous and tends to a positive limit at $0$ and to
$\infty$, its infimum over $(0,\infty)$ is attained. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Details summary="Consequences for how scales are chosen in practice">
The proposition says the naive choice $A = \max_i |x_i|$ is generally **not** optimal: it drives the
clipping term to zero at the cost of an inflated rounding term. Standard responses:

- **Percentile clipping.** Set $A$ at, say, the 99.9th percentile of $|X|$ and accept clipping on the
  rest. Directly targets the trade-off above.
- **MSE-optimal search.** Grid-search $A$ to minimise the measured reconstruction error on a
  calibration set — the empirical version of minimising $\mathrm{MSE}(A)$.
- **Finer granularity.** Use one scale per channel, or per group of 64 or 128 weights, instead of one
  per tensor. Each group then has its own $A$, so a single large value inflates the loading factor
  only for its own group rather than for the whole tensor. This is usually the highest-leverage
  change, and it is why group-wise 4-bit formats work at all.

Granularity costs memory: one FP16 scale per 128 weights adds $16/128 = 0.125$ bits per weight, so a
nominally 4-bit format is really 4.125 bits. Quote that honestly when comparing formats.
</Details>

## The formats in use

<Note>
- **INT8** — 8-bit integers with a scale, usually symmetric per channel for weights. Well supported
  in hardware; adequate for weights almost everywhere, and the difficult case is activations.
- **FP8** — two variants, E4M3 (4 exponent, 3 mantissa bits) and E5M2 (5 and 2). Trades mantissa
  precision for exponent range, so it handles wide dynamic range better than INT8 at the same bit
  count, at coarser resolution near zero.
- **NF4** — a 4-bit format whose 16 levels are placed at quantiles of a standard normal rather than
  uniformly, on the argument that neural network weights are approximately Gaussian. Non-uniform
  quantizers can beat uniform ones when the density is known, which is the Lloyd–Max result; NF4 is
  that idea specialised to an assumed Gaussian.
</Note>

<OpenProblem>
There is no satisfactory theory predicting *end-task* degradation from quantization error. The chain
from weight-level MSE, through layer output perturbation, to next-token distribution shift, to
downstream accuracy is not analytically controlled for networks of this depth: layer-wise error
bounds compose multiplicatively through Lipschitz constants that are far too loose to be informative.
Quantization methods are selected by measurement, and claims that a format "preserves quality" are
empirical claims about particular benchmarks, not theorems.
</OpenProblem>
