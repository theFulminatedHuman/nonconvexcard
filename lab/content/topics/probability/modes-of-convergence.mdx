## Four notions of convergence

<Definition id="modes" title="The four modes">
Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ and $X$ be random variables on a common probability space.

- $X_n \to X$ **almost surely** ($X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X$) if $\P\big(\lim_n X_n = X\big) = 1$.
- $X_n \to X$ **in probability** ($X_n \xrightarrow{\P} X$) if $\P(|X_n - X| > \eps) \to 0$ for every $\eps>0$.
- $X_n \to X$ **in $L^p$** ($X_n \xrightarrow{L^p} X$) if $\E|X_n - X|^p \to 0$.
- $X_n \to X$ **in distribution** ($X_n \rightsquigarrow X$) if $F_{X_n}(t) \to F_X(t)$ at every $t$ where $F_X$ is continuous.
</Definition>

Only the last is a statement about laws alone; the other three compare the random variables
themselves and require them to live on the same space.

<Theorem id="implications" title="Implications between modes">
$$
X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X \;\Longrightarrow\; X_n \xrightarrow{\P} X, \qquad
X_n \xrightarrow{L^p} X \;\Longrightarrow\; X_n \xrightarrow{\P} X, \qquad
X_n \xrightarrow{\P} X \;\Longrightarrow\; X_n \rightsquigarrow X .
$$
None of the reverse implications holds in general, and almost-sure and $L^p$ convergence do not imply
each other.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
**Almost sure $\Rightarrow$ in probability.** Fix $\eps>0$ and set $A_n = \{|X_n - X| > \eps\}$. On the
event $\{X_n \to X\}$, which has probability one, only finitely many $A_n$ occur, so
$\one_{A_n} \to 0$ almost surely. Since $\one_{A_n} \le 1$, dominated convergence gives
$\P(A_n) = \E \one_{A_n} \to 0$.

**$L^p$ $\Rightarrow$ in probability.** Markov's inequality applied to $|X_n - X|^p$:
$$
\P(|X_n - X| > \eps) = \P\big(|X_n-X|^p > \eps^p\big) \le \frac{\E|X_n-X|^p}{\eps^p} \to 0 .
$$

**In probability $\Rightarrow$ in distribution.** Let $t$ be a continuity point of $F_X$ and
$\eps > 0$. Then
$$
F_{X_n}(t) = \P(X_n \le t) \le \P(X \le t+\eps) + \P(|X_n - X| > \eps) = F_X(t+\eps) + o(1),
$$
and symmetrically $F_{X_n}(t) \ge F_X(t-\eps) - o(1)$. Letting $n \to \infty$ and then $\eps \downarrow 0$,
continuity of $F_X$ at $t$ squeezes $F_{X_n}(t) \to F_X(t)$.

**Counterexamples.** Take $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$.

- *In probability but not almost surely.* Partition $[0,1]$ into consecutive blocks and let $X_n = \one\{U \in I_n\}$ where $|I_n| = 1/n$ and the $I_n$ cycle through $[0,1]$. Then $\P(X_n \ne 0) = 1/n \to 0$, but $\sum_n |I_n| = \infty$ and the blocks cover $[0,1]$ infinitely often, so $X_n = 1$ infinitely often almost surely.
- *In probability but not in $L^1$.* $X_n = n\,\one\{U < 1/n\}$ satisfies $X_n \xrightarrow{\P}0$ and even $X_n \to 0$ almost surely, yet $\E X_n = 1$ for all $n$.
- *Almost surely but not in $L^1$.* The same example.
- *In $L^p$ but not almost surely.* The first example with $X_n = \one\{U \in I_n\}$ has $\E|X_n|^p = 1/n \to 0$.
- *In distribution but not in probability.* Let $X_n = (-1)^n Z$ for a standard normal $Z$. Every $X_n$ has the same law, so $X_n \rightsquigarrow Z$, but $|X_n - X_{n+1}| = 2|Z|$ does not go to zero.
</Proof>

<Note title="A useful partial converse">
Convergence in probability plus a summable rate gives almost-sure convergence: if
$\sum_n \P(|X_n - X| > \eps) < \infty$ for every $\eps > 0$, then
<TopicRef id="probability/probability-spaces">Borel–Cantelli</TopicRef> gives
$X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.}X$. This is precisely how exponential concentration bounds upgrade
convergence-in-probability statements throughout this laboratory.
</Note>

## The law of large numbers

<Theorem id="wlln" title="Weak law of large numbers">
Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be uncorrelated with common mean $\mu$ and variance at most $\sigma^2 < \infty$,
and let $\bar X_n = \frac1n\sum_{i\le n}X_i$. Then $\bar X_n \xrightarrow{\P} \mu$; quantitatively,
$$
\P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge t\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{nt^2}\quad\text{for every } t>0 .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
$\E \bar X_n = \mu$ by linearity. Because the $X_i$ are uncorrelated, the covariances vanish and
$$
\Var(\bar X_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \Var(X_i) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
$$
Chebyshev's inequality — that is, Markov applied to $(\bar X_n - \mu)^2$ — gives
$\P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge t) \le \Var(\bar X_n)/t^2 \le \sigma^2/(nt^2)$, which tends to $0$.
</Proof>

<Assumptions>
Note exactly what was used: *uncorrelated* (not independent), finite variance, common mean. The
conclusion is a $1/n$ decay of the failure probability, which is polynomial — much weaker than the
exponential decay available under stronger assumptions on the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-inequalities">concentration</TopicRef> page.
</Assumptions>

<Theorem id="slln" title="Strong law of large numbers">
If $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. with $\E|X_1| < \infty$ and $\E X_1 = \mu$, then
$\bar X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mu$. Conversely, if $\E|X_1| = \infty$ then
$\limsup_n |\bar X_n| = \infty$ almost surely.
</Theorem>

Kolmogorov's strong law is quoted rather than proved: the standard proofs (via truncation and
Kolmogorov's maximal inequality, or via the ergodic theorem) are longer than this page warrants, and
the references give them properly. What is worth stressing is the hypothesis gap: the strong law needs
only a **first** moment, while the elementary weak law above needed a second.

## The central limit theorem

<Theorem id="clt" title="Central limit theorem (Lindeberg–Lévy)">
Let $X_1, X_2,\dots$ be i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 \in (0,\infty)$. Then
$$
\frac{\sqrt{n}\,(\bar X_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \rightsquigarrow \Ncal(0,1) .
$$
</Theorem>

The characteristic-function proof is standard and is not reproduced here. The structure worth
remembering is: $\varphi_{\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu)/\sigma}(t) = \big(\varphi_{(X_1-\mu)/\sigma}(t/\sqrt n)\big)^n$,
expand the inner characteristic function to second order, and recognise
$(1 - t^2/2n + o(1/n))^n \to e^{-t^2/2}$.

<Theorem id="berry-esseen" title="Berry–Esseen bound">
Under the hypotheses above with additionally $\rho = \E|X_1 - \mu|^3 < \infty$, there is an absolute
constant $C$ (one may take $C \le 0.5$) with
$$
\sup_{t\in\R}\left|\P\left(\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \le t\right) - \Phi(t)\right|
\le \frac{C\rho}{\sigma^3\sqrt n} .
$$
</Theorem>

This is the quantitative form of the CLT, quoted from the literature. Read the bound carefully: it
controls the **uniform** error in the CDF by something of order $n^{-1/2}$. That is exactly the
problem.

<Claim kind="empirical" id="clt-not-tail" title="The CLT does not license Gaussian tail estimates">
It is tempting to argue: "$\bar X_n$ is approximately $\Ncal(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ by the CLT, therefore
$\P(|\bar X_n - \mu| > t) \approx 2\big(1 - \Phi(t\sqrt n/\sigma)\big) \le e^{-nt^2/2\sigma^2}$."
This step is not valid as stated, and the reason is quantitative rather than philosophical.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The CLT is a statement about **pointwise convergence of the CDF at fixed $t$**. Berry–Esseen says the
error in that approximation is of order $n^{-1/2}$ *uniformly*. So when the true tail probability is
itself smaller than $n^{-1/2}$ — which is the regime every generalisation bound and every
"with probability $1-\delta$" claim lives in — the CLT approximation carries an error larger than the
quantity being estimated. It tells you nothing.

Concretely: for $n = 100$ the Berry–Esseen error is on the order of $10^{-1}$, so the CLT cannot
distinguish a tail probability of $10^{-3}$ from one of $10^{-6}$. Yet those are exactly the
probabilities one needs for a $\delta = 0.001$ confidence statement.

This is why the entire machinery of the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-inequalities">concentration inequalities</TopicRef>
page exists. Hoeffding's inequality gives a *non-asymptotic, valid-for-every-$n$* Gaussian-type tail
bound under a boundedness hypothesis; the CLT gives an asymptotic shape with no error control in the
tail. They are not substitutes.
</NotSaid>
