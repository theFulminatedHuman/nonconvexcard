## Expectation

<Definition id="expectation" title="Expectation">
For a random variable $X \ge 0$, $\E X = \int_\Omega X \, d\P \in [0,\infty]$, defined as the supremum
of the integrals of simple functions below $X$. For general $X$, write $X = X^+ - X^-$ and set
$\E X = \E X^+ - \E X^-$ whenever at least one term is finite; $X$ is **integrable** when
$\E|X| < \infty$.
</Definition>

Expectation is linear, monotone, and satisfies $|\E X| \le \E|X|$. The following identity is the one
that turns tail bounds into moment bounds and back, and it is used constantly on the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/sub-gaussian-random-variables">sub-Gaussian</TopicRef> page.

<Proposition id="tail-formula" title="Tail formula for the expectation">
For $X \ge 0$,
$$
\E X = \int_0^\infty \P(X > t)\,dt, \qquad\text{and more generally}\qquad
\E[X^p] = p\int_0^\infty t^{p-1}\,\P(X>t)\,dt \quad (p > 0).
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Write $X = \int_0^\infty \one\{X > t\}\,dt$ pointwise — for each $\omega$ the integrand is $1$ for
$t < X(\omega)$ and $0$ after, so the integral is $X(\omega)$. Both $X$ and the integrand are
non-negative and jointly measurable, so Tonelli's theorem allows the order of integration to be
exchanged:
$$
\E X = \E\int_0^\infty \one\{X>t\}\,dt = \int_0^\infty \E\,\one\{X>t\}\,dt = \int_0^\infty \P(X>t)\,dt .
$$
For the $p$-th moment, apply the same identity to $X^p$ and substitute $t \mapsto t^p$:
$\P(X^p > s) = \P(X > s^{1/p})$, and $ds = p t^{p-1}dt$ under $s = t^p$.
</Proof>

<Note title="The layer-cake picture">
The identity says: the area under the graph of $X$ can be swept horizontally instead of vertically.
That is all "layer cake" means, and it is why a bound of the form $\P(X > t) \le Ce^{-t^2/K^2}$
immediately yields bounds on every moment of $X$ — integrate the Gaussian tail.
</Note>

## Variance, covariance and the decompositions that matter

<Definition id="variance" title="Variance and covariance">
For square-integrable $X, Y$,
$$
\Var(X) = \E\big[(X - \E X)^2\big] = \E[X^2] - (\E X)^2, \qquad
\Cov(X,Y) = \E\big[(X-\E X)(Y - \E Y)\big] .
$$
For a random vector $X \in \R^d$ the **covariance matrix** is
$\Sigma = \E[(X - \E X)(X-\E X)^\top] \in \mathbb{S}^d$, and $\Sigma \succeq 0$ because
$v^\top \Sigma v = \Var(\ip{v}{X}) \ge 0$.
</Definition>

<Proposition id="variance-decomposition" title="Bias–variance and variance decompositions">
Let $\hat\theta$ be a square-integrable estimator of a constant $\theta$, and let $\mathcal{G}$ be a
sub-$\sigma$-algebra. Then
$$
\underbrace{\E\big[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2\big]}_{\text{mean squared error}}
= \underbrace{\big(\E\hat\theta - \theta\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\Var(\hat\theta)}_{\text{variance}},
$$
and
$$
\Var(X) = \E\big[\Var(X \mid \mathcal{G})\big] + \Var\big(\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\big)
$$
(the **law of total variance**).
</Proposition>

<Proof>
For the first identity, insert $\E\hat\theta$ and expand:
$$
\E(\hat\theta - \theta)^2 = \E\big[(\hat\theta - \E\hat\theta) + (\E\hat\theta - \theta)\big]^2
= \Var(\hat\theta) + 2(\E\hat\theta - \theta)\E[\hat\theta - \E\hat\theta] + (\E\hat\theta-\theta)^2 ,
$$
and the cross term vanishes because $\E[\hat\theta - \E\hat\theta] = 0$. Note where the hypotheses go:
$\theta$ must be non-random, and $\hat\theta$ square-integrable.

For the second, let $m = \E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$. Then
$\Var(X\mid\mathcal{G}) = \E[X^2\mid\mathcal{G}] - m^2$, so taking expectations and using the tower
property (below),
$$
\E\big[\Var(X\mid\mathcal{G})\big] = \E[X^2] - \E[m^2],
\qquad
\Var(m) = \E[m^2] - (\E m)^2 = \E[m^2] - (\E X)^2 .
$$
Adding gives $\E[X^2] - (\E X)^2 = \Var(X)$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The bias–variance identity is *exact algebra*, not an approximation or a heuristic trade-off. What is
heuristic is the claim that reducing one must increase the other; that claim is a description of
certain model families, and the
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/bias-variance-and-double-descent">double descent</TopicRef> page shows
regimes where it plainly fails. Keep the identity and the folklore separate.
</KeyIdea>

The vector version of the variance-of-a-sum computation is used in every mini-batch argument:
$$
\Var\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j}\Cov(X_i,X_j)
\;\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{=}\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n} .
$$
The middle expression is the honest one. The $\sigma^2/n$ rate is a *consequence of independence*,
and correlated samples — adjacent tokens in a text corpus, say — do not enjoy it.

## Conditional expectation

<Definition id="cond-exp-def" title="Conditional expectation">
Let $X$ be integrable and $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ a sub-$\sigma$-algebra. A random
variable $Z$ is **a conditional expectation of $X$ given $\mathcal{G}$**, written
$Z = \E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$, if

1. $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, and
2. $\E[Z\one_G] = \E[X\one_G]$ for every $G \in \mathcal{G}$.

Such a $Z$ exists and is unique up to almost-sure equality.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="cond-exp-existence" title="Conditional expectation as an L2 projection">
If $\E[X^2] < \infty$, then $\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto the
closed subspace $L^2(\mathcal{G}) \subseteq L^2(\mathcal{A})$ of square-integrable
$\mathcal{G}$-measurable variables. Consequently
$$
\E[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \argmin_{Z \in L^2(\mathcal{G})} \E\big[(X - Z)^2\big] .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
$L^2(\mathcal{A})$ with $\ip{U}{V} = \E[UV]$ is a Hilbert space and $L^2(\mathcal{G})$ is a closed
subspace, so the projection $Z = \Pi_{L^2(\mathcal{G})}X$ exists and is characterised by
$X - Z \perp L^2(\mathcal{G})$, i.e. $\E[(X-Z)W] = 0$ for all $W \in L^2(\mathcal{G})$.

Taking $W = \one_G$ for $G \in \mathcal{G}$ recovers property (2) of the definition, and $Z$ is
$\mathcal{G}$-measurable by construction, so $Z$ is a conditional expectation. The minimisation
statement is the "nearest point" part of the
<TopicRef id="foundations/linear-algebra">projection theorem</TopicRef>.

Uniqueness: if $Z, Z'$ both satisfy (1) and (2) then $\E[(Z-Z')\one_G] = 0$ for all
$G \in \mathcal{G}$; taking $G = \{Z - Z' > 0\}$ and $G = \{Z-Z'<0\}$, both of which are in
$\mathcal{G}$, forces $\P(Z \neq Z') = 0$.
</Proof>

<Intuition>
"Conditional expectation is the best guess at $X$ given the information in $\mathcal{G}$" is exactly
right, and the theorem says in what sense: best in mean square, among all guesses that only use that
information. Every regression objective in this laboratory is an attempt to approximate this
projection from a finite sample, which is why the Bayes-optimal predictor for squared loss is
$\E[Y \mid X]$ and nothing else.
</Intuition>

<Proposition id="tower" title="Tower property and pulling out what is known">
Let $X, Y$ be integrable and $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$ sub-$\sigma$-algebras.

1. **Tower / smoothing.** $\E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\ \big|\ \mathcal{H}\big] = \E[X\mid\mathcal{H}]$; in particular $\E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\big] = \E X$.
2. **Taking out what is known.** If $Y$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable and $XY$ is integrable, then $\E[XY\mid\mathcal{G}] = Y\,\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$.
3. **Independence.** If $X$ is independent of $\mathcal{G}$ then $\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}] = \E X$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
(1) Write $Z = \E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ and $W = \E[Z\mid\mathcal{H}]$. For $H \in \mathcal{H}$ we have
$H \in \mathcal{G}$ too, so $\E[W\one_H] = \E[Z\one_H] = \E[X\one_H]$, and $W$ is
$\mathcal{H}$-measurable; that is the defining property of $\E[X\mid\mathcal{H}]$. Uniqueness finishes
the argument. Taking $\mathcal{H}$ trivial gives the second claim.

(2) First take $Y = \one_{G_0}$ for $G_0 \in \mathcal{G}$. For any $G \in \mathcal{G}$,
$$
\E\big[\one_{G_0}\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\one_G\big] = \E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\one_{G\cap G_0}\big]
= \E[X\one_{G\cap G_0}] = \E[(X\one_{G_0})\one_G] ,
$$
which identifies $\one_{G_0}\E[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ as $\E[X\one_{G_0}\mid\mathcal{G}]$. Extend to
simple $Y$ by linearity and to general integrable $Y$ by monotone convergence.

(3) $\E X$ is (trivially) $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, and for $G \in \mathcal{G}$ independence gives
$\E[X\one_G] = \E X\,\P(G) = \E[\E X \cdot \one_G]$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Common mistake">
$\E[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is a **random variable**, not a number, and the conditional variance
$\Var(X\mid\mathcal{G})$ likewise. Writing $\E[X \mid Y = y]$ for a number is fine once $y$ is fixed,
but the tower property is a statement about the random object, and treating it as a constant is how
the law of total variance gets mangled.
</Warning>
