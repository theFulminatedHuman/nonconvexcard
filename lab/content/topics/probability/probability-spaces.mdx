<Intuition>
Almost nothing in machine learning needs measure theory — until it does. The moment you write
"with probability at least $1-\delta$ over the draw of the training set, for all $f$ in the class",
you are quantifying over an uncountable family of events, and whether that statement even parses
depends on the setup below. This page exists so that later pages can be precise cheaply.
</Intuition>

## The three axioms

<Definition id="prob-space" title="Probability space">
A **probability space** is a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, \P)$ where

- $\Omega$ is a set of outcomes;
- $\mathcal{A}$ is a **$\sigma$-algebra** on $\Omega$: a collection of subsets containing $\Omega$ and closed under complement and countable union;
- $\P : \mathcal{A} \to [0,1]$ is a **probability measure**: $\P(\Omega) = 1$ and, for pairwise disjoint $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$, $\P\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) = \sum_i \P(A_i)$ (countable additivity).

Members of $\mathcal{A}$ are **events**.
</Definition>

The $\sigma$-algebra is the part that is easy to dismiss and occasionally essential. It is the
collection of sets to which a probability *can* be assigned; on $\Omega = \R$ the standard choice is
the **Borel** $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{B}(\R)$, the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing all open
intervals.

<Proposition id="monotonicity" title="Elementary consequences of the axioms">
Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\P)$ be a probability space and $A, B, A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$. Then

1. $\P(A^c) = 1 - \P(A)$ and $\P(\emptyset) = 0$;
2. if $A \subseteq B$ then $\P(A) \le \P(B)$ (monotonicity);
3. $\P(A \cup B) = \P(A) + \P(B) - \P(A\cap B)$;
4. if $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots$ then $\P(A_n) \uparrow \P\left(\bigcup_n A_n\right)$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
(1) $\Omega = A \cup A^c$ disjointly, so $1 = \P(A) + \P(A^c)$. Taking $A = \Omega$ gives
$\P(\emptyset)=0$.

(2) $B = A \cup (B \setminus A)$ disjointly, so $\P(B) = \P(A) + \P(B\setminus A) \ge \P(A)$ since
probabilities are non-negative.

(3) Decompose $A \cup B = (A\setminus B) \cup (A \cap B) \cup (B \setminus A)$ disjointly and add;
then note $\P(A) = \P(A\setminus B) + \P(A\cap B)$ and similarly for $B$.

(4) Set $D_1 = A_1$ and $D_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}$ for $n \ge 2$. The $D_n$ are disjoint with
$\bigcup_{k\le n} D_k = A_n$ and $\bigcup_k D_k = \bigcup_n A_n$. Countable additivity gives
$\P\left(\bigcup_n A_n\right) = \sum_k \P(D_k) = \lim_n \sum_{k \le n}\P(D_k) = \lim_n \P(A_n)$, and the
partial sums increase.
</Proof>

<Lemma id="union-bound" title="Union bound (Boole's inequality)">
For any events $A_1, A_2, \dots$,
$$
\P\left(\bigcup_{i} A_i\right) \le \sum_i \P(A_i) .
$$
</Lemma>

<Proof>
Let $B_1 = A_1$ and $B_i = A_i \setminus \bigcup_{j<i}A_j$. The $B_i$ are disjoint with the same
union, and $B_i \subseteq A_i$, so by countable additivity and monotonicity
$\P(\bigcup_i A_i) = \sum_i \P(B_i) \le \sum_i \P(A_i)$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The union bound is three lines to prove and it is the workhorse of this entire laboratory. It is how
a bound for one random variable becomes a bound for the maximum of many; it is the source of the
$\log N$ in <TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/johnson-lindenstrauss">Johnson–Lindenstrauss</TopicRef>,
of the $\log|\Fcal|$ in finite-class generalisation bounds, and of the covering-number factors in
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/nets-covering-and-packing">net arguments</TopicRef>. It is
also lossy: it ignores all dependence, and much of high-dimensional probability is about recovering
what it throws away.
</KeyIdea>

## Random variables and distributions

<Definition id="rv-def" title="Random variable">
A **random variable** is a measurable map $X : \Omega \to \R$, meaning
$X^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{A}$ for every $B \in \mathcal{B}(\R)$. We write
$\{X \in B\}$ for $X^{-1}(B)$ and $\P(X \in B)$ for $\P(X^{-1}(B))$. A **random vector** is a
measurable map into $\R^d$, equivalently a $d$-tuple of random variables.
</Definition>

<Proposition id="pushforward" title="The distribution of a random variable">
The map $\mu_X(B) := \P(X \in B)$ defines a probability measure on $(\R, \mathcal{B}(\R))$, the
**distribution** or **law** of $X$. It is determined by the **cumulative distribution function**
$F_X(t) = \P(X \le t)$, which is non-decreasing, right-continuous, with limits $0$ at $-\infty$ and
$1$ at $+\infty$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
$\mu_X(\R) = \P(\Omega) = 1$. For disjoint $B_1, B_2, \dots$, the preimages $X^{-1}(B_i)$ are disjoint
events, so countable additivity of $\P$ transfers to $\mu_X$. Right-continuity of $F_X$ follows from
part (4) of the previous proposition applied to the decreasing sets $\{X \le t + 1/n\}$; the limits at
$\pm\infty$ follow from continuity along monotone sequences likewise. That $F_X$ determines $\mu_X$ is
the uniqueness statement for measures agreeing on the generating class of half-lines
$\{(-\infty,t]\}$, which is a standard $\pi$–$\lambda$ argument and is not repeated here.
</Proof>

<Note title="Two random variables, same law">
Nothing in a distribution remembers the underlying $\Omega$. $X$ and $-X$ for a symmetric $X$ have
the same law but are different functions on $\Omega$; independence, conditioning and coupling are all
statements about the functions, not the laws. This is why "identically distributed" and
"independent" are separate hypotheses.
</Note>

## Independence

<Definition id="independence" title="Independence">
Events $A_1,\dots,A_n$ are **independent** if for every subset $S \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$
$$
\P\left(\bigcap_{i\in S} A_i\right) = \prod_{i \in S}\P(A_i) .
$$
Random variables $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are independent if the events $\{X_i \in B_i\}$ are independent for
all Borel $B_i$, equivalently if the joint law is the product of the marginals.
</Definition>

<Warning title="Common mistake">
Pairwise independence is strictly weaker than independence. Let $X_1, X_2$ be independent Rademacher
signs and $X_3 = X_1X_2$. Any two of the three are independent, but
$\P(X_1 = X_2 = X_3 = 1) = 1/4 \neq 1/8$. The distinction is not pedantic: Chebyshev-type bounds need
only pairwise independence, while the Chernoff method on the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-inequalities">concentration</TopicRef> page
needs full independence, because it factorises a moment generating function over *all* the variables
at once.
</Warning>

## Almost sure events and Borel–Cantelli

<Definition id="as-def" title="Almost surely">
An event $A$ holds **almost surely** (a.s.) if $\P(A) = 1$. A statement holds a.s. if the set of
outcomes where it holds is an event of probability one.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="borel-cantelli" title="Borel–Cantelli lemmas">
Let $A_1, A_2, \dots$ be events and let
$\{A_n \text{ i.o.}\} = \bigcap_{n\ge1}\bigcup_{k \ge n} A_k$ be the event that infinitely many occur.

1. If $\sum_n \P(A_n) < \infty$ then $\P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 0$.
2. If the $A_n$ are independent and $\sum_n \P(A_n) = \infty$ then $\P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 1$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
(1) For every $n$, monotonicity and the union bound give
$$
\P(A_k \text{ i.o.}) \le \P\left(\bigcup_{k\ge n}A_k\right) \le \sum_{k \ge n}\P(A_k) .
$$
The right side is the tail of a convergent series, so it tends to $0$ as $n \to \infty$, while the
left side does not depend on $n$. Hence it is $0$.

(2) Fix $n \le m$. By independence and $1 - p \le e^{-p}$,
$$
\P\left(\bigcap_{k=n}^{m} A_k^c\right) = \prod_{k=n}^m \big(1 - \P(A_k)\big)
\le \exp\left(-\sum_{k=n}^m \P(A_k)\right) \xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{} 0 ,
$$
because the series diverges. So $\P\left(\bigcup_{k \ge n}A_k\right) = 1$ for every $n$, and an
intersection of countably many probability-one events has probability one.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Borel–Cantelli is the standard route from a *quantitative* tail bound to an *almost sure* statement.
If a concentration inequality gives $\P(|Z_n - z| > \eps_n) \le \delta_n$ with $\sum_n \delta_n < \infty$,
then $Z_n \to z$ almost surely. That is exactly how the strong law is upgraded from the weak law, and
how almost-sure convergence of SGD is obtained from its expected-error bounds on the
<TopicRef id="stochastic-optimization/sgd-convergence">SGD</TopicRef> page.
</KeyIdea>
