## The primal problem and its Lagrangian

Consider
$$
p^\star = \min_{x\in\R^d} f_0(x) \quad\text{subject to}\quad
f_i(x)\le0\ (i=1,\dots,m), \quad h_j(x)=0\ (j=1,\dots,r).
$$

<Definition id="lagrangian" title="Lagrangian and dual function">
The **Lagrangian** is
$$
\mathcal{L}(x,\alpha,\nu) = f_0(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i f_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^r \nu_j h_j(x),
$$
with $\alpha \in \R^m_{\ge0}$ and $\nu\in\R^r$. The **dual function** is
$$
g(\alpha,\nu) = \inf_{x}\mathcal{L}(x,\alpha,\nu),
$$
and the **dual problem** is $d^\star = \max_{\alpha\ge0,\nu} g(\alpha,\nu)$.
</Definition>

<Intuition>
The multipliers are prices. Instead of forbidding a constraint violation, charge $\alpha_i$ per unit
of violation of $f_i(x) \le 0$ and let $x$ range freely. If the prices are right, the unconstrained
buyer chooses to satisfy the constraints anyway. The dual problem is the search for the prices that
make that happen, and the duality gap measures whether prices can do the job at all.
</Intuition>

<Proposition id="dual-concave" title="The dual function is always concave">
$g$ is concave on $\R^m_{\ge0}\times\R^r$, regardless of any property of $f_0, f_i, h_j$. It may take
the value $-\infty$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
For fixed $x$, the map $(\alpha,\nu)\mapsto \mathcal{L}(x,\alpha,\nu)$ is affine. $g$ is the pointwise
infimum of this family over $x$, and a pointwise infimum of affine functions is concave: for
$(\alpha,\nu) = \theta(\alpha_1,\nu_1) + (1-\theta)(\alpha_2,\nu_2)$,
$$
g(\alpha,\nu) = \inf_x\Big[\theta\mathcal{L}(x,\alpha_1,\nu_1)+(1-\theta)\mathcal{L}(x,\alpha_2,\nu_2)\Big]
\ge \theta\inf_x\mathcal{L}(x,\alpha_1,\nu_1)+(1-\theta)\inf_x\mathcal{L}(x,\alpha_2,\nu_2),
$$
using that the infimum of a sum is at least the sum of the infima.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
This is remarkable and is the practical reason duality matters: **the dual of an arbitrarily nasty
problem is a concave maximisation**, hence a convex problem. Sometimes the dual is tractable when the
primal is not, and sometimes — as in the <TopicRef id="machine-learning/support-vector-machines">SVM</TopicRef> —
the dual is where the interesting structure (kernels, support vectors) becomes visible.
</KeyIdea>

<Theorem id="weak-duality" title="Weak duality">
$d^\star \le p^\star$, with no assumptions on $f_0,f_i,h_j$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Let $x$ be primal feasible and $(\alpha,\nu)$ dual feasible ($\alpha\ge0$). Then
$$
\mathcal{L}(x,\alpha,\nu) = f_0(x) + \underbrace{\sum_i\alpha_if_i(x)}_{\le0} + \underbrace{\sum_j\nu_jh_j(x)}_{=0} \le f_0(x),
$$
the first bracket because each $\alpha_i \ge 0$ and each $f_i(x)\le0$, and the second because
$h_j(x) = 0$. Taking the infimum over $x$ on the left,
$$
g(\alpha,\nu) = \inf_{x'}\mathcal{L}(x',\alpha,\nu) \le \mathcal{L}(x,\alpha,\nu) \le f_0(x).
$$
This holds for every feasible $x$ and every dual feasible $(\alpha,\nu)$, so
$\max_{\alpha,\nu}g \le \min_x f_0$.
</Proof>

<Theorem id="slater" title="Strong duality under Slater's condition">
Suppose $f_0,\dots,f_m$ are convex, the $h_j$ are affine, and there exists a **strictly feasible**
point: some $\tilde x$ in the relative interior of the domain with $f_i(\tilde x)<0$ for all $i$ with
$f_i$ not affine, and $h_j(\tilde x)=0$. Then $d^\star = p^\star$, and if $p^\star$ is finite the dual
optimum is attained.
</Theorem>

Quoted from Boyd–Vandenberghe, where it is proved via a separating-hyperplane argument applied to the
set $\{(u,v,s) : \exists x,\ f_i(x)\le u_i,\ h_j(x)=v_j,\ f_0(x)\le s\}$. Slater's condition is mild
and is satisfied by every problem in this laboratory that uses duality — in particular by the soft-margin
SVM, where any $\xi_i$ large enough is strictly feasible.

<Note title="When strong duality fails">
Convexity alone is not enough. The problem $\min e^{-x}$ subject to $x^2/y \le 0$ over $y > 0$ is
convex with $p^\star = 1$ and $d^\star = 0$: a genuine gap. Non-convex problems typically have gaps —
that is exactly what makes them hard — although some do not, and the ones that do not are often
solvable.
</Note>

## The KKT conditions

<Theorem id="kkt" title="Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions">
Suppose $f_0,f_i,h_j$ are differentiable and strong duality holds with both optima attained, at
$x^\star$ and $(\alpha^\star,\nu^\star)$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{(1) primal feasibility:} && f_i(x^\star)\le0,\quad h_j(x^\star)=0;\\
&\text{(2) dual feasibility:} && \alpha_i^\star\ge0;\\
&\text{(3) complementary slackness:} && \alpha_i^\star f_i(x^\star)=0 \ \ \forall i;\\
&\text{(4) stationarity:} && \nabla f_0(x^\star) + \sum_i\alpha_i^\star\nabla f_i(x^\star) + \sum_j\nu_j^\star\nabla h_j(x^\star)=0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Conversely, if the problem is convex and $(x,\alpha,\nu)$ satisfies (1)–(4), then $x$ is primal
optimal, $(\alpha,\nu)$ is dual optimal, and the duality gap is zero.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
**Necessity.** Strong duality and attainment give
$$
f_0(x^\star) = p^\star = d^\star = g(\alpha^\star,\nu^\star)
= \inf_x \mathcal{L}(x,\alpha^\star,\nu^\star)
\le \mathcal{L}(x^\star,\alpha^\star,\nu^\star)
= f_0(x^\star) + \sum_i\alpha_i^\star f_i(x^\star),
$$
the last equality using $h_j(x^\star)=0$. So $\sum_i\alpha_i^\star f_i(x^\star)\ge0$. But each term is
a product of a non-negative $\alpha_i^\star$ and a non-positive $f_i(x^\star)$, hence non-positive.
A sum of non-positive terms that is $\ge0$ forces every term to vanish: that is (3).

Consequently the chain of inequalities above is tight, so
$\mathcal{L}(x^\star,\alpha^\star,\nu^\star) = \inf_x\mathcal{L}(x,\alpha^\star,\nu^\star)$: the point
$x^\star$ minimises the Lagrangian over all $x$. Since $\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\alpha^\star,\nu^\star)$ is
differentiable, its gradient vanishes at an interior minimiser, which is (4). Conditions (1) and (2)
are feasibility of the two optima.

**Sufficiency in the convex case.** Assume (1)–(4) with $f_0,f_i$ convex and $h_j$ affine. Then
$\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\alpha,\nu)$ is convex (a non-negative combination of convex functions plus an
affine one), so the stationarity condition (4) implies $x$ is a global minimiser of the Lagrangian by
the <TopicRef id="optimization/convex-sets-and-functions">stationary-point proposition</TopicRef>.
Hence
$$
g(\alpha,\nu) = \mathcal{L}(x,\alpha,\nu) = f_0(x) + \sum_i\alpha_if_i(x) + \sum_j\nu_jh_j(x) = f_0(x),
$$
where the last step uses (3) and (1). So the dual objective at $(\alpha,\nu)$ equals the primal
objective at $x$; by weak duality $g(\alpha,\nu)\le p^\star \le f_0(x)$, and both ends are equal, so
both are optimal.
</Proof>

<Corollary id="complementary-slackness" title="Complementary slackness">
At an optimum, for each $i$,
$$
\alpha_i^\star>0 \implies f_i(x^\star)=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
f_i(x^\star)<0 \implies \alpha_i^\star=0 .
$$
</Corollary>

<Proof>
Both are contrapositives of $\alpha_i^\star f_i(x^\star)=0$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Complementary slackness is the mathematical origin of **sparsity in the dual**. In the
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/support-vector-machines">SVM</TopicRef> the constraints are
$y_i(w^\top x_i + b)\ge1$, one per training point. Complementary slackness says $\alpha_i > 0$ only
for points with $y_i(w^\top x_i+b)=1$ — the points exactly on the margin. Every other point has
$\alpha_i = 0$ and drops out of the solution entirely. "Support vector" is not a heuristic notion; it
is the name for a constraint with a non-zero multiplier.
</KeyIdea>

## Worked example: the dual of a quadratic program

<Derivation title="Dual of min ½xᵀQx + cᵀx subject to Ax ≤ b, with Q ≻ 0">
<Step why="Form the Lagrangian with multiplier α ≥ 0 for the inequality constraints.">
$$
\mathcal{L}(x,\alpha) = \tfrac12x^\top Qx + c^\top x + \alpha^\top(Ax - b).
$$
</Step>
<Step why="Q ≻ 0 makes the Lagrangian strictly convex in x, so the inner minimisation is solved by setting the gradient to zero.">
$$
\nabla_x\mathcal{L} = Qx + c + A^\top\alpha = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x^\star(\alpha) = -Q^{-1}(c + A^\top\alpha).
$$
</Step>
<Step why="Substitute back. The quadratic evaluates to minus half the squared Q-inverse norm of the combined linear term.">
$$
g(\alpha) = -\tfrac12(c+A^\top\alpha)^\top Q^{-1}(c+A^\top\alpha) - b^\top\alpha .
$$
</Step>
<Step why="The dual problem is a maximisation of a concave quadratic over the non-negative orthant — itself a quadratic program, but with the inequality constraints replaced by simple sign constraints.">
$$
d^\star = \max_{\alpha\ge0}\ \left\{-\tfrac12\alpha^\top AQ^{-1}A^\top\alpha - \alpha^\top(AQ^{-1}c + b) - \tfrac12 c^\top Q^{-1}c\right\}.
$$
</Step>
<Step why="Slater's condition holds whenever some x satisfies Ax < b, so the gap is zero and the primal solution is recovered from the dual optimum through the stationarity map of step 2.">
$x^\star = -Q^{-1}(c + A^\top\alpha^\star)$.
</Step>
</Derivation>

Note the structural change: the primal has $m$ general linear inequality constraints; the dual has
only $\alpha\ge0$. The constraint complexity has moved into the quadratic form $AQ^{-1}A^\top$. That
trade is exactly what the SVM dual exploits, where $AQ^{-1}A^\top$ becomes the kernel matrix.
