## Newton's method

<Definition id="newton-def" title="Newton's method">
$$
x_{t+1} = x_t - \big[\nabla^2f(x_t)\big]^{-1}\nabla f(x_t).
$$
Equivalently: minimise the *second-order* Taylor model of $f$ at $x_t$ exactly, rather than the
quadratic upper bound of the descent lemma.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="newton-quadratic" title="Local quadratic convergence">
Let $f$ be twice differentiable with $\nabla^2f$ being $M$-Lipschitz, and let $x^\star$ satisfy
$\nabla f(x^\star)=0$ and $\nabla^2f(x^\star)\succeq\mu I$ with $\mu>0$. Then there is
$r>0$ such that whenever $\norm{x_t-x^\star}_2\le r$,
$$
\norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2 \le \frac{M}{\mu}\norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Write $H_t = \nabla^2f(x_t)$ and $e_t = x_t - x^\star$. By the fundamental theorem of calculus applied
to $\nabla f$ along the segment from $x^\star$ to $x_t$, and $\nabla f(x^\star)=0$,
$$
\nabla f(x_t) = \int_0^1 \nabla^2f\big(x^\star + s e_t\big)e_t\,ds .
$$
Therefore
$$
e_{t+1} = e_t - H_t^{-1}\nabla f(x_t)
= H_t^{-1}\left[H_te_t - \int_0^1\nabla^2f(x^\star+se_t)e_t\,ds\right]
= H_t^{-1}\int_0^1\big[H_t - \nabla^2f(x^\star+se_t)\big]e_t\,ds .
$$
By Lipschitzness of the Hessian, $\norm{H_t - \nabla^2f(x^\star+se_t)} \le M\norm{x_t - x^\star - se_t}_2 = M(1-s)\norm{e_t}_2$,
so
$$
\norm{e_{t+1}}_2 \le \norm{H_t^{-1}}\,\norm{e_t}_2^2 \int_0^1 M(1-s)\,ds = \frac{M}{2}\norm{H_t^{-1}}\norm{e_t}_2^2 .
$$
Finally, by continuity of $\nabla^2 f$ there is $r>0$ with $\nabla^2f(x)\succeq\tfrac\mu2 I$ for
$\norm{x-x^\star}_2\le r$, hence $\norm{H_t^{-1}}\le 2/\mu$, giving the constant $M/\mu$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Quadratic convergence means the number of correct digits **doubles** each step: $10^{-2}$, $10^{-4}$,
$10^{-8}$, $10^{-16}$. In the local regime Newton's method essentially terminates in five or six
iterations regardless of conditioning. The hypotheses are what cost: the theorem is *local*, requires
a nondegenerate Hessian, and says nothing about how to reach the neighbourhood.
</KeyIdea>

<Proposition id="newton-affine-invariance" title="Affine invariance">
Let $T$ be invertible and $\tilde f(z) = f(Tz)$. If $\tilde x_t = T^{-1}x_t$ then the Newton iterates
of $\tilde f$ from $\tilde x_0$ satisfy $\tilde x_t = T^{-1}x_t$ for all $t$. No such statement holds
for gradient descent.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
$\nabla\tilde f(z) = T^\top\nabla f(Tz)$ and $\nabla^2\tilde f(z) = T^\top\nabla^2f(Tz)T$. Hence
$$
\big[\nabla^2\tilde f(z)\big]^{-1}\nabla\tilde f(z)
= T^{-1}\big[\nabla^2f(Tz)\big]^{-1}T^{-\top}T^\top\nabla f(Tz)
= T^{-1}\big[\nabla^2f(Tz)\big]^{-1}\nabla f(Tz),
$$
so the Newton step transforms as $T^{-1}$ times the original step. Induction gives the claim. For
gradient descent the step is $T^\top\nabla f(Tz)$, which does not transform as $T^{-1}$ times
$\nabla f(Tz)$ unless $T$ is orthogonal.
</Proof>

This is why no condition number appears in the Newton rate and no step size needs tuning: the method
does not see the coordinate system. Everything a rescaling could break, it is immune to.

## Quasi-Newton: BFGS and L-BFGS

Newton's method needs $\nabla^2 f$ ($O(d^2)$ memory) and a solve ($O(d^3)$ time). Quasi-Newton methods
build an approximation $B_t \approx \nabla^2f(x_t)^{-1}$ from gradient differences alone.

<Definition id="secant-def" title="Secant condition">
Let $s_t = x_{t+1}-x_t$ and $g_t = \nabla f(x_{t+1})-\nabla f(x_t)$. A curvature approximation
$H_{t+1}\approx\nabla^2f$ should satisfy the **secant condition** $H_{t+1}s_t = g_t$, which is the
exact relation for a quadratic.
</Definition>

<Proposition id="secant-condition" title="The BFGS update">
The BFGS update to the inverse approximation $B_t \approx H_t^{-1}$ is
$$
B_{t+1} = \left(I - \frac{s_tg_t^\top}{g_t^\top s_t}\right)B_t\left(I-\frac{g_ts_t^\top}{g_t^\top s_t}\right) + \frac{s_ts_t^\top}{g_t^\top s_t}.
$$
It satisfies $B_{t+1}g_t = s_t$ (the secant condition in inverse form), preserves positive
definiteness when $g_t^\top s_t>0$, and is the solution of
$\min_B \norm{B - B_t}_W$ subject to $B = B^\top$ and $Bg_t = s_t$ for a particular weighted
Frobenius norm.
</Proposition>

The variational characterisation is proved in Nocedal–Wright and is quoted here. Two consequences
matter:

- $g_t^\top s_t > 0$ is a **curvature condition**; it holds automatically for strongly convex $f$ and must be enforced by a line search otherwise. If it fails, the update is skipped — a detail that matters in practice.
- Each update is a rank-two modification, so applying $B_t$ costs $O(d^2)$ rather than $O(d^3)$.

<Algorithm title="L-BFGS two-loop recursion">
<ol>
<li><strong>State.</strong> The last $m$ pairs $(s_i, g_i)$, typically $m \in [5,20]$. Never form $B_t$.</li>
<li><strong>First loop</strong> (newest to oldest): $q \leftarrow \nabla f(x_t)$; for each $i$, set $\alpha_i = \rho_i s_i^\top q$ with $\rho_i = 1/(g_i^\top s_i)$, then $q \leftarrow q - \alpha_i g_i$.</li>
<li><strong>Scale.</strong> $r \leftarrow \gamma q$ with $\gamma = (s_{\text{last}}^\top g_{\text{last}})/(g_{\text{last}}^\top g_{\text{last}})$.</li>
<li><strong>Second loop</strong> (oldest to newest): $\beta = \rho_i g_i^\top r$, then $r \leftarrow r + (\alpha_i - \beta)s_i$.</li>
<li><strong>Return</strong> $-r$ as the search direction; take a line search along it.</li>
</ol>
</Algorithm>

Cost per iteration: $O(md)$ time and memory. That is the whole reason L-BFGS is the default
general-purpose optimiser for smooth deterministic problems of moderate size.

## Conjugate gradient

<Theorem id="cg-optimality" title="Conjugate gradient minimises over Krylov subspaces">
For $f(x) = \tfrac12x^\top Ax - b^\top x$ with $A\succ0$, the conjugate gradient iterates satisfy
$$
x_t = \argmin\Big\{f(x) : x \in x_0 + \operatorname{span}\{r_0, Ar_0,\dots,A^{t-1}r_0\}\Big\},
\qquad r_0 = b - Ax_0,
$$
and consequently
$$
\frac{\norm{x_t-x^\star}_A}{\norm{x_0-x^\star}_A} \le 2\left(\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}\right)^t ,
$$
with exact termination at $t = d$ in exact arithmetic, and at $t = $ (number of distinct eigenvalues)
in general.
</Theorem>

Quoted from Nocedal–Wright / Trefethen–Bau. Note the rate is the same $\sqrt\kappa$ as
<TopicRef id="advanced-optimization/acceleration">acceleration</TopicRef> — and for good reason: CG
solves the optimal-polynomial problem exactly, adaptively, and acceleration solves it with a fixed
schedule. CG is the accelerated method for quadratics; Nesterov's contribution was extending the
rate to general convex functions.

## Why these are rare in deep learning

<Claim kind="empirical" id="second-order-deep-learning" title="Second-order methods are rarely used at scale">
Adam and SGD with momentum dominate deep learning; L-BFGS and Newton-type methods do not, despite
their superior rates on the problems where those rates apply.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The reasons are specific, and none of them is "second-order methods do not work":

1. **Cost.** $d \sim 10^9$ makes $O(d^2)$ memory impossible. L-BFGS's $O(md)$ is affordable, so cost alone is not decisive.
2. **Stochasticity.** L-BFGS's curvature pairs $(s_t, g_t)$ are built from gradient *differences*. With mini-batch gradients, the difference of two noisy gradients is dominated by noise unless the batch is large, and the secant condition becomes meaningless. This is the binding obstacle.
3. **The local regime never arrives.** Newton's quadratic convergence is a statement about a neighbourhood of a nondegenerate minimiser. Deep networks are trained for a fixed budget and stopped far from any such point; the Hessian at the stopping point is typically singular, with a large number of near-zero eigenvalues.
4. **Non-convexity.** Where the Hessian is indefinite the Newton step can point uphill. Trust-region and cubic-regularised variants fix this in theory and are the subject of active research; they are not yet competitive at scale.

Hessian-free and Kronecker-factored (K-FAC) methods are serious attempts to get second-order
information cheaply, and they do help on some problems. The honest summary is that the question is
open in practice and the theory does not currently favour either side.
</NotSaid>
