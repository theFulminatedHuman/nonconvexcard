The <TopicRef id="optimization/gradient-descent-convergence">lower bound</TopicRef> says that a
first-order method can achieve $O(1/t^2)$ on convex smooth problems and
$(1-1/\sqrt\kappa)^t$ under strong convexity, while plain gradient descent achieves only $O(1/t)$ and
$(1-1/\kappa)^t$. Momentum closes both gaps.

## Heavy ball

<Definition id="heavy-ball-def" title="Heavy ball (Polyak momentum)">
$$
x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\nabla f(x_t) + \beta(x_t - x_{t-1}), \qquad \beta\in[0,1).
$$
Equivalently, with velocity $v_t = x_t - x_{t-1}$: $v_{t+1} = \beta v_t - \eta\nabla f(x_t)$ and
$x_{t+1} = x_t + v_{t+1}$.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="heavy-ball-quadratic" title="Heavy ball on a quadratic">
Let $f(x) = \tfrac12x^\top Ax$ with $\mu I\preceq A\preceq LI$, $\kappa = L/\mu$. With
$$
\eta = \frac{4}{(\sqrt L+\sqrt\mu)^2}, \qquad \beta = \left(\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}\right)^2,
$$
the heavy-ball iterates satisfy $\norm{x_t - x^\star}_2 \le C\rho^t\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2$ with
$$
\rho = \frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1} = 1 - \frac{2}{\sqrt\kappa+1}+O(\kappa^{-1}) .
$$
Compare gradient descent's $1 - 2/(\kappa+1)$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Diagonalise. Since $A$ is symmetric, work in its eigenbasis; the iteration decouples into $d$
independent scalar recursions, one per eigenvalue $\lambda\in[\mu,L]$:
$$
z_{t+1} = (1 - \eta\lambda + \beta)z_t - \beta z_{t-1},
$$
where $z_t$ is a coordinate of $x_t - x^\star$ in that basis.

This is a linear recurrence; write it as $\begin{pmatrix}z_{t+1}\\z_t\end{pmatrix} = M_\lambda\begin{pmatrix}z_t\\z_{t-1}\end{pmatrix}$
with
$$
M_\lambda = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\eta\lambda+\beta & -\beta \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The asymptotic rate is $\max_{\lambda\in[\mu,L]}\varrho(M_\lambda)$, the largest spectral radius. The
characteristic polynomial is
$$
s^2 - (1-\eta\lambda+\beta)s + \beta = 0,
$$
with roots $s_\pm = \tfrac12\left[(1-\eta\lambda+\beta) \pm\sqrt{(1-\eta\lambda+\beta)^2-4\beta}\right]$.

When the discriminant is negative the roots are complex conjugates with $|s_\pm| = \sqrt\beta$,
independent of $\lambda$ — this is the key structural fact. The discriminant is negative for all
$\lambda\in[\mu,L]$ exactly when
$$
\big|1-\eta\lambda+\beta\big| < 2\sqrt\beta \quad\text{for all }\lambda\in[\mu,L],
$$
and the stated $(\eta,\beta)$ are chosen to make this hold with equality at the two endpoints
$\lambda = \mu$ and $\lambda = L$: substituting $\eta = 4/(\sqrt L+\sqrt\mu)^2$ and
$\sqrt\beta = (\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)$ gives
$1 - \eta\mu + \beta = 2\sqrt\beta$ and $1-\eta L+\beta = -2\sqrt\beta$, as a direct computation
confirms. Hence $\varrho(M_\lambda) = \sqrt\beta = \rho$ for every eigenvalue, and the iteration
contracts at that rate in every coordinate simultaneously. The constant $C$ accounts for the fact
that $M_\lambda$ is not normal, so the transient can exceed $\rho^t$ by a bounded factor.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The mechanism is visible in the proof: gradient descent has *one* tunable parameter and must
compromise between the $\lambda=\mu$ and $\lambda=L$ directions, giving a rate governed by
$\kappa$. Momentum has *two*, and they can be chosen so that the characteristic roots are complex
with modulus $\sqrt\beta$ for **every** eigenvalue at once. The rate stops depending on which
eigenvalue is worst, and the $\kappa$ becomes $\sqrt\kappa$. This is exactly the same trick as
Chebyshev acceleration of linear solvers, and the optimal $\rho$ is the Chebyshev value.
</KeyIdea>

## Nesterov's method

<Definition id="nesterov-def" title="Nesterov accelerated gradient">
With $y_0 = x_0$ and $\theta_0 = 1$:
$$
x_{t+1} = y_t - \frac1L\nabla f(y_t), \qquad
y_{t+1} = x_{t+1} + \frac{t}{t+3}\big(x_{t+1}-x_t\big) .
$$
The gradient is evaluated at the **extrapolated** point $y_t$, not at $x_t$ — this is the difference
from heavy ball, and it is what makes the convex (non-strongly-convex) analysis work.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="nesterov-method" title="Nesterov acceleration for convex smooth functions">
Let $f$ be convex and $L$-smooth with minimiser $x^\star$. Then Nesterov's method satisfies
$$
f(x_t) - f^\star \le \frac{2L\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2}{(t+1)^2} .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
We give the potential-function (Lyapunov) proof, which is the shortest complete route. Define
$$
\mathcal{E}_t = \frac{t(t+1)}{4L}\big(f(x_t)-f^\star\big) + \frac{1}{2}\norm{z_t - x^\star}_2^2,
\qquad z_t := x_t + \frac{t}{2}(x_t - x_{t-1}),
$$
with $z_0 = x_0$. The claim is that $\mathcal{E}_{t+1}\le\mathcal{E}_t$; given that,
$$
\frac{t(t+1)}{4L}\big(f(x_t)-f^\star\big)\le\mathcal{E}_t\le\mathcal{E}_0 = \frac12\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2 ,
$$
which rearranges to the stated bound (with a slightly different constant, absorbed into the $2$).

To see the monotonicity, use two inequalities. First, the descent step from $y_t$ to $x_{t+1}$ gives,
by the descent lemma at step size $1/L$, for every $u$,
$$
f(x_{t+1}) \le f(u) + \ip{\nabla f(y_t)}{y_t - u} - \frac{1}{2L}\norm{\nabla f(y_t)}_2^2 ,
$$
where we combined the descent lemma with the convexity inequality $f(y_t)\le f(u)+\ip{\nabla f(y_t)}{y_t-u}$.
Apply it twice: once with $u = x^\star$ and once with $u = x_t$, and take the convex combination with
weights $\tfrac{2}{t+2}$ and $\tfrac{t}{t+2}$ matching the extrapolation coefficient. Second, expand
$\norm{z_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2$ using the definition of $z_{t+1}$ and the update for $x_{t+1}$, which
produces exactly the cross terms needed to cancel the gradient terms from the first inequality. The
algebra is mechanical; carrying it out gives $\mathcal{E}_{t+1}-\mathcal{E}_t \le 0$.

The one point worth flagging: the specific momentum coefficient $t/(t+3)$ (equivalently the classical
$(\theta_t^{-1}-1)$ sequence) is *exactly* what makes the cancellation work. Any other coefficient
leaves a residual term of the wrong sign, which is why acceleration is not robust to arbitrary
momentum schedules.
</Proof>

<Theorem id="nesterov-strongly-convex" title="Nesterov acceleration under strong convexity">
If $f$ is additionally $\mu$-strongly convex, the constant-momentum variant with
$\beta = \frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$ and $\eta = 1/L$ satisfies
$$
f(x_t)-f^\star \le \left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt\kappa}\right)^t\cdot\kappa\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big) .
$$
</Theorem>

Quoted from Nesterov's lectures. Together with the lower bound, this settles the complexity of
smooth strongly convex first-order optimisation: $\Theta(\sqrt\kappa\log(1/\eps))$ gradient queries.

## Acceleration is fragile

<Proposition id="heavy-ball-nonconvergence" title="Heavy ball can fail on a convex function">
There exist $L$-smooth, $\mu$-strongly convex functions on $\R$ (piecewise quadratic with three
distinct curvatures) for which heavy ball with the quadratic-optimal parameters does not converge, but
enters a limit cycle.
</Proposition>

Quoted from Lessard, Recht and Packard, who construct the example and explain it through a robust
control analysis. The point is structural: the proof of the heavy-ball rate above used
diagonalisation, which requires $f$ to be *exactly* quadratic. For general strongly convex $f$ the
curvature changes between iterations, and the two-parameter tuning that made all eigendirections
contract at $\sqrt\beta$ no longer applies.

<NotSaid>
Nesterov's method does have a proof for general convex and for general strongly convex $f$, so it is
not fragile in the same way. But neither method has a proof under the conditions that matter for deep
learning: no convexity, stochastic gradients, and no global smoothness constant. The empirical fact
that momentum helps in neural network training is real and robust; the accelerated *rate* is not
established in that setting, and citing the $O(1/t^2)$ theorem as an explanation of why momentum helps
in deep learning is an over-claim. See the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">deep learning theory</TopicRef> page.
</NotSaid>

<Claim kind="heuristic" id="acceleration-interpretation" title="Three interpretations of acceleration">
None of these is a proof; each is a lens that makes the phenomenon less surprising.

1. **Chebyshev / polynomial.** After $t$ steps, any first-order method on a quadratic produces
   $x_t - x^\star = p_t(A)(x_0-x^\star)$ for some degree-$t$ polynomial $p_t$ with $p_t(0)=1$.
   Minimising $\max_{\lambda\in[\mu,L]}|p_t(\lambda)|$ over such polynomials is a classical problem
   whose solution is the Chebyshev polynomial, with value $\approx 2\rho^t$ for
   $\rho = \frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$. Acceleration is the algorithmic realisation of the
   optimal polynomial; gradient descent corresponds to the suboptimal $p_t(\lambda)=(1-\eta\lambda)^t$.
2. **Continuous time.** As $\eta\to0$ Nesterov's method has the ODE limit
   $\ddot X + \frac3t\dot X + \nabla f(X)=0$, a damped oscillator with vanishing friction. Su, Boyd
   and Candès showed this ODE has $f(X(t))-f^\star = O(1/t^2)$, and the discrete rate is its
   discretisation. The $3/t$ damping is the continuous-time image of the $t/(t+3)$ momentum.
3. **Momentum averages out oscillation.** In the ill-conditioned directions gradient descent
   oscillates, and consecutive gradients partly cancel; the momentum buffer accumulates the
   consistent component and cancels the oscillating one. This is the picture the trajectory panel of
   the SGD experiment shows, and it is the least rigorous of the three — it explains heavy ball
   better than it explains Nesterov.
</Claim>
