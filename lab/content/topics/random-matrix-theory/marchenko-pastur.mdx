<Intuition>
You have $n$ samples from $\Ncal(0, I_p)$ and you form the sample covariance
$\hat\Sigma = \frac1n\sum_i x_ix_i^\top$. Since $\E\hat\Sigma = I_p$, you might expect every eigenvalue
near $1$. For $p$ fixed and $n\to\infty$ that is correct. But if $p$ grows with $n$ — say $p = n/2$ —
the eigenvalues spread over an interval of width $O(1)$ and **stay there**, no matter how large $n$
gets. The number of parameters being estimated grows with the data, and the spread is what that costs.
</Intuition>

## The law

<Theorem id="mp-law" title="Marchenko–Pastur law">
Let $X \in \R^{n\times p}$ have i.i.d. entries with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and set
$\hat\Sigma = \frac1n X^\top X$. Suppose $n,p\to\infty$ with $p/n \to \lambda \in (0,\infty)$. Then the
empirical spectral distribution of $\hat\Sigma$ converges weakly, almost surely, to the
**Marchenko–Pastur law** $\mu_{MP}$, which has

- density $\displaystyle \rho_\lambda(x) = \frac{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{2\pi\sigma^2\lambda x}$ on $[a,b]$, where $a = \sigma^2(1-\sqrt\lambda)^2$ and $b = \sigma^2(1+\sqrt\lambda)^2$;
- plus an atom of mass $1 - 1/\lambda$ at $0$ when $\lambda > 1$.
</Theorem>

The proof (via the Stieltjes transform $s(z) = \int (x-z)^{-1}d\mu(x)$, which satisfies a quadratic
self-consistent equation in the limit) is in the references and is not reproduced here.

<Proposition id="mp-density-normalised" title="The density integrates to one">
For $\lambda \in (0,1]$ and $\sigma = 1$, $\int_a^b \rho_\lambda(x)\,dx = 1$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Substitute $x = 1 + \lambda - 2\sqrt\lambda\cos\theta$, which maps $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ onto $[a,b]$
since $a = 1+\lambda - 2\sqrt\lambda$ and $b = 1+\lambda+2\sqrt\lambda$. A short computation gives
$(b-x)(x-a) = 4\lambda\sin^2\theta$ and $dx = 2\sqrt\lambda\sin\theta\,d\theta$, so
$$
\int_a^b \rho_\lambda \,dx
= \int_0^\pi \frac{2\sqrt\lambda\,\sin\theta}{2\pi\lambda\,x}\cdot 2\sqrt\lambda\sin\theta\,d\theta
= \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin^2\theta}{1+\lambda-2\sqrt\lambda\cos\theta}\,d\theta .
$$
The standard integral $\int_0^\pi \frac{\sin^2\theta}{1 - 2r\cos\theta + r^2}d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$
for $|r|<1$, applied with $r = \sqrt\lambda$ after dividing numerator and denominator by $1$, gives
the value $\frac2\pi\cdot\frac\pi2 = 1$. (For $\lambda = 1$ the integral is still convergent because
the $1/x$ singularity at $a=0$ is integrable against $\sqrt{x-a}$.)
</Proof>

<Proposition id="mp-edges" title="Bulk edges and conditioning">
With $\sigma = 1$ and $\lambda = p/n \le 1$, the extreme eigenvalues of $\hat\Sigma$ satisfy, almost
surely in the limit,
$$
\lambda_{\max}(\hat\Sigma) \to (1+\sqrt\lambda)^2, \qquad
\lambda_{\min}(\hat\Sigma) \to (1-\sqrt\lambda)^2,
$$
so the condition number satisfies
$$
\kappa(\hat\Sigma) \to \left(\frac{1+\sqrt\lambda}{1-\sqrt\lambda}\right)^2 .
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The edges are the endpoints of the support of the limiting measure, and the convergence of the
extreme eigenvalues to them is the
<TopicRef id="random-matrix-theory/spectral-norm-of-random-matrices">Bai–Yin law</TopicRef> applied to
$\hat\Sigma = \frac1n X^\top X$: $\lambda_{\max}(\hat\Sigma) = \sigma_{\max}(X)^2/n$ and
$\sigma_{\max}(X)/\sqrt n \to 1 + \sqrt\lambda$, and similarly for the minimum. The condition number
is the ratio.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Put numbers in. At $\lambda = 0.01$ ($n = 100p$), $\kappa \approx 1.5$ — the sample covariance is
essentially as well conditioned as the truth. At $\lambda = 0.5$, $\kappa \approx 34$. At
$\lambda = 0.9$, $\kappa \approx 1500$. At $\lambda = 1$ the smallest eigenvalue reaches $0$ and
$\hat\Sigma$ is singular. Every method that inverts a sample covariance — ordinary least squares,
linear discriminant analysis, Gaussian maximum likelihood, Newton's method on a quadratic — degrades
along exactly this curve, and the degradation is not a numerical artefact but the true geometry of the
estimate.
</KeyIdea>

<Corollary id="classical-limit" title="Recovering the classical limit">
As $\lambda \downarrow 0$ the support $[(1-\sqrt\lambda)^2,(1+\sqrt\lambda)^2]$ shrinks to $\{1\}$,
so $\mu_{MP} \Rightarrow \delta_1$. This is the classical statement: for $p$ fixed and $n\to\infty$,
$\hat\Sigma\to I_p$ and all eigenvalues converge to $1$.
</Corollary>

<Proof>
The endpoints are continuous in $\lambda$ with common limit $1$ at $\lambda = 0$, and a sequence of
probability measures supported on intervals shrinking to a point converges weakly to the point mass
there.
</Proof>

<Note title="Two asymptotic regimes, two different answers">
The classical regime ($p$ fixed, $n\to\infty$) and the high-dimensional regime ($p/n\to\lambda>0$)
give qualitatively different conclusions about the *same* estimator. Neither is wrong; they describe
different experiments. The question for a practitioner is which one their data resembles, and for
modern data — thousands of features, hundreds of samples — the answer is almost always the second.
This is the whole motivation for the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-statistics/covariance-estimation">high-dimensional statistics</TopicRef>
section.
</Note>

## What the law does not describe

<Claim kind="empirical" id="mp-not-real-data" title="Real covariance spectra are not Marchenko–Pastur">
Plot the eigenvalue histogram of the covariance of a real dataset — word embeddings, gene expression,
daily asset returns — and it will typically show a Marchenko–Pastur-like bulk **plus a small number of
large outlying eigenvalues**. The bulk is well described by the law; the outliers are not, and they
are usually the interesting part.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The theorem above assumes the population covariance is $\sigma^2 I_p$ — no signal at all. Real data
has signal, and the relevant model is the **spiked covariance model**:
$\Sigma = I_p + \sum_{k=1}^r h_k v_kv_k^\top$ with $r$ small. There, the correct statement is the
Baik–Ben Arous–Péché phase transition: a spike of strength $h$ is detectable in the sample spectrum
if and only if $h > \sqrt\lambda$, and below that threshold the corresponding sample eigenvalue is
absorbed into the bulk and the sample eigenvector is asymptotically uncorrelated with $v_k$.

That is a sharp and somewhat brutal statement: there is a signal strength below which no amount of
eigen-analysis recovers the direction, *not because the estimator is bad* but because the information
is not in the sample. It is quoted here from the reference and is a separate theorem, not a corollary
of the Marchenko–Pastur law.
</NotSaid>
