## The variational reduction

<Lemma id="variational-norm" title="Bilinear variational form of the spectral norm">
For any $A \in \R^{n\times p}$,
$$
\norm{A} = \max_{u \in S^{n-1}}\ \max_{v\in S^{p-1}}\ \ip{Au}{v} = \max_{u,v} u^\top A^\top v .
$$
Moreover, if $\Ncal \subseteq S^{p-1}$ and $\mathcal{M}\subseteq S^{n-1}$ are $\eps$-nets with
$\eps<1/2$, then
$$
\norm{A} \le \frac{1}{1-2\eps}\max_{v\in\mathcal{M}}\max_{u\in\Ncal} \ip{Au}{v}.
$$
</Lemma>

<Proof>
The first identity is the definition of the operator norm combined with
$\norm{w}_2 = \max_{v\in S^{n-1}}\ip{w}{v}$ (attained at $w/\norm{w}_2$).

For the net statement, let $u_\star, v_\star$ attain the maximum, and choose $u\in\Ncal$,
$v\in\mathcal{M}$ within $\eps$ of them. Then
$$
\norm{A} = \ip{Au_\star}{v_\star}
= \ip{Au}{v} + \ip{A(u_\star - u)}{v_\star} + \ip{Au}{v_\star - v},
$$
and each of the last two terms is at most $\eps\norm{A}$ in absolute value, by the definition of the
operator norm and Cauchy–Schwarz. Rearranging gives the claim.
</Proof>

## The main bound

<Theorem id="operator-norm-bound" title="Spectral norm of a matrix with sub-Gaussian entries">
Let $A\in\R^{n\times p}$ have independent, mean-zero entries with
$\norm{A_{ij}}_{\psi_2}\le K$. Then there is an absolute constant $C$ such that for every $t \ge 0$,
$$
\P\Big(\norm{A} \ge CK\big(\sqrt n + \sqrt p + t\big)\Big) \le 2e^{-t^2}.
$$
In particular $\E\norm{A} \le CK(\sqrt n + \sqrt p)$.
</Theorem>

<Assumptions>
Independence of the **entries** (not merely of the rows), mean zero, uniformly bounded $\psi_2$
norm. No identical distribution and no symmetry is needed.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Set $\eps = 1/4$ and fix $\tfrac14$-nets $\Ncal \subseteq S^{p-1}$ and $\mathcal{M}\subseteq S^{n-1}$.
By the <TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/nets-covering-and-packing">volumetric bound</TopicRef>,
$$
|\Ncal| \le 12^p, \qquad |\mathcal{M}| \le 12^n .
$$

**Step 1: one pair at a time.** Fix $u \in S^{p-1}$ and $v \in S^{n-1}$ and consider
$$
\ip{Au}{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^p A_{ij}u_jv_i .
$$
This is a linear combination of the independent, mean-zero, sub-Gaussian entries $A_{ij}$ with
coefficients $u_jv_i$. By the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/sub-gaussian-random-variables">sum rule</TopicRef> it is
sub-Gaussian with
$$
\norm{\ip{Au}{v}}_{\psi_2}^2 \le C_0K^2\sum_{i,j}u_j^2v_i^2 = C_0K^2\norm{u}_2^2\norm{v}_2^2 = C_0K^2 .
$$
Hence for every $s\ge0$,
$$
\P\big(\ip{Au}{v}\ge s\big) \le 2\exp\left(-\frac{c_0 s^2}{K^2}\right).
$$

**Step 2: union bound over the nets.** Let $s = CK(\sqrt n+\sqrt p + t)$ for a constant $C$ to be
chosen. Then
$$
\P\left(\max_{u\in\Ncal}\max_{v\in\mathcal{M}}\ip{Au}{v}\ge s\right)
\le 12^{n+p}\cdot 2\exp\left(-\frac{c_0s^2}{K^2}\right)
= 2\exp\Big((n+p)\log 12 - \frac{c_0s^2}{K^2}\Big).
$$
Now $s^2/K^2 = C^2(\sqrt n+\sqrt p+t)^2 \ge C^2\big(\tfrac{n+p}{2} + t^2\big)$, using
$(a+b+c)^2 \ge \tfrac12(a+b)^2 + c^2 \ge \tfrac12(a^2+b^2)+c^2$ for non-negative $a,b,c$. Choosing $C$
large enough that $c_0C^2/2 \ge \log 12 + 1$ and $c_0C^2 \ge 1$, the exponent is at most
$-(n+p) - t^2 \le -t^2$, so the probability is at most $2e^{-t^2}$.

**Step 3: remove the net.** On the complementary event, the net lemma with $\eps=1/4$ gives
$\norm{A} \le \frac{1}{1-1/2}\,s = 2s$, which is the claim after renaming the constant.

The bound on the expectation follows by integrating the tail:
$\E\norm{A}\le CK(\sqrt n+\sqrt p) + CK\int_0^\infty 2e^{-t^2}dt$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Count the exponents once more. The net has $e^{(n+p)\log 12}$ points, and each fails with probability
$e^{-c s^2/K^2}$. For the union bound to survive we need $s^2 \gtrsim K^2(n+p)$, that is
$s \gtrsim K\sqrt{n+p} \asymp K(\sqrt n+\sqrt p)$. The answer is forced by the arithmetic of the net
size against the concentration exponent — there is no cleverness left in the argument once the two
ingredients are in place.
</KeyIdea>

<Note title="Comparing norms">
The Frobenius norm of the same matrix is $\norm{A}_F \approx K\sqrt{np}$, since it is a sum of $np$
squared entries. So the theorem says
$$
\norm{A} \asymp K(\sqrt n+\sqrt p) \ll K\sqrt{np} \asymp \norm{A}_F
$$
whenever both $n,p$ are large. A random matrix has a much smaller operator norm than its Frobenius
norm allows in the worst case — its singular values are spread out, not concentrated on one. This is
the quantitative statement behind "random matrices are well conditioned when $n \gg p$".
</Note>

## Sharp constants: the Bai–Yin law

<Theorem id="bai-yin" title="Bai–Yin law">
Let $A \in \R^{n\times p}$ have i.i.d. entries with mean $0$, variance $1$ and finite fourth moment,
and let $n,p \to \infty$ with $p/n \to \lambda \in (0,1]$. Then almost surely
$$
\sigma_{\max}(A) = \sqrt n + \sqrt p + o(\sqrt n),
\qquad \sigma_{\min}(A) = \sqrt n - \sqrt p + o(\sqrt n) .
$$
</Theorem>

Quoted from the references. The point of comparison with the theorem above is instructive: the net
argument gives the right *order* with an unspecified constant $C$, valid for every finite $n,p$ and
with an exponential tail. Bai–Yin gives the exact constant $1$ in front of each square root, but only
asymptotically and only almost surely. Both are useful, for different purposes — and the
finite-sample statement is the one a statistician needs.

## Wigner matrices and the semicircle law

<Definition id="wigner" title="Wigner matrix">
A **Wigner matrix** is a symmetric $W \in \R^{d\times d}$ whose entries $\{W_{ij}\}_{i\le j}$ are
independent with mean $0$ and variance $1$ (the diagonal may have a different variance). The
normalisation of interest is $W/\sqrt d$, which keeps the spectrum $O(1)$.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="semicircle" title="Wigner semicircle law">
Let $W_d$ be Wigner matrices with uniformly bounded fourth moments and let
$\mu_d = \frac1d\sum_{i=1}^d \delta_{\lambda_i(W_d/\sqrt d)}$ be the empirical spectral distribution.
Then $\mu_d$ converges weakly, almost surely, to the **semicircle law** with density
$$
\rho_{sc}(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^2}\,\one\{|x|\le2\}.
$$
</Theorem>

The standard proofs are the moment method — show
$\frac1d\E\tr\big((W/\sqrt d)^{2k}\big) \to \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}$, the Catalan numbers, which are
the moments of the semicircle — and the Stieltjes transform method, which derives the self-consistent
equation $s(z) = \frac{1}{-z - s(z)}$ for the limiting transform. Both are given in the references;
neither is short, and neither is reproduced here.

<Note title="What to take from it">
Two facts are worth carrying. First, the spectrum of a normalised Wigner matrix fills the interval
$[-2,2]$ with a definite shape — it does not concentrate at a point, and it does not have Gaussian
tails. Second, the limit is **universal**: it depends on the entry variance and nothing else, not on
whether the entries are Gaussian, Rademacher or anything with four moments. Universality is the
organising theme of random matrix theory, and it is why simulations with convenient distributions are
informative about the general case — for the *bulk*. Extreme eigenvalues are more delicate and are
governed by the fourth moment.
</Note>

## Matrix concentration

The net argument above is specific to the operator norm of a matrix with independent entries. A more
flexible tool handles sums of *dependent-within-summand* random matrices.

<Theorem id="matrix-bernstein" title="Matrix Bernstein inequality">
Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be independent, mean-zero, symmetric random matrices in $\R^{d\times d}$ with
$\norm{X_i}\le M$ almost surely, and set $\sigma^2 = \norm{\sum_i \E X_i^2}$. Then for all $t\ge0$,
$$
\P\left(\norm{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i} \ge t\right)
\le 2d\exp\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\sigma^2 + \tfrac23 Mt}\right).
$$
</Theorem>

Quoted from Tropp's survey. Compare with the scalar
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-inequalities">Bernstein inequality</TopicRef>:
the bound is identical except for the factor $2d$ in front, which is the price of passing from a
number to a $d\times d$ matrix. Since it appears inside a logarithm after inversion, the practical
cost is an extra $\log d$ in every sample-complexity statement — which is exactly the $\log d$ that
appears in covariance-estimation rates on the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-statistics/covariance-estimation">next section</TopicRef>.
