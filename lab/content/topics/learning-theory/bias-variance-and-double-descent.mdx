## The identity

<Proposition id="bv-exact" title="The bias–variance decomposition is an identity">
Let $y = f^\star(x)+\varepsilon$ with $\E[\varepsilon\mid x]=0$ and $\Var(\varepsilon\mid x)=\sigma^2$,
and let $\hat f_S$ be an estimator trained on a random sample $S$. Then for each fixed $x$,
$$
\E_{S,\varepsilon}\Big[\big(y - \hat f_S(x)\big)^2\Big]
= \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}
+ \underbrace{\big(\E_S\hat f_S(x) - f^\star(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+ \underbrace{\Var_S\big(\hat f_S(x)\big)}_{\text{variance}} .
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Write $\bar f(x) = \E_S\hat f_S(x)$ and expand around it:
$$
y - \hat f_S(x) = \varepsilon + \big(f^\star(x)-\bar f(x)\big) + \big(\bar f(x)-\hat f_S(x)\big).
$$
Square and take expectations over $S$ and $\varepsilon$, which are independent. The three cross terms
each vanish: $\E\varepsilon = 0$ kills the two involving $\varepsilon$, and
$\E_S[\bar f(x)-\hat f_S(x)] = 0$ kills the third. The three squares are the three named terms.
</Proof>

<Note title="Two things this identity is not">
It is not a trade-off, and it is not specific to any model. It is algebra, valid for every estimator
and every distribution with the stated moments. The noise term $\sigma^2$ is irreducible — no
estimator can beat it — so the *excess* risk is bias$^2$ + variance.
</Note>

<ExperimentRef id="bias-variance" />

<Claim kind="heuristic" id="bv-tradeoff-heuristic" title="The bias–variance trade-off">
The *trade-off* — that increasing model capacity necessarily decreases bias and increases variance,
so that risk is U-shaped in capacity — is an empirical regularity of classical model families, not a
consequence of the identity.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The identity says risk $=$ noise $+$ bias$^2$ $+$ variance. It says nothing about how either term
moves with capacity. For polynomial regression with $p \le n$, as in the experiment on this page, the
U-curve is real and reproducible. For $p > n$ it is not, and the reason is specific: once the model
interpolates, the fit is no longer determined by the loss alone and depends on which of the infinitely
many zero-loss solutions the algorithm selects. The variance of *that* object is a different quantity
with different behaviour.
</NotSaid>

## Past the interpolation threshold

Consider least squares with $p$ features and $n$ samples. For $p<n$ the solution is the usual
$\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$. For $p>n$ the system is underdetermined and the natural choice
is the **minimum-norm interpolator**
$$
\hat\beta = \argmin\{\norm\beta_2 : X\beta = y\} = X^\dagger y = X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}y ,
$$
which is what gradient descent from $\beta_0 = 0$ converges to — see the implicit-bias discussion on
the <TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">deep learning theory</TopicRef> page.

<Proposition id="interpolation-threshold" title="Why the risk blows up at the threshold">
For the least-squares estimator with $p<n$ and fixed design $X$, the variance term of the risk is
$$
\Var = \sigma^2\,\tr\big((X^\top X)^{-1}\Sigma\big) \ \ge\ \frac{\sigma^2\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)}{\lambda_{\min}(X^\top X)} ,
$$
which diverges as $\lambda_{\min}(X^\top X)\to0$. For i.i.d. Gaussian design,
$\lambda_{\min}(X^\top X)/n \to (1-\sqrt{p/n})^2$, which vanishes exactly at $p = n$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
With $\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ and $y = X\beta^\star+\varepsilon$,
$\hat\beta - \beta^\star = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon$, so
$$
\E\big[(\hat\beta-\beta^\star)(\hat\beta-\beta^\star)^\top\big] = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}
$$
using $\E[\varepsilon\varepsilon^\top]=\sigma^2I$. The prediction variance at a test point $x$ with
second moment $\Sigma = \E[xx^\top]$ is
$\E_x\big[x^\top(\hat\beta-\beta^\star)\big]^2 = \sigma^2\tr\big((X^\top X)^{-1}\Sigma\big)$, and
$\tr(AB) \ge \lambda_{\min}(B)\tr(A)\ge\lambda_{\min}(B)\lambda_{\max}(A)$ for PSD $A,B$ gives the
lower bound. The eigenvalue limit is the
<TopicRef id="random-matrix-theory/marchenko-pastur">Marchenko–Pastur</TopicRef> lower bulk edge with
$\lambda = p/n$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The divergence at $p=n$ is not mysterious and not specific to machine learning: it is the
conditioning of a square random matrix. At $p=n$ the design is square and nearly singular, so the
fit must use enormous coefficients to interpolate, and those coefficients amplify the noise. For
$p>n$ the matrix $XX^\top$ is $n\times n$ with aspect ratio $n/p<1$, hence **better** conditioned
again — and the minimum-norm constraint keeps the coefficients small. That is the mechanism behind
the second descent, at least in the linear case.
</KeyIdea>

<Theorem id="double-descent-linear" title="Double descent for minimum-norm linear regression">
In the proportional asymptotic regime $p/n\to\gamma$ with isotropic Gaussian features and a
well-specified linear model, the risk of the minimum-norm interpolator has an explicit limit
$\mathcal{R}(\gamma)$ which

- increases on $\gamma\in(0,1)$ and diverges as $\gamma\uparrow1$;
- decreases on $\gamma\in(1,\infty)$, approaching $\sigma^2 + \norm{\beta^\star}_2^2$-dependent limits;
- can be **lower** at some $\gamma>1$ than at every $\gamma<1$, depending on the signal-to-noise ratio.
</Theorem>

Quoted from Hastie, Montanari, Rosset and Tibshirani, who compute $\mathcal{R}(\gamma)$ exactly using
random matrix theory. The companion result of Bartlett, Long, Lugosi and Tsigler characterises
**benign overfitting**: the minimum-norm interpolator has small risk precisely when the spectrum of
the feature covariance has a particular shape — a few large eigenvalues carrying the signal and a long
tail of small ones that absorbs the noise without contributing to prediction. Their effective-rank
conditions are the sharpest available statement of when interpolation is harmless.

<Claim kind="empirical" id="dd-empirical" title="Double descent in deep networks">
Nakkiran et al. report test-error curves with a clear second descent in deep networks, as a function
of model width, of training set size (where more data can transiently hurt), and of training time
(epoch-wise double descent).
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The empirical phenomenon is robust and has been replicated. What is **not** established:

- that the mechanism is the same as in the linear case. The linear analysis relies on the minimum-$\ell^2$-norm characterisation of the interpolator; for a deep network trained by SGD the implicit bias is not known in closed form, so the analogy is suggestive rather than derived.
- that "wider is better" in general. The curves depend on the noise level, the parameterisation, and the amount of explicit regularisation; adding optimal ridge regularisation removes the peak entirely in the linear case, and label noise is what makes the peak prominent in the deep case.
- that double descent explains why deep learning generalises. It describes a shape of the risk curve; it does not identify the inductive bias that makes the interpolating solution good.

A fair summary: the linear theory is rigorous and complete; the deep learning phenomenon is
well-documented and its explanation is open.
</NotSaid>
