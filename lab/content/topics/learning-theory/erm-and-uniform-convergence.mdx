## The setup

Data $(X,Y)\sim\Dcal$ on $\Xcal\times\Ycal$, a hypothesis class
$\Hcal \subseteq \{h : \Xcal\to\Ycal\}$, a loss $\ell(h(x),y) \in [0,1]$. Define

$$
\underbrace{R(h) = \E_{\Dcal}\big[\ell(h(X),Y)\big]}_{\text{risk}},
\qquad
\underbrace{\hat R_n(h) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(h(x_i),y_i)}_{\text{empirical risk}} .
$$

**Empirical risk minimisation** returns $\hat h \in \argmin_{h\in\Hcal}\hat R_n(h)$.

<Intuition>
For a *fixed* $h$, $\hat R_n(h)$ is an average of $n$ i.i.d. bounded variables with mean $R(h)$, so
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-inequalities">Hoeffding</TopicRef> gives
$|\hat R_n(h)-R(h)| \lesssim \sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n}$. But $\hat h$ is *not* fixed — it was chosen by
looking at the data, so it is correlated with the very sample used to evaluate it. The whole subject
exists to handle that correlation, and the standard device is to bound the deviation **uniformly**
over $\Hcal$, so that whichever $h$ the algorithm picks is covered.
</Intuition>

<Proposition id="erm-decomposition" title="Excess risk decomposition for ERM">
Let $h^\star \in \argmin_{h\in\Hcal}R(h)$ and let
$$
\Delta_n = \sup_{h\in\Hcal}\big|R(h)-\hat R_n(h)\big|
$$
be the uniform deviation. Then
$$
R(\hat h) - \inf_{h\in\Hcal}R(h) \le 2\Delta_n,
$$
and therefore
$$
R(\hat h) - \underbrace{\inf_{\text{all }h}R(h)}_{\text{Bayes risk}}
\le \underbrace{2\Delta_n}_{\text{estimation error}} + \underbrace{\Big(\inf_{h\in\Hcal}R(h) - \inf_{\text{all }h}R(h)\Big)}_{\text{approximation error}} .
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Chain three inequalities:
$$
R(\hat h) \le \hat R_n(\hat h) + \Delta_n
\le \hat R_n(h^\star) + \Delta_n
\le R(h^\star) + 2\Delta_n .
$$
The first and third are the definition of $\Delta_n$; the middle is the definition of $\hat h$ as an
empirical minimiser. Subtracting $R(h^\star) = \inf_{h\in\Hcal}R(h)$ gives the first claim, and adding
and subtracting the Bayes risk gives the second.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
This three-line argument is the reason the whole subject is about $\Delta_n$. It also displays the
fundamental tension: enlarging $\Hcal$ decreases the approximation error and increases $\Delta_n$.
That is the honest statement of the bias–variance trade for ERM — and note that it is a statement
about *this bound*, not about the true risk, which is exactly the loophole the double-descent
phenomenon exploits.
</KeyIdea>

## Finite classes

<Theorem id="finite-class" title="Generalisation bound for a finite hypothesis class">
Let $|\Hcal| = N$ and $\ell\in[0,1]$. With probability at least $1-\delta$ over the sample,
$$
\Delta_n = \sup_{h\in\Hcal}\big|R(h)-\hat R_n(h)\big| \le \sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2n}},
$$
and consequently $R(\hat h)\le\inf_{h\in\Hcal}R(h) + 2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2n}}$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Fix $h\in\Hcal$. The variables $\ell(h(x_i),y_i)$ are i.i.d. in $[0,1]$ with mean $R(h)$, so
Hoeffding's inequality gives, for any $t>0$,
$$
\P\big(|R(h)-\hat R_n(h)| \ge t\big)\le 2e^{-2nt^2}.
$$
Union bound over the $N$ hypotheses:
$$
\P\big(\Delta_n \ge t\big) \le 2Ne^{-2nt^2}.
$$
Setting the right side to $\delta$ and solving for $t$ gives $t = \sqrt{\log(2N/\delta)/(2n)}$.
The risk bound follows from the decomposition proposition.
</Proof>

<Assumptions>
Bounded loss, i.i.d. sample, and a hypothesis class **fixed before seeing the data**. The last is the
one most easily violated in practice: any hyperparameter chosen by looking at the test set
effectively enlarges $\Hcal$, and the bound must be inflated accordingly.
</Assumptions>

<Note title="Reading the bound">
$\sqrt{\log N/n}$: the dependence on the class size is *logarithmic*, so doubling the number of
hypotheses costs almost nothing. A class of $2^{10^6}$ hypotheses — the number of functions
representable by a million binary parameters — gives $\sqrt{10^6\log2/n}$, needing $n \sim 10^6$ for a
non-trivial bound. That is the parameter-counting bound, and it is roughly the intuition that
"you need as many examples as parameters".
</Note>

<Theorem id="realizable-case" title="Faster rate in the realizable case">
Suppose some $h^\star\in\Hcal$ has $R(h^\star)=0$ and the loss is the zero-one loss. Then with
probability at least $1-\delta$, any ERM solution $\hat h$ (which necessarily has $\hat R_n(\hat h)=0$)
satisfies
$$
R(\hat h) \le \frac{\log(N/\delta)}{n} .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Call $h$ **bad** if $R(h)>\eps$. A bad $h$ has zero empirical risk only if all $n$ samples are
classified correctly by it, which happens with probability $(1-R(h))^n \le (1-\eps)^n \le e^{-n\eps}$
by independence. Union bounding over the at most $N$ bad hypotheses,
$$
\P\big(\exists \text{ bad } h \text{ with }\hat R_n(h)=0\big) \le Ne^{-n\eps}.
$$
Since $h^\star$ has zero empirical risk, ERM returns some hypothesis with zero empirical risk; on the
complement of the above event, every such hypothesis has risk at most $\eps$. Setting
$Ne^{-n\eps}=\delta$ gives $\eps = \log(N/\delta)/n$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
$1/n$ instead of $1/\sqrt n$ — a genuinely different rate, obtained by replacing Hoeffding's
two-sided bound with the exact one-sided multiplicative bound available when the variance is small.
This is the simplest instance of a **fast rate** under a low-noise condition, and it is the reason
interpolating classifiers are not automatically suspect: when the problem is realizable, fitting the
training data exactly is the right thing to do.
</KeyIdea>

## PAC learnability and the necessity of inductive bias

<Definition id="pac-def" title="PAC learnability">
$\Hcal$ is **agnostically PAC learnable** if there is a function
$n_\Hcal(\eps,\delta)$, polynomial in $1/\eps$ and $1/\delta$, and an algorithm $\mathcal{A}$, such
that for every distribution $\Dcal$ and every $n \ge n_\Hcal(\eps,\delta)$,
$$
\P\Big(R(\mathcal{A}(S)) \le \inf_{h\in\Hcal}R(h)+\eps\Big) \ge 1-\delta .
$$
</Definition>

<Theorem id="no-free-lunch" title="No free lunch">
Let $\Xcal$ be a domain of size $2m$ and consider the zero-one loss with $\Ycal=\{0,1\}$ and
$\Hcal$ the class of **all** functions $\Xcal\to\{0,1\}$. For every algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ using
$n \le m$ samples there is a distribution $\Dcal$ on which
$$
\E_{S\sim\Dcal^n}\big[R(\mathcal{A}(S))\big] \ge \frac14,
$$
while some $h\in\Hcal$ has $R(h)=0$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Let $\Dcal_f$ be, for each of the $2^{2m}$ functions $f : \Xcal\to\{0,1\}$, the distribution that is
uniform on $\Xcal$ in $x$ and deterministic $y = f(x)$. Each $\Dcal_f$ is realizable by $f \in \Hcal$.

Average the algorithm's risk over $f$ chosen uniformly at random. Given a sample $S$ of size
$n \le m$, the algorithm has seen at most $n$ of the $2m$ domain points, so at least $m$ points are
unseen. For an unseen point $x$, the value $f(x)$ is independent of $S$ and uniform on $\{0,1\}$, so
$\mathcal{A}(S)$ predicts it correctly with probability exactly $1/2$. Hence
$$
\E_f\E_S\big[R(\mathcal{A}(S))\big] \ge \P(X \text{ unseen})\cdot\frac12 \ge \frac{m}{2m}\cdot\frac12 = \frac14 .
$$
An average over $f$ of at least $1/4$ means some particular $f$ achieves at least $1/4$, which is the
claim.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Inductive bias is not a regrettable practical compromise; it is a mathematical necessity. Learning is
impossible over the class of all functions, so *every* working learning algorithm encodes
assumptions about which functions are plausible — through the hypothesis class, the regulariser, the
architecture, or the optimiser's implicit preferences. "This model has no assumptions built in" is
always false. The interesting question is never whether there is an inductive bias but what it is,
which is why the implicit-bias results on the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">deep learning theory</TopicRef> page matter so
much.
</KeyIdea>

## From finite to infinite classes

The finite-class bound is useless for $\Hcal$ infinite — every interesting class, including all
linear classifiers. Two routes out, both developed on the
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/vc-dimension-and-rademacher">next page</TopicRef>:

1. **Combinatorial.** Replace $N$ by the number of distinct *labellings* the class can produce on $n$ points, the growth function $\Pi_\Hcal(n)$. Sauer's lemma bounds this by $O(n^{d})$ where $d$ is the VC dimension, so $\log \Pi_\Hcal(n) = O(d\log n)$ takes the place of $\log N$.
2. **Analytic.** Bound $\E\Delta_n$ directly by the Rademacher complexity of the class, which is a scale-sensitive measure that does not require finiteness at all.

The bridge to both is **symmetrisation**, which replaces the unknown population risk by a second
independent sample:

<Lemma id="symmetrisation" title="Symmetrisation">
Let $\mathcal{G}$ be a class of functions $\Xcal\to[0,1]$. Then
$$
\E_S\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\Big(\E g - \frac1n\sum_i g(x_i)\Big)\right]
\le 2\,\E_{S,\eps}\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\eps_i g(x_i)\right],
$$
where $\eps_i$ are independent Rademacher signs.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
Let $S' = (x_1',\dots,x_n')$ be an independent "ghost sample" from the same distribution, so
$\E g = \E_{S'}\frac1n\sum_ig(x_i')$. Then
$$
\E_S\sup_g\Big(\E g - \tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i g(x_i)\Big)
= \E_S\sup_g\ \E_{S'}\Big[\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\big(g(x_i')-g(x_i)\big)\Big]
\le \E_{S,S'}\sup_g \tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\big(g(x_i')-g(x_i)\big),
$$
moving the supremum inside the expectation (Jensen, since $\sup$ is convex).

Now for each $i$ independently, swapping $x_i$ and $x_i'$ leaves the joint distribution unchanged
while flipping the sign of the $i$-th term. Hence for any fixed signs $\eps\in\{\pm1\}^n$,
$$
\E_{S,S'}\sup_g \tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\big(g(x_i')-g(x_i)\big)
= \E_{S,S'}\sup_g \tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\eps_i\big(g(x_i')-g(x_i)\big),
$$
and averaging over uniformly random $\eps$ preserves the equality. Finally split the supremum of the
difference into the two suprema,
$$
\sup_g\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\eps_i\big(g(x_i')-g(x_i)\big)
\le \sup_g\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\eps_ig(x_i') + \sup_g\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i(-\eps_i)g(x_i),
$$
and note both terms have the same expectation by symmetry of $\eps$, giving the factor $2$.
</Proof>

## What uniform convergence cannot do

<Claim kind="empirical" id="uniform-convergence-not-necessary" title="Uniform convergence is not necessary for generalisation">
There are settings — including realistic overparameterised networks — where the trained model
generalises well while the uniform deviation $\Delta_n$ over any hypothesis class containing it is
close to its maximum possible value, making every uniform-convergence bound vacuous.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
Nagarajan and Kolter construct explicit examples where the generalisation gap of the learned
predictor is small but *any* bound derived by uniform convergence over a class containing the
algorithm's possible outputs is near-trivial — even when the class is restricted to exactly the
hypotheses the algorithm can return on samples of that size. The obstruction is not looseness of the
union bound; it is that the two-sided uniform deviation is genuinely large.

What follows and what does not:

- Uniform convergence remains **sufficient**: when the bound is non-vacuous, it is a real guarantee, and for classical model classes it is the right tool.
- It is not **necessary**, so the failure of a uniform bound is not evidence that a model will generalise badly.
- Explaining generalisation in the overparameterised regime therefore requires algorithm-dependent analysis — stability, PAC-Bayes with a data-dependent prior, margin-based bounds, or implicit-bias characterisations — not a better bound on $\Delta_n$.

This is an active research area, and there is no settled answer.
</NotSaid>
