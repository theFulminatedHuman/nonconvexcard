## VC dimension

<Definition id="vc-def" title="Shattering, growth function, VC dimension">
Let $\Hcal\subseteq\{\pm1\}^\Xcal$. For $C = \{x_1,\dots,x_n\}\subseteq\Xcal$ write
$$
\Hcal|_C = \big\{(h(x_1),\dots,h(x_n)) : h\in\Hcal\big\}\subseteq\{\pm1\}^n .
$$
$\Hcal$ **shatters** $C$ if $|\Hcal|_C| = 2^n$. The **growth function** is
$\Pi_\Hcal(n) = \max_{|C|=n}|\Hcal|_C|$, and the **VC dimension** is
$$
\mathrm{VC}(\Hcal) = \sup\{n : \Pi_\Hcal(n)=2^n\} .
$$
</Definition>

<Theorem id="sauer" title="Sauer–Shelah lemma">
If $\mathrm{VC}(\Hcal)=d<\infty$ then for every $n$,
$$
\Pi_\Hcal(n) \le \sum_{k=0}^d\binom nk \le \left(\frac{en}{d}\right)^d \quad (n \ge d).
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
It suffices to show: for any finite $C$ with $|C|=n$ and any set family
$\mathcal{F}\subseteq\{\pm1\}^C$ that shatters no subset of size $d+1$,
$|\mathcal{F}| \le \sum_{k\le d}\binom nk$. We prove the stronger statement

$$
|\mathcal{F}| \le \big|\{B\subseteq C : \mathcal{F}\text{ shatters }B\}\big| ,
$$

from which the lemma follows, since a family shattering no set of size $>d$ can shatter at most
$\sum_{k\le d}\binom nk$ subsets.

Induct on $n$. For $n=0$ both sides are $1$. For $n\ge1$ pick $x\in C$ and set $C' = C\setminus\{x\}$.
Split $\mathcal{F}$ by its behaviour on $x$: let
$$
\mathcal{F}_0 = \{f|_{C'} : f\in\mathcal{F}\},
\qquad
\mathcal{F}_1 = \{f|_{C'} : \text{both extensions of } f|_{C'} \text{ to } x \text{ lie in }\mathcal{F}\}.
$$
Then $|\mathcal{F}| = |\mathcal{F}_0| + |\mathcal{F}_1|$: each element of $\mathcal{F}_0$ accounts for
at least one $f\in\mathcal{F}$, and those restrictions realised twice are counted once more by
$\mathcal{F}_1$.

Apply the inductive hypothesis to both families on $C'$:
$$
|\mathcal{F}_0| \le \#\{B\subseteq C' : \mathcal{F}_0\text{ shatters }B\},
\qquad
|\mathcal{F}_1| \le \#\{B\subseteq C' : \mathcal{F}_1\text{ shatters }B\} .
$$
Now observe two facts. If $\mathcal{F}_0$ shatters $B\subseteq C'$ then so does $\mathcal{F}$
(restriction cannot create labellings). And if $\mathcal{F}_1$ shatters $B\subseteq C'$ then
$\mathcal{F}$ shatters $B\cup\{x\}$ — by definition of $\mathcal{F}_1$, every labelling of $B$
achievable in $\mathcal{F}_1$ is achievable in $\mathcal{F}$ with *both* values at $x$.

The two collections of shattered sets produced this way are disjoint (the second all contain $x$, the
first none do) and both consist of sets shattered by $\mathcal{F}$. Hence
$|\mathcal{F}|\le\#\{B\subseteq C : \mathcal{F}\text{ shatters }B\}$, completing the induction.

For the numerical bound, when $n\ge d$,
$$
\sum_{k=0}^d\binom nk \le \left(\frac nd\right)^d\sum_{k=0}^d\binom nk\left(\frac dn\right)^k
\le \left(\frac nd\right)^d\left(1+\frac dn\right)^n \le \left(\frac nd\right)^de^d ,
$$
using $(d/n)^{k-d}\ge1$ for $k \le d$, the binomial theorem, and $1+u\le e^u$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Sauer's lemma is a **phase transition**: the growth function is exactly $2^n$ up to $n=d$ and then
switches to polynomial $n^d$ forever after. There is no intermediate behaviour. This is what makes VC
theory work: substituting $\log\Pi_\Hcal(n) = O(d\log n)$ for $\log N$ in the
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/erm-and-uniform-convergence">finite-class bound</TopicRef> gives a
useful result, whereas $\log2^n = n$ would give nothing at all.
</KeyIdea>

<Theorem id="vc-bound" title="VC generalisation bound">
Let $\mathrm{VC}(\Hcal)=d$ and let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. With probability at least $1-\delta$,
$$
\sup_{h\in\Hcal}\big|R(h)-\hat R_n(h)\big| \le C\sqrt{\frac{d\log(n/d)+\log(1/\delta)}{n}} .
$$
</Theorem>

The derivation combines symmetrisation, Sauer's lemma applied to the (at most $\Pi_\Hcal(2n)$)
labellings on the doubled sample, and Massart's lemma below; it is carried out in the references and
the structure is exactly the one reproduced for the Rademacher bound in the next section.

<Proposition id="halfspace-vc" title="VC dimension of linear classifiers">
Let $\Hcal = \{x\mapsto\sign(\ip{w}{x}) : w\in\R^d\}$ be the homogeneous halfspaces. Then
$\mathrm{VC}(\Hcal)=d$. With a bias term, $\mathrm{VC}=d+1$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
**Lower bound.** The standard basis $e_1,\dots,e_d$ is shattered: for any target labelling
$y\in\{\pm1\}^d$, take $w = y$, and then $\sign(\ip{w}{e_i}) = \sign(y_i) = y_i$.

**Upper bound.** Let $x_1,\dots,x_{d+1}\in\R^d$ be any $d+1$ points. Being $d+1$ vectors in a
$d$-dimensional space they are linearly dependent: there are coefficients $a_i$, not all zero, with
$\sum_i a_ix_i=0$. Split the index set into $P = \{i : a_i>0\}$ and $N = \{i:a_i<0\}$; at least one is
non-empty, and we may assume both are (if $N=\emptyset$ then $\sum_{i\in P}a_ix_i = 0$ with positive
coefficients, and the argument below runs with $N$ empty and the second sum zero).

Consider the labelling $y_i = +1$ for $i \in P$ and $y_i=-1$ for $i\in N$ (arbitrary elsewhere).
Suppose some $w$ realises it. Then
$$
0 = \ip{w}{\textstyle\sum_i a_ix_i} = \sum_{i\in P}a_i\ip{w}{x_i} + \sum_{i\in N}a_i\ip{w}{x_i}.
$$
Every term in the first sum has $a_i>0$ and $\ip{w}{x_i}>0$, so it is positive; every term in the
second has $a_i<0$ and $\ip{w}{x_i}<0$, so it too is positive. A sum of strictly positive terms
cannot be zero — contradiction. Hence no $d+1$ points are shattered.
</Proof>

<Note title="Parameter counting is a heuristic, not a theorem">
For halfspaces, $\mathrm{VC} = $ number of parameters. That coincidence makes "VC dimension counts
parameters" tempting, and it is false. The one-parameter class
$\{x\mapsto\sign(\sin(\omega x)) : \omega\in\R\}$ has **infinite** VC dimension, and a
$k$-parameter class can have VC dimension anywhere from $0$ to $\infty$. Capacity is about the
richness of the realisable labellings, not the number of knobs.
</Note>

## Rademacher complexity

<Definition id="rademacher-def" title="Rademacher complexity">
For a class $\mathcal{G}$ of functions $\Xcal\to\R$ and a sample $S = (x_1,\dots,x_n)$, the
**empirical Rademacher complexity** is
$$
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{G}) = \E_\eps\left[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\eps_ig(x_i)\right],
$$
with $\eps_i$ i.i.d. Rademacher, and the **Rademacher complexity** is
$\mathfrak{R}_n(\mathcal{G}) = \E_S\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{G})$.
</Definition>

<Intuition>
$\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S$ measures how well the class can correlate with **pure noise** on the given
sample. A class that can fit any random labelling has complexity near its maximum; a class that
cannot has small complexity. It is a direct measure of the capacity to overfit, evaluated on the
actual data — which is why it is scale-sensitive and data-dependent in a way VC dimension is not.
</Intuition>

<Theorem id="rademacher-bound" title="Rademacher generalisation bound">
Let $\mathcal{G}$ be a class of functions $\Xcal\to[0,1]$. With probability at least $1-\delta$ over
the sample, simultaneously for all $g\in\mathcal{G}$,
$$
\E g \le \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ng(x_i) + 2\mathfrak{R}_n(\mathcal{G}) + \sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2n}} .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Let $\Phi(S) = \sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\big(\E g - \frac1n\sum_ig(x_i)\big)$.

**Step 1: concentration.** Changing one sample point $x_i$ changes each empirical average by at most
$1/n$ (since $g\in[0,1]$), and the supremum is $1$-Lipschitz in the uniform norm by the
<TopicRef id="foundations/analysis-prerequisites">supremum inequality</TopicRef>. So $\Phi$ has
bounded differences with $c_i = 1/n$, and
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-inequalities">McDiarmid's inequality</TopicRef>
gives, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
$$
\Phi(S) \le \E\Phi + \sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2n}} .
$$

**Step 2: symmetrisation.** By the
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/erm-and-uniform-convergence">symmetrisation lemma</TopicRef>,
$\E\Phi \le 2\mathfrak{R}_n(\mathcal{G})$.

Combining, and unpacking the definition of $\Phi$ as a supremum (so that the bound holds for every
$g$ simultaneously) gives the statement.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Two ingredients, each from an earlier page: McDiarmid to concentrate the uniform deviation around its
mean, and symmetrisation to bound that mean. The decomposition is worth remembering because it
localises the difficulty: the concentration step is universal and cheap, and *all* the class-specific
work is in bounding $\mathfrak{R}_n$.
</KeyIdea>

<Lemma id="massart" title="Massart's finite lemma">
Let $A\subseteq\R^n$ be finite with $\norm{a}_2\le r$ for all $a\in A$. Then
$$
\E_\eps\left[\max_{a\in A}\ip{\eps}{a}\right] \le r\sqrt{2\log|A|} .
$$
</Lemma>

<Proof>
This is the maximal inequality for sub-Gaussian variables. For each fixed $a$, $\ip{\eps}{a}$ is a
sum of independent Rademacher terms with coefficients $a_i$, hence sub-Gaussian with parameter
$\norm{a}_2\le r$:
$\E e^{\lambda\ip{\eps}{a}} \le e^{\lambda^2r^2/2}$ by
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/sub-gaussian-random-variables">Hoeffding's lemma</TopicRef>
applied coordinatewise.

Then for any $\lambda>0$, by Jensen and the union over $A$,
$$
\exp\left(\lambda\,\E\max_a\ip{\eps}{a}\right)
\le \E\exp\left(\lambda\max_a\ip{\eps}{a}\right)
= \E\max_a e^{\lambda\ip{\eps}{a}}
\le \sum_{a\in A}\E e^{\lambda\ip{\eps}{a}} \le |A|e^{\lambda^2r^2/2}.
$$
Taking logarithms and dividing by $\lambda$,
$$
\E\max_a\ip{\eps}{a} \le \frac{\log|A|}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda r^2}{2},
$$
minimised at $\lambda = \sqrt{2\log|A|}/r$ with value $r\sqrt{2\log|A|}$.
</Proof>

Applying Massart with $A = \Hcal|_S \subseteq\{\pm1\}^n$, so $r = \sqrt n$ and
$|A| \le \Pi_\Hcal(n) \le (en/d)^d$, gives
$$
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\Hcal) \le \frac{\sqrt n\sqrt{2d\log(en/d)}}{n} = \sqrt{\frac{2d\log(en/d)}{n}},
$$
which recovers the VC bound. So the Rademacher route subsumes the combinatorial one.

<Proposition id="linear-rademacher" title="Rademacher complexity of a norm-bounded linear class">
Let $\mathcal{G} = \{x\mapsto\ip{w}{x} : \norm{w}_2\le B\}$ and suppose $\norm{x_i}_2\le R$. Then
$$
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{G}) \le \frac{BR}{\sqrt n} .
$$
The bound does not depend on the dimension of $x$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
$$
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{G})
= \E_\eps\sup_{\norm w_2\le B}\frac1n\sum_i\eps_i\ip{w}{x_i}
= \E_\eps\sup_{\norm w_2\le B}\left\langle w,\ \frac1n\sum_i\eps_ix_i\right\rangle
= \frac Bn\,\E_\eps\norm{\sum_i\eps_ix_i}_2 ,
$$
the last step because $\sup_{\norm w_2\le B}\ip{w}{v} = B\norm{v}_2$. By Jensen (concavity of
$\sqrt{\cdot}$) and independence of the signs,
$$
\E_\eps\norm{\sum_i\eps_ix_i}_2 \le \sqrt{\E_\eps\norm{\sum_i\eps_ix_i}_2^2}
= \sqrt{\sum_{i}\norm{x_i}_2^2} \le R\sqrt n ,
$$
the cross terms vanishing because $\E[\eps_i\eps_j]=0$ for $i\ne j$. Hence
$\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S \le BR\sqrt n/n = BR/\sqrt n$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
This is the single most important capacity bound in the subject, and the reason margin-based methods
work in high dimension. The complexity is controlled by $\norm{w}_2\norm{x}_2$, **not** by the
dimension. So a linear classifier in $\R^{10^6}$ with small norm is a low-capacity object, while the
VC dimension of the unconstrained class is $10^6$. The <TopicRef id="machine-learning/support-vector-machines">SVM</TopicRef>
margin bound is this proposition plus the contraction lemma, and the same mechanism is what
norm-based bounds for neural networks attempt to exploit.
</KeyIdea>

<Lemma id="contraction" title="Talagrand contraction">
If $\phi:\R\to\R$ is $K$-Lipschitz then
$\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\phi\circ\mathcal{G}) \le K\,\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{G})$.
</Lemma>

Quoted from Ledoux–Talagrand via the references. It is the tool that converts a bound on the
complexity of the *predictor* class into a bound on the complexity of the *loss* class: the hinge
loss is $1$-Lipschitz, the logistic loss is $1$-Lipschitz, and a ramp surrogate for the zero-one
loss at margin $\gamma$ is $1/\gamma$-Lipschitz — whence the $1/\gamma$ in every margin bound.

## Two other routes to generalisation

<Definition id="stability-def" title="Uniform stability">
An algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ is **$\beta$-uniformly stable** if for every pair of samples $S, S'$
differing in one point, and every $(x,y)$,
$$
\big|\ell(\mathcal{A}(S)(x),y) - \ell(\mathcal{A}(S')(x),y)\big| \le \beta .
$$
Bousquet and Elisseeff show that such an algorithm satisfies
$\E[R(\mathcal{A}(S)) - \hat R_n(\mathcal{A}(S))]\le\beta$, and that regularised ERM with a
$\lambda$-strongly convex regulariser is $O(1/(\lambda n))$-stable.
</Definition>

<Definition id="pac-bayes-def" title="PAC-Bayes bound">
For a prior $P$ over hypotheses fixed before seeing the data and any posterior $Q$ (possibly
data-dependent), with probability at least $1-\delta$,
$$
\E_{h\sim Q}R(h) \le \E_{h\sim Q}\hat R_n(h) + \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q\,\|\,P) + \log(2\sqrt n/\delta)}{2n}} .
$$
</Definition>

Both are **algorithm-dependent**, which is what makes them candidates for explaining deep learning
where uniform convergence fails: stability speaks about what the optimiser does, and PAC-Bayes
charges only for the information the posterior adds to the prior. Neither has yet produced a
non-vacuous bound for a state-of-the-art network trained on a standard benchmark, which is the
honest state of the art.
