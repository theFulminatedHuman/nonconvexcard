Data $(x_i,y_i)_{i=1}^n$ with $x_i\in\R^d$ and $y_i\in\{\pm1\}$. A hyperplane is
$\{x : \ip{w}{x}+b = 0\}$, and the classifier is $x\mapsto\sign(\ip{w}{x}+b)$.

## The geometric margin

<Proposition id="margin-formula" title="The geometric margin">
The Euclidean distance from $x_0$ to the hyperplane $\{\ip wx+b=0\}$ is
$$
\frac{|\ip{w}{x_0}+b|}{\norm w_2},
$$
and for a correctly classified point the **signed** distance is
$\dfrac{y_0(\ip{w}{x_0}+b)}{\norm{w}_2}$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The distance is $\min\{\norm{x_0-x}_2 : \ip wx+b=0\}$. Writing $x = x_0+v$, the constraint becomes
$\ip wv = -(\ip w{x_0}+b)$, and we minimise $\norm v_2$ subject to that single linear equation. By
Cauchy–Schwarz, $|\ip wv| \le \norm w_2\norm v_2$, so
$\norm v_2 \ge |\ip w{x_0}+b|/\norm w_2$, with equality when $v$ is parallel to $w$ — which is
achievable. The signed version follows by multiplying through by $y_0 \in \{\pm1\}$.
</Proof>

<Intuition>
The quantity $y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b)$ is the **functional margin**; dividing by $\norm w_2$ makes it the
**geometric margin**, a distance in the input space. The functional margin can be inflated by scaling
$(w,b)$, which changes no decision; the geometric margin cannot. Any sensible objective must depend
only on the latter, and the canonical normalisation below is how that is arranged.
</Intuition>

## Hard margin

We want the hyperplane that maximises the distance to the closest point:
$$
\max_{w,b}\ \min_i \frac{y_i(\ip{w}{x_i}+b)}{\norm w_2}.
$$

<Proposition id="hard-margin-primal" title="The hard-margin primal">
Assume the data is linearly separable. The margin-maximisation problem is equivalent to
$$
\boxed{\ \min_{w,b}\ \tfrac12\norm w_2^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(\ip{w}{x_i}+b)\ge1,\ i=1,\dots,n\ }
$$
which is a strictly convex quadratic program in $w$ (convex, not strict, in $(w,b)$ jointly).
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The objective $\min_i y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b)/\norm w_2$ is invariant under $(w,b)\mapsto(cw,cb)$ for
$c>0$, so we may fix the scale. Choose the **canonical normalisation**
$\min_i y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b) = 1$, which is possible because the data is separable (so the minimum is
positive and can be scaled to $1$). Under that normalisation the geometric margin is exactly
$1/\norm w_2$, so maximising it is minimising $\norm w_2$, equivalently $\tfrac12\norm w_2^2$.

Replacing the equality $\min_i(\cdot)=1$ by the inequalities $y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b)\ge1$ does not change
the optimum: at a minimiser of $\norm w_2$ subject to the inequalities, at least one constraint must
be active, since otherwise all constraints hold with slack and $(w,b)$ could be scaled down by a
factor $c<1$, reducing the objective.

Convexity: the objective is a convex quadratic and the constraints are affine. Strict convexity in
$w$ follows from $\nabla_w^2 = I \succ 0$; there is no curvature in $b$, so the pair $(w,b)$ is not a
strictly convex problem, but the minimiser is still unique for separable data because the active
constraints pin down $b$.
</Proof>

### The Lagrangian and the dual

<Theorem id="hard-margin-dual" title="The hard-margin SVM dual">
With multipliers $\alpha\in\R^n_{\ge0}$ for the $n$ constraints, the dual problem is
$$
\boxed{\
\max_{\alpha\ge0}\ \ \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\ip{x_i}{x_j}
\quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_iy_i = 0 \ }
$$
and strong duality holds. The primal solution is recovered as
$$
w^\star = \sum_i\alpha_i^\star y_ix_i,
\qquad
b^\star = y_k - \ip{w^\star}{x_k}\ \text{ for any } k \text{ with } \alpha_k^\star>0 .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
**Step 1: the Lagrangian.** Write the constraints as $1 - y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b)\le0$:
$$
\mathcal{L}(w,b,\alpha) = \frac12\norm w_2^2 + \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i\Big(1 - y_i\big(\ip{w}{x_i}+b\big)\Big),
\qquad \alpha\ge0 .
$$

**Step 2: minimise over $w$.** $\mathcal{L}$ is strictly convex in $w$, so set the gradient to zero:
$$
\nabla_w\mathcal{L} = w - \sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
w = \sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i .
$$
This is the **first stationarity condition**, and it already carries the central structural fact: the
optimal $w$ is a linear combination of the training inputs, with coefficients $\alpha_iy_i$.

**Step 3: minimise over $b$.** $\mathcal{L}$ is *affine* in $b$ with coefficient
$-\sum_i\alpha_iy_i$. An affine function has infimum $-\infty$ unless its coefficient vanishes. Hence
$$
g(\alpha) = \inf_{w,b}\mathcal{L} = -\infty \quad\text{unless}\quad \sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0 .
$$
This is where the dual **equality constraint comes from**: it is not imposed, it is the condition for
the dual function to be finite.

**Step 4: substitute back.** On the set where $\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0$, substituting
$w = \sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i$ gives
$$
\frac12\norm w_2^2 = \frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\ip{x_i}{x_j},
\qquad
\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\ip{w}{x_i} = \sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\ip{x_i}{x_j},
$$
and the $b$ term vanishes by the equality constraint. Therefore
$$
g(\alpha) = \frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\ip{x_i}{x_j} + \sum_i\alpha_i - \sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\ip{x_i}{x_j}
= \sum_i\alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\ip{x_i}{x_j} .
$$

**Step 5: strong duality.** The primal is convex with affine constraints, and for separable data
there is a strictly feasible point (scale up any separating $(w,b)$ so that all margins exceed $1$),
so <TopicRef id="advanced-optimization/duality-and-kkt">Slater's condition</TopicRef> applies and
$d^\star=p^\star$ with the dual optimum attained.

**Step 6: recover $b$.** For any $k$ with $\alpha_k^\star>0$, complementary slackness gives
$y_k(\ip{w^\star}{x_k}+b^\star)=1$, hence $b^\star = y_k^{-1} - \ip{w^\star}{x_k} = y_k - \ip{w^\star}{x_k}$
using $y_k^{-1}=y_k$ for $y_k\in\{\pm1\}$. In practice one averages over all such $k$ for numerical
stability.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Three things happened in that derivation and all three matter.

1. **The dual involves the data only through the inner products $\ip{x_i}{x_j}$.** Replace them with $K(x_i,x_j)$ and the entire derivation goes through verbatim. That is the kernel trick, and it is available *because* the dual was derived, not as a separate idea.
2. **The dual has $n$ variables and the primal has $d+1$.** For $d\gg n$ — the kernel case, where $d$ may be infinite — the dual is the tractable one.
3. **The equality constraint is an artefact of the unregularised bias.** Formulations without a bias term, or with $b$ inside the penalty, have no such constraint. It is worth knowing which convention a piece of software uses.
</KeyIdea>

<Corollary id="support-vectors" title="Support vectors">
At the optimum, $\alpha_i^\star>0$ only for points with $y_i(\ip{w^\star}{x_i}+b^\star)=1$ — the points
lying exactly on the margin. Consequently
$$
w^\star = \sum_{i\in\mathrm{SV}}\alpha_i^\star y_ix_i, \qquad \mathrm{SV} = \{i : \alpha_i^\star>0\},
$$
and deleting any non-support vector from the training set does not change the solution.
</Corollary>

<Proof>
Complementary slackness gives $\alpha_i^\star\big(1-y_i(\ip{w^\star}{x_i}+b^\star)\big)=0$, so
$\alpha_i^\star>0$ forces the constraint to be active. For the deletion claim: remove a point $j$ with
$\alpha_j^\star=0$. The pair $(w^\star,b^\star)$ is still feasible for the reduced problem, and the
reduced primal optimum cannot be larger than the full one (fewer constraints), so
$(w^\star,b^\star)$, together with $\alpha^\star$ restricted to the remaining indices, satisfies the
KKT conditions of the reduced problem — which are sufficient by convexity.
</Proof>

## Soft margin

Real data is not separable, so relax the constraints with slacks $\xi_i \ge 0$:
$$
\min_{w,b,\xi}\ \frac12\norm w_2^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b)\ge 1-\xi_i,\qquad \xi_i\ge0 .
$$

<Theorem id="soft-margin-dual" title="The soft-margin SVM dual">
The dual is
$$
\boxed{\
\max_{\alpha}\ \sum_i\alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\ip{x_i}{x_j}
\quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le\alpha_i\le C,\quad \sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0 \ }
$$
— identical to the hard-margin dual except that the multipliers are **boxed above by $C$**. The slack
variables do not appear.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
**Step 1: Lagrangian with two families of multipliers**, $\alpha_i\ge0$ for the margin constraints and
$\mu_i\ge0$ for $\xi_i\ge0$:
$$
\mathcal{L}(w,b,\xi,\alpha,\mu)
= \frac12\norm w_2^2 + C\sum_i\xi_i + \sum_i\alpha_i\Big(1-\xi_i - y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b)\Big) - \sum_i\mu_i\xi_i .
$$

**Step 2: stationarity in $w$ and $b$.** Exactly as before,
$$
\nabla_w\mathcal{L}=0 \Rightarrow w = \sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i,
\qquad
\partial_b\mathcal{L}=0 \Rightarrow \sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0 .
$$

**Step 3: stationarity in $\xi$.** $\mathcal{L}$ is affine in each $\xi_i$ with coefficient
$C - \alpha_i - \mu_i$. Again an affine function is unbounded below unless the coefficient vanishes:
$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\xi_i} = C - \alpha_i - \mu_i = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mu_i = C - \alpha_i .
$$
Combined with $\mu_i\ge0$ this gives $\alpha_i\le C$ — **the box constraint, derived rather than
imposed**. And substituting $\mu_i = C-\alpha_i$ makes all $\xi$ terms cancel:
$$
C\xi_i - \alpha_i\xi_i - \mu_i\xi_i = \xi_i(C - \alpha_i - \mu_i) = 0 .
$$

**Step 4: substitute.** What remains is exactly the hard-margin computation of Step 4 above, so
$$
g(\alpha) = \sum_i\alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\ip{x_i}{x_j},
$$
now on the domain $\{0\le\alpha\le C,\ \sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0\}$.

**Step 5: strong duality.** The problem is convex with affine constraints and is always strictly
feasible: take any $(w,b)$ and $\xi_i$ large enough. So Slater applies with no separability
assumption, and $d^\star = p^\star$.
</Proof>

<Proposition id="kkt-classification" title="The three regimes of a training point">
At the optimum, exactly one of the following holds for each $i$:

| $\alpha_i^\star$ | $\xi_i^\star$ | Margin $y_i(\ip{w^\star}{x_i}+b^\star)$ | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $0$ | $\ge1$ | interior, correctly classified with room to spare |
| $(0,C)$ | $0$ | $=1$ | on the margin — a *margin* support vector |
| $C$ | $\ge0$ | $\le1$ | inside the margin or misclassified — a *bound* support vector |
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The KKT conditions are: $\alpha_i(1-\xi_i-y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b))=0$, $\mu_i\xi_i=0$, and
$\mu_i = C-\alpha_i$.

If $\alpha_i=0$ then $\mu_i = C>0$, so $\xi_i=0$, and the margin constraint gives
$y_i(\cdot)\ge1-\xi_i = 1$.

If $0<\alpha_i<C$ then $\mu_i = C-\alpha_i>0$, so $\xi_i=0$; and $\alpha_i>0$ activates the margin
constraint, giving $y_i(\cdot) = 1-\xi_i = 1$.

If $\alpha_i=C$ then $\mu_i=0$ and $\xi_i$ is unconstrained by that condition; $\alpha_i>0$ activates
the margin constraint, giving $y_i(\cdot) = 1-\xi_i \le 1$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
This table is the whole behaviour of the algorithm in one place, and every entry was *derived* from
the KKT conditions rather than asserted. Note that $C$ has a clean interpretation: it is the cap on
how much influence a single point may have. Small $C$ means no point can pull the boundary far — a
wide, soft margin tolerant of violations; large $C$ approaches the hard-margin problem. It is the
inverse of a regularisation strength, as the next proposition makes explicit.
</KeyIdea>

<Proposition id="hinge-equivalence" title="The soft-margin SVM is regularised hinge-loss minimisation">
The soft-margin primal is equivalent to the unconstrained convex problem
$$
\min_{w,b}\ \frac{1}{2}\norm{w}_2^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^n\big[1 - y_i(\ip{w}{x_i}+b)\big]_+,
\qquad [u]_+ = \max(u,0) .
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
For fixed $(w,b)$, minimise over $\xi$. The constraints are
$\xi_i \ge 1 - y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b)$ and $\xi_i\ge0$, i.e.
$\xi_i \ge [1-y_i(\ip w{x_i}+b)]_+$, and the objective is increasing in each $\xi_i$. So the optimal
$\xi_i$ is exactly that lower bound, and substituting gives the stated objective.
</Proof>

<Note title="Why this matters">
The equivalence reveals the SVM as one member of a family: replace the hinge by the logistic loss and
you get <TopicRef id="machine-learning/logistic-regression">regularised logistic regression</TopicRef>;
replace it by the squared loss and you get <TopicRef id="machine-learning/linear-and-ridge-regression">ridge
regression</TopicRef>. What distinguishes the hinge is that it is **exactly zero** for margins above
$1$, which is what produces sparsity in $\alpha$ — the same non-smoothness-creates-sparsity mechanism
as $\ell^1$ versus $\ell^2$ regularisation.
</Note>

## The kernel SVM

Since the dual depends on the data only through $\ip{x_i}{x_j}$, substitute any
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/kernel-methods-and-rkhs">positive definite kernel</TopicRef>:
$$
\max_{0\le\alpha\le C,\ \alpha^\top y=0}\ \sum_i\alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jK(x_i,x_j),
$$
with decision function
$$
f(x) = \sum_{i\in\mathrm{SV}}\alpha_i^\star y_iK(x_i,x) + b^\star .
$$
Positive definiteness of $K$ is exactly what makes the dual objective concave (the matrix
$Q_{ij} = y_iy_jK(x_i,x_j)$ is PSD, being $\diag(y)\mathbf{K}\diag(y)$), so the dual remains a convex
program. Note also that $f$ is precisely the form the
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/kernel-methods-and-rkhs">representer theorem</TopicRef> predicts, with
the additional information that most coefficients are zero.

## The margin generalisation bound

<Theorem id="margin-bound" title="Margin-based generalisation bound">
Let $\norm{x_i}_2\le R$ and consider the class $\Fcal_B = \{x\mapsto\ip wx : \norm w_2\le B\}$. Fix
$\gamma>0$. With probability at least $1-\delta$, simultaneously for all $f\in\Fcal_B$,
$$
\P\big(Y f(X) \le 0\big)
\;\le\;
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\one\big\{y_if(x_i)\le\gamma\big\}
+ \frac{2BR}{\gamma\sqrt n} + \sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2n}} .
$$
**No dependence on the dimension $d$.**
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Let $\phi_\gamma(u) = \min\big(1,\max(0, 1 - u/\gamma)\big)$ be the ramp surrogate: it is
$1/\gamma$-Lipschitz, equals $1$ for $u\le0$, equals $0$ for $u\ge\gamma$, and satisfies
$$
\one\{u\le0\} \le \phi_\gamma(u) \le \one\{u\le\gamma\}.
$$

By the <TopicRef id="learning-theory/vc-dimension-and-rademacher">Rademacher bound</TopicRef> applied
to the class $\{(x,y)\mapsto\phi_\gamma(yf(x)) : f\in\Fcal_B\}$,
$$
\E\phi_\gamma(Yf(X)) \le \frac1n\sum_i\phi_\gamma(y_if(x_i)) + 2\mathfrak{R}_n(\phi_\gamma\circ\Fcal_B) + \sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2n}} .
$$
By <TopicRef id="learning-theory/vc-dimension-and-rademacher">Talagrand's contraction lemma</TopicRef>,
$\mathfrak{R}_n(\phi_\gamma\circ\Fcal_B)\le\frac1\gamma\mathfrak{R}_n(\Fcal_B)$ (the sign $y_i$ can be
absorbed into the Rademacher variables, since $y_i\eps_i$ is again a Rademacher sequence). And by the
linear-class proposition, $\mathfrak{R}_n(\Fcal_B)\le BR/\sqrt n$.

Finally use the two-sided sandwich on $\phi_\gamma$: the left-hand side is at least
$\P(Yf(X)\le0)$ and the empirical term is at most the empirical $\gamma$-margin error.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Read the bound. It trades **empirical margin error** against $BR/(\gamma\sqrt n)$. Maximising the
margin $\gamma$ at fixed $\norm w_2 \le B$ — which is exactly what the SVM primal does — directly
minimises the bound's second term. So the algorithm is not a heuristic that happens to have a bound
attached; the bound is the justification for the objective.

And the absence of $d$ is the point. A linear classifier in infinite dimension (the kernel case) can
still generalise, provided the margin is large relative to $BR$. This is the precise reason the
kernel trick is not automatically a recipe for overfitting.
</KeyIdea>

<NotSaid>
The bound has real limitations. $\gamma$ must be fixed **before** seeing the data, or a union bound
over a grid of $\gamma$ values must be paid. $B$ and $R$ are typically unknown and must be estimated
or bounded a priori. And the bound is usually numerically loose: for realistic $n$, $B$, $R$ and
$\gamma$, the term $2BR/(\gamma\sqrt n)$ often exceeds $1$, making the statement vacuous even when the
classifier performs well. It explains the *shape* of the right objective without certifying the
performance of a particular trained model — which is the standard situation in
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/erm-and-uniform-convergence">learning theory</TopicRef>.
</NotSaid>

## Solving the dual

<Algorithm title="SMO — sequential minimal optimisation">
<ol>
<li><strong>Observation.</strong> The equality constraint $\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0$ means no single $\alpha_i$ can be changed alone; two must move together.</li>
<li><strong>Select</strong> a pair $(i,j)$ violating the KKT conditions most severely.</li>
<li><strong>Solve exactly</strong> the two-variable subproblem: with $\alpha_iy_i+\alpha_jy_j$ held fixed, the objective is a one-dimensional concave quadratic on an interval, so it has a closed-form maximiser followed by clipping to the box $[0,C]$.</li>
<li><strong>Update</strong> $b$ from the KKT conditions of the changed pair.</li>
<li><strong>Repeat</strong> until the KKT conditions hold to tolerance.</li>
</ol>
</Algorithm>

Each step is $O(n)$ (to update the cached decision values) and closed-form, which is why SMO made
kernel SVMs practical. There is no need for a general QP solver.

<Claim kind="empirical" id="svm-scaling" title="Why SVMs are no longer the default">
SVMs dominated applied machine learning from roughly 1995 to 2012 and are now rare in large-scale
settings.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The reasons are computational, not theoretical:

- The kernel matrix is $O(n^2)$ to store and the exact solve is $O(n^3)$; SMO reduces the constant but not the asymptotics. At $n = 10^7$ this is impossible.
- Prediction costs $O(|\mathrm{SV}|)$ kernel evaluations, and the number of support vectors typically grows linearly with $n$ on noisy problems, so inference does not amortise.
- Multi-class requires one-versus-rest or one-versus-one reductions rather than a native formulation.
- The kernel must be chosen; learning the representation from data, which is what deep networks do, has proved more effective on perceptual tasks.

What has *not* aged is the mathematics. The margin theory, the duality derivation and the
representer-theorem structure are all still correct and still the cleanest example in the subject of
an algorithm derived from a generalisation bound. Linear SVMs, trained by primal methods on the hinge
loss, remain competitive and widely used for high-dimensional sparse data.
</NotSaid>
