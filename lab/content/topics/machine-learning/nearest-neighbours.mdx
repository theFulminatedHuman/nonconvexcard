## The rule and its asymptotics

Given $(x_i,y_i)_{i=1}^n$ and a query $x$, order the training points by distance to $x$ and average
the labels of the $k$ closest.

<Theorem id="cover-hart" title="Cover–Hart inequality">
For the zero-one loss with $\Ycal=\{0,1\}$, the asymptotic risk $R_{1\text{NN}}$ of the
$1$-nearest-neighbour rule satisfies
$$
R^\star \le R_{1\text{NN}} \le 2R^\star\big(1 - R^\star\big) \le 2R^\star ,
$$
where $R^\star$ is the Bayes risk.
</Theorem>

<Theorem id="knn-consistency" title="Universal consistency of k-NN (Stone)">
If $k = k_n \to\infty$ and $k_n/n\to0$, then the $k$-NN rule is **universally consistent**: its risk
converges to the Bayes risk for every distribution on $\R^d\times\{0,1\}$, with no smoothness or
density assumptions whatsoever.
</Theorem>

Both are quoted from the references. Stone's theorem is a remarkable statement — there is a fully
distribution-free learning rule — and it is worth pausing on why it does not contradict
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/erm-and-uniform-convergence">no free lunch</TopicRef>. The resolution is
that consistency is an **asymptotic** statement with no rate: for every distribution the risk
eventually converges, but the sample size at which it becomes small depends on the distribution, and
no free lunch says that dependence cannot be bounded uniformly.

<Theorem id="knn-rate" title="Nonparametric rate for k-NN regression">
Let $y = f(x)+\varepsilon$ with $f$ being $\Lambda$-Lipschitz on $[0,1]^d$, $\Var(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2$,
and $x$ having a density bounded above and below on $[0,1]^d$. Then the $k$-NN regression estimate
satisfies, at a fixed interior point,
$$
\E\big(\hat f(x)-f(x)\big)^2 \;\lesssim\; \underbrace{\Lambda^2\left(\frac kn\right)^{2/d}}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\frac{\sigma^2}{k}}_{\text{variance}},
$$
which is minimised at $k \asymp n^{2/(d+2)}$, giving
$$
\E\big(\hat f(x)-f(x)\big)^2 \;\lesssim\; n^{-2/(d+2)} .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
**Variance.** $\hat f(x) = \frac1k\sum_{j=1}^ky_{(j)}$ averages $k$ labels, whose noise terms are
independent with variance $\sigma^2$, giving a variance contribution $\sigma^2/k$.

**Bias.** The bias is at most $\Lambda$ times the average distance to the $k$-th nearest neighbour.
Because the density is bounded below by some $c>0$, a ball of radius $r$ around $x$ has probability
mass at least $c\,v_dr^d$ where $v_d$ is the volume of the unit ball. For the ball to contain
about $k$ of the $n$ points we need $c\,v_dr^dn \asymp k$, i.e.
$$
r_k \asymp \left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{1/d}.
$$
(Formally: $\P(\norm{X-x}_2 > r) \le 1 - cv_dr^d$, so the probability that fewer than $k$ of $n$
points land in the ball of radius $r_k$ with $cv_dr_k^dn = 2k$ is exponentially small by
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-inequalities">Chernoff</TopicRef>.) Lipschitzness
then gives $|\E\hat f(x)-f(x)| \lesssim \Lambda r_k \asymp \Lambda(k/n)^{1/d}$.

**Balance.** Adding bias$^2$ and variance and optimising: differentiating
$\Lambda^2(k/n)^{2/d} + \sigma^2/k$ in $k$ and setting to zero gives
$k \asymp (\sigma^2/\Lambda^2)^{d/(d+2)}n^{2/(d+2)}$, at which both terms are of order
$n^{-2/(d+2)}$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The exponent $2/(d+2)$ is the curse of dimensionality written as a number. To reach accuracy $\eps$
one needs $n \asymp \eps^{-(d+2)/2}$ samples — **exponential in $d$**. At $d = 10$ and $\eps = 0.1$
that is $10^6$ samples; at $d=100$ it is $10^{51}$. And this rate is *minimax optimal* over the
Lipschitz class, so no method does better: the problem is hard, not the algorithm.

Contrast this with the $O(p/n)$ sample complexity of
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-statistics/covariance-estimation">covariance estimation</TopicRef>. Both
are called "the curse of dimensionality" and they differ by an exponential. The difference is
parametric versus nonparametric: assuming a parametric form is what converts $\eps^{-d}$ into
$d/\eps^2$.
</KeyIdea>

## Why distances stop being informative

<Proposition id="distance-concentration" title="Distance contrast vanishes">
Let $x, x_1,\dots,x_n$ be i.i.d. with independent coordinates of unit variance and bounded
$\psi_2$ norm in $\R^d$. Then
$$
\frac{\max_i\norm{x_i-x}_2}{\min_i\norm{x_i-x}_2} \xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{\P} 1
$$
for fixed $n$, and more quantitatively, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
$$
\max_i\norm{x_i - x}_2 - \min_i\norm{x_i-x}_2 \;\lesssim\; \sqrt{\log(n/\delta)} ,
$$
while both are of order $\sqrt{2d}$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
By the <TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-of-the-norm">norm concentration
theorem</TopicRef> applied to $x_i - x$, whose coordinates are independent with variance $2$,
$$
\P\Big(\big|\norm{x_i-x}_2 - \sqrt{2d}\big| \ge t\Big) \le 2e^{-ct^2}
$$
with $c$ depending only on the $\psi_2$ bound. Union bounding over the $n$ points and setting
$2ne^{-ct^2}=\delta$ gives $t \asymp\sqrt{\log(n/\delta)}$: with probability $1-\delta$ **all** $n$
distances lie in the window $\sqrt{2d}\pm t$. Hence the max and the min differ by at most $2t$, and
their ratio is at most $(\sqrt{2d}+t)/(\sqrt{2d}-t)\to1$ as $d\to\infty$ with $n$ fixed.
</Proof>

<NotSaid>
Read the hypotheses. The coordinates are assumed **independent and identically scaled**. That is the
worst case for nearest neighbours and it is not what real data looks like. The proposition says: if
your data is isotropic noise, distances carry no information. It does **not** say nearest-neighbour
search is meaningless on real high-dimensional data, and interpreting it that way is a misreading that
was common for years after Beyer et al. raised the question.
</NotSaid>

<Claim kind="empirical" id="intrinsic-dimension" title="Nearest neighbours work better than the theory predicts">
$k$-NN and approximate nearest-neighbour search perform far better on real high-dimensional data —
image embeddings, text embeddings, audio features — than the $n^{-2/(d+2)}$ rate with $d$ the ambient
dimension would allow.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The standard explanation is that the data concentrates near a low-dimensional structure, so the
relevant exponent involves the **intrinsic** dimension $d_0 \ll d$ rather than the ambient one. There
are theorems supporting this: under a manifold assumption the $k$-NN rate becomes
$n^{-2/(d_0+2)}$, and doubling-dimension conditions give similar results.

But this is not a complete explanation of practice, for two reasons. Estimating $d_0$ for real data
is itself hard and the estimates are unstable. And the manifold hypothesis is an assumption about
data that is rarely tested in the settings where it is invoked. The honest position: the gap between
the isotropic worst case and observed performance is real, low intrinsic dimension is the best
available explanation, and it has not been verified quantitatively for the embeddings used in
production <TopicRef id="rag/vector-search-and-ann">retrieval systems</TopicRef>.
</NotSaid>
