## Least squares

Given $X\in\R^{n\times p}$ with $\rank X = p$ and $y\in\R^n$, minimise
$L(\beta) = \tfrac12\norm{y - X\beta}_2^2$.

<Theorem id="normal-equations" title="Normal equations and the projection interpretation">
$L$ is minimised uniquely at
$$
\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y,
$$
characterised by the **normal equations** $X^\top(y - X\hat\beta)=0$. The fitted values are
$\hat y = X\hat\beta = Hy$ with $H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ the orthogonal projector onto
$\operatorname{col}(X)$, and the residual $y-\hat y$ is orthogonal to every column of $X$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
$L$ is convex ($\nabla^2L = X^\top X\succeq0$) and differentiable, with
$$
\nabla L(\beta) = X^\top(X\beta - y).
$$
Setting this to zero gives the normal equations. Since $\rank X = p$, $X^\top X$ is invertible
($v^\top X^\top Xv = \norm{Xv}_2^2 = 0$ forces $v=0$), so the stationary point is unique and, by
convexity, is the global minimiser.

For the projection claim, note $H^\top = H$ and
$H^2 = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top = H$, so $H$ is an orthogonal projector by the
characterisation in the <TopicRef id="foundations/linear-algebra">projection theorem</TopicRef>. Its
range is $\operatorname{col}(X)$, and the normal equations say exactly that $y-\hat y \perp
\operatorname{col}(X)$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Least squares is projection; no calculus is needed. The normal equations are the statement
"the residual is orthogonal to the model space", which is the
<TopicRef id="foundations/linear-algebra">projection theorem</TopicRef>. Everything else — the
Pythagorean decomposition of the sum of squares, the $R^2$ statistic, the geometry of ANOVA — follows
from the same picture.
</KeyIdea>

<Theorem id="gauss-markov" title="Gauss–Markov theorem">
Suppose $y = X\beta^\star+\varepsilon$ with $\E\varepsilon = 0$ and $\Cov(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2I_n$.
Then $\hat\beta$ is unbiased, $\Cov(\hat\beta) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$, and for every other
estimator of the form $\tilde\beta = Ay$ that is unbiased for all $\beta^\star$,
$$
\Cov(\tilde\beta) \succeq \Cov(\hat\beta) .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Unbiasedness: $\E\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X\beta^\star = \beta^\star$. Covariance:
$\hat\beta - \beta^\star = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon$, so
$\Cov(\hat\beta) = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(\sigma^2I)X(X^\top X)^{-1} = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$.

Let $\tilde\beta = Ay$ be unbiased for every $\beta^\star$: $\E Ay = AX\beta^\star = \beta^\star$ for
all $\beta^\star$ forces $AX = I_p$. Write $A = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D$; then $AX = I_p$ requires
$DX = 0$. Now
$$
\Cov(\tilde\beta) = \sigma^2AA^\top
= \sigma^2\Big[(X^\top X)^{-1} + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top D^\top + DX(X^\top X)^{-1} + DD^\top\Big] .
$$
The two middle terms vanish because $DX = 0$ (and its transpose), leaving
$\Cov(\tilde\beta) = \Cov(\hat\beta) + \sigma^2DD^\top \succeq \Cov(\hat\beta)$, since $DD^\top\succeq0$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="What Gauss–Markov does not say">
It says least squares is best among **linear unbiased** estimators. Both restrictions are severe. Drop
unbiasedness and ridge does better in mean squared error, always — the next theorem proves it. Drop
linearity and, for non-Gaussian noise, nonlinear estimators can beat it. Quoting Gauss–Markov as a
reason not to regularise is a misreading.
</Warning>

## Ridge regression

$$
\hat\beta_\lambda = \argmin_\beta\left\{\frac{1}{2}\norm{y-X\beta}_2^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\norm\beta_2^2\right\}.
$$

<Proposition id="ridge-closed-form" title="Ridge in closed form and in the SVD basis">
$$
\hat\beta_\lambda = (X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y ,
$$
and if $X = U\Sigma V^\top$ is the SVD with singular values $\sigma_j$, then
$$
\hat\beta_\lambda = \sum_{j}\frac{\sigma_j}{\sigma_j^2+\lambda}\,\ip{u_j}{y}\,v_j,
\qquad
\hat y_\lambda = \sum_j \frac{\sigma_j^2}{\sigma_j^2+\lambda}\ip{u_j}{y}u_j .
$$
The solution exists and is unique for every $\lambda>0$, even when $\rank X<p$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The objective is $\lambda$-strongly convex, so it has a unique minimiser, found by setting the
gradient $X^\top(X\beta-y)+\lambda\beta$ to zero. Substituting $X = U\Sigma V^\top$ and using
$V^\top V = I$,
$$
X^\top X + \lambda I = V(\Sigma^\top\Sigma+\lambda I)V^\top,
\qquad
(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top = V(\Sigma^\top\Sigma+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma^\top U^\top,
$$
and reading off the $j$-th component gives the stated coefficients.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The shrinkage factor $\sigma_j^2/(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)$ is near $1$ for $\sigma_j^2\gg\lambda$ and near
$0$ for $\sigma_j^2\ll\lambda$. Ridge leaves the well-determined directions alone and suppresses the
poorly-determined ones — precisely the directions where, by
<TopicRef id="random-matrix-theory/marchenko-pastur">Marchenko–Pastur</TopicRef>, the sample
covariance is least trustworthy. The effective number of parameters is
$\sum_j\sigma_j^2/(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)$, continuously interpolating between $p$ at $\lambda=0$ and $0$
as $\lambda\to\infty$.
</KeyIdea>

<Theorem id="ridge-beats-ols" title="There is always a ridge parameter that beats least squares">
Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions with $\rank X = p$, define
$m(\lambda) = \E\norm{\hat\beta_\lambda - \beta^\star}_2^2$. Then $m$ is differentiable at $0$ with
$$
m'(0) = -2\sigma^2\tr\big((X^\top X)^{-2}\big) < 0 ,
$$
so $m(\lambda)<m(0)$ for all sufficiently small $\lambda>0$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Work in the SVD basis, where the problem decouples. Write $z = U^\top y$, $\theta = V^\top\beta^\star$,
so $z_j = \sigma_j\theta_j + \tilde\varepsilon_j$ with $\tilde\varepsilon = U^\top\varepsilon$ having
$\E\tilde\varepsilon=0$, $\Cov(\tilde\varepsilon)=\sigma^2I$. The ridge estimator's $j$-th component is
$\hat\theta_j = \frac{\sigma_j}{\sigma_j^2+\lambda}z_j$, with
$$
\E\hat\theta_j = \frac{\sigma_j^2}{\sigma_j^2+\lambda}\theta_j,
\qquad
\Var(\hat\theta_j) = \frac{\sigma_j^2\sigma^2}{(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)^2}.
$$
Hence, since $\norm{\cdot}_2$ is invariant under the orthogonal $V$,
$$
m(\lambda) = \sum_j\left[\underbrace{\frac{\lambda^2\theta_j^2}{(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)^2}}_{\text{bias}^2}
+ \underbrace{\frac{\sigma^2\sigma_j^2}{(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)^2}}_{\text{variance}}\right].
$$
Differentiate term by term at $\lambda=0$. The bias term is $O(\lambda^2)$, so its derivative
vanishes. The variance term has derivative
$$
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left.\frac{\sigma^2\sigma_j^2}{(\sigma_j^2+\lambda)^2}\right|_{\lambda=0}
= -\frac{2\sigma^2\sigma_j^2}{\sigma_j^6} = -\frac{2\sigma^2}{\sigma_j^4}.
$$
Summing, $m'(0) = -2\sigma^2\sum_j\sigma_j^{-4} = -2\sigma^2\tr\big((X^\top X)^{-2}\big)<0$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Read the two terms. Bias grows like $\lambda^2$ — *second* order — while variance falls like $\lambda$
— *first* order. So at $\lambda = 0$ there is always a free lunch, and the unbiased estimator is
inadmissible in mean squared error. The effect is largest when some $\sigma_j$ is small, since the
derivative scales as $\sigma_j^{-4}$, which is exactly the ill-conditioned case. This computation is
the regression counterpart of the shrinkage result on the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-statistics/covariance-estimation">covariance estimation</TopicRef> page,
and both are instances of the James–Stein phenomenon.
</KeyIdea>

## Ridge versus lasso

<Proposition id="lasso-vs-ridge" title="Why lasso is sparse and ridge is not">
Consider the orthogonal design $X^\top X = nI$, where both problems decouple across coordinates. With
$z = X^\top y/n$,
$$
\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}}_j = \frac{z_j}{1+\lambda},
\qquad
\hat\beta^{\text{lasso}}_j = \sign(z_j)\max\big(|z_j|-\lambda,\ 0\big) .
$$
Ridge multiplies; lasso subtracts and truncates. Only the second can return exactly zero.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
With $X^\top X = nI$ the objective separates. For ridge, minimise
$\tfrac n2(\beta_j - z_j)^2 + \tfrac\lambda2\beta_j^2$ — a quadratic, minimised at
$z_j/(1+\lambda/n)$; absorb $n$ into $\lambda$. For lasso, minimise
$\tfrac n2(\beta_j-z_j)^2 + \lambda|\beta_j|$, whose solution is the
<TopicRef id="advanced-optimization/proximal-and-projected-methods">soft-thresholding operator</TopicRef>
computed there.
</Proof>

<Intuition>
Geometrically: the constrained forms are "minimise $\norm{y-X\beta}_2^2$ subject to
$\norm\beta_q \le t$". The $\ell^2$ ball is round, so the tangency point between the elliptical
level sets of the loss and the ball is almost surely in the interior of a face — no coordinate is
zero. The $\ell^1$ ball has vertices on the axes, and an elliptical level set expanding from a generic
centre hits a vertex with positive probability. Sparsity is a consequence of the **non-smoothness** of
$\norm{\cdot}_1$ at the axes, which is the same fact as the flat region in the soft-threshold formula.
</Intuition>

| | Ridge | Lasso |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Penalty | $\lambda\norm\beta_2^2$ | $\lambda\norm\beta_1$ |
| Closed form | yes | only for orthogonal design |
| Sparse | no | yes |
| Strictly convex objective | yes | no (unique solution not guaranteed) |
| Works when $p>n$ | yes | yes |
| Rate under sparsity | $p/n$ | $s\log p/n$ |
| Prox operator | scaling | soft threshold |

The rate comparison is the content of the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-statistics/sparse-recovery-and-the-lasso">lasso theorem</TopicRef>: when
the truth is sparse, lasso's $s\log p/n$ beats ridge's $p/n$; when it is dense, ridge wins.
