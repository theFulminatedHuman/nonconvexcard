<Intuition>
Full fine-tuning updates every weight, so it needs optimizer state for every weight — for a 7B model
in mixed-precision Adam that is over 100 GB before a single activation is stored. But the *change*
needed to specialise an already-capable model to a narrow task is plausibly far simpler than the
model itself. LoRA takes that intuition literally: constrain the update to have low rank, and train
only its two thin factors.
</Intuition>

## The parameterisation

<Definition id="lora-def" title="Low-rank adapter">
For a frozen pretrained matrix $W_0 \in \R^{d\times k}$, a rank-$r$ adapter replaces it with
$$
W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA,
\qquad B \in \R^{d\times r},\quad A \in \R^{r\times k},\quad r \ll \min(d,k),
$$
where $A$ and $B$ are trained and $W_0$ is not. The scalar $\alpha$ is a fixed hyperparameter.
</Definition>

<Claim kind="proposition" id="lora-param-count" title="Trainable parameter count">
The adapter has $r(d+k)$ trainable parameters against $dk$ for the full matrix, a reduction factor of
$$
\frac{dk}{r(d+k)}.
$$
For a square matrix $d = k$ this is $d/(2r)$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
$B$ contributes $dr$ entries and $A$ contributes $rk$, totalling $r(d+k)$; the full matrix has $dk$.
The ratio follows, and at $d=k$ it is $d^2/(2rd) = d/(2r)$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Details summary="What this is worth in practice">
A Llama-2-7B attention projection has $d = k = 4096$. At $r = 8$ the adapter trains
$8\cdot 8192 = 65{,}536$ parameters instead of $16.8$ million — a $256\times$ reduction, matching
$d/(2r) = 4096/16$.

The saving that matters is not the parameter count itself but the **optimizer state**. Mixed-precision
Adam carries roughly 16 bytes per *trainable* parameter (fp32 master weights, two moment estimates,
and the fp16 gradient). Frozen weights need none of it. That is why a model that cannot be
fine-tuned on a single 80 GB card can be LoRA-tuned on one comfortably — and why QLoRA, which also
holds $W_0$ in 4-bit, pushes it further still.
</Details>

## Why $B$ starts at zero

<Claim kind="proposition" id="lora-zero-init" title="Zero initialisation preserves the pretrained function">
Initialise $B = 0$ and $A$ with i.i.d. entries of variance $\sigma^2 &gt; 0$. Then at initialisation
$W = W_0$, so the adapted network computes exactly the pretrained function; yet the gradient with
respect to $B$ is generally non-zero, so training is not stuck.
</Claim>

<Proof>
With $B = 0$ we have $BA = 0$ and hence $W = W_0$, giving the first claim.

For the second, let $\mathcal L$ be the loss and $G = \partial\mathcal L/\partial W \in \R^{d\times k}$
the gradient with respect to the effective matrix. By the chain rule through $W = W_0 + \frac\alpha r BA$,
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial B} = \frac{\alpha}{r}\,G A^\top,
\qquad
\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial A} = \frac{\alpha}{r}\,B^\top G .
$$
At initialisation the second vanishes because $B=0$, but the first is $\frac\alpha r GA^\top$, which
is non-zero whenever $G \ne 0$ and $A$ has full row rank — an event of probability one for a
continuous initialisation. So $B$ moves off zero at the first step, after which $A$ receives gradient
too. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The asymmetry is the point. Initialising *both* factors at zero would leave both gradients zero, a
genuine stationary point. Initialising both randomly would perturb the pretrained function before any
learning, discarding some of what you are paying to keep. Exactly one factor at zero gives you the
pretrained function at step zero *and* a live gradient.
</KeyIdea>

## The $\alpha/r$ scaling

<Claim kind="proposition" id="lora-scaling" title="The α/r scaling makes updates rank-invariant in magnitude">
Let $B$ and $A$ have i.i.d. mean-zero entries with variances $\sigma_B^2$ and $\sigma_A^2$
respectively, independent of each other. Then
$$
\E\left[\left\|\frac{\alpha}{r}BA\right\|_F^2\right] = \frac{\alpha^2}{r}\,dk\,\sigma_B^2\sigma_A^2 ,
$$
so the expected squared magnitude falls as $1/r$ rather than growing linearly with $r$ as it would
without the scaling.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Write $M = BA$, so $M_{ij} = \sum_{t=1}^r B_{it}A_{tj}$. The summands are independent and mean zero,
so
$$
\E\big[M_{ij}^2\big] = \sum_{t=1}^r \E\big[B_{it}^2\big]\E\big[A_{tj}^2\big] = r\,\sigma_B^2\sigma_A^2 ,
$$
the cross terms vanishing by independence and zero mean. Summing over the $dk$ entries,
$\E\|M\|_F^2 = dk\,r\,\sigma_B^2\sigma_A^2$. Multiplying by the scale factor $(\alpha/r)^2$ gives
$$
\E\left\|\tfrac{\alpha}{r}M\right\|_F^2 = \frac{\alpha^2}{r^2}\cdot dk\,r\,\sigma_B^2\sigma_A^2
= \frac{\alpha^2 dk\,\sigma_B^2\sigma_A^2}{r}. \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Proof>

<Note>
The practical consequence is the one the original work claims: sweeping $r$ does not require
re-tuning the learning rate, because $\alpha/r$ absorbs the rank dependence. The proposition
describes magnitudes *at initialisation* under an idealised i.i.d. model; it says nothing about the
scale of the learned update after training, and the common report that $\alpha = 2r$ works well
across ranks is an empirical convention rather than a consequence of this calculation.
</Note>

## What a rank constraint can and cannot represent

<Claim kind="theorem" id="eckart-young" title="Eckart–Young–Mirsky">
Let $M \in \R^{d\times k}$ have singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_{\min(d,k)}$
and SVD $M = \sum_i \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$. Then for any $r$, the truncation
$M_r = \sum_{i\le r}\sigma_i u_iv_i^\top$ satisfies
$$
\min_{\operatorname{rank}(N)\le r}\|M - N\|_F^2 = \|M-M_r\|_F^2 = \sum_{i&gt;r}\sigma_i^2,
$$
and the analogous statement in spectral norm with value $\sigma_{r+1}$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
We prove the Frobenius case. Let $N$ have rank at most $r$. The Frobenius norm is unitarily
invariant, so writing $M = U\Sigma V^\top$ and $\tilde N = U^\top N V$ (which still has rank at most
$r$), we have $\|M-N\|_F = \|\Sigma - \tilde N\|_F$. It therefore suffices to minimise over rank-$r$
matrices $\tilde N$ against the diagonal $\Sigma$.

By the Weyl inequalities for singular values, for any matrices $P,Q$,
$\sigma_{i+j-1}(P+Q) \le \sigma_i(P) + \sigma_j(Q)$. Apply this with $P = M - N$, $Q = N$, $j = r+1$:
since $\operatorname{rank} N \le r$ we have $\sigma_{r+1}(N) = 0$, giving
$$
\sigma_{i+r}(M) \ \le\ \sigma_i(M-N) \qquad \text{for all } i \ge 1 .
$$
Hence
$$
\|M-N\|_F^2 = \sum_{i\ge1}\sigma_i(M-N)^2 \ \ge\ \sum_{i\ge1}\sigma_{i+r}(M)^2 = \sum_{i&gt;r}\sigma_i(M)^2 .
$$
The truncation $M_r$ attains this value, since $M - M_r = \sum_{i&gt;r}\sigma_iu_iv_i^\top$ has
singular values exactly $\sigma_{r+1},\sigma_{r+2},\dots$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Eckart–Young tells you *the price of a rank budget, given the matrix*: it is the tail of the singular
value spectrum. So LoRA at rank $r$ can represent the ideal full fine-tuning update well **exactly
when that update has rapidly decaying singular values**. The theorem converts the question "is LoRA
enough?" into an empirical question about a spectrum — and notice that it does not answer it.
</KeyIdea>

## The hypothesis underneath

<Claim kind="conjecture" id="intrinsic-dim-hypothesis" title="The low-rank update hypothesis">
For a pretrained model of sufficient capability and a downstream task in its neighbourhood, there
exists a weight update $\Delta W^\star$ achieving near-full-fine-tuning performance whose singular
values decay fast enough that $\operatorname{rank}_\eps(\Delta W^\star) \ll \min(d,k)$.
</Claim>

<Warning>
This is the load-bearing assumption of every low-rank adaptation method, and it is **not** a theorem.
What exists is evidence:
- Measurements that fine-tuning reaches strong performance when optimisation is confined to a random
  low-dimensional subspace, with the required dimension shrinking as pretrained models get better.
- Direct reports that LoRA at modest rank matches full fine-tuning on many downstream tasks.
- Observations that the spectrum of measured $\Delta W$ from full fine-tuning does decay quickly.

What does not exist is a proof, a characterisation of which tasks satisfy it, or a way to predict the
needed rank before training. There are also documented failure cases — notably when fine-tuning must
inject substantial *new knowledge* rather than re-weight existing capability, where reported gaps to
full fine-tuning are larger. Treat "LoRA is as good as full fine-tuning" as a claim with a scope that
has to be checked per task, not as an established fact.
</Warning>

<Details summary="Merging, and why it is free at inference">
Because the adapter is additive and the scaling is a constant, after training you may form
$$
W_{\text{merged}} = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA
$$
once and discard $A$ and $B$. Inference then costs exactly what the original model cost — no extra
matrices, no extra latency. This is a genuine advantage over adapter layers inserted into the
residual stream, which add sequential work at every forward pass.

The corresponding cost: merged adapters cannot be swapped per request. Serving many tasks from one
base model means keeping them unmerged and paying a small per-token overhead, which is the trade
made by multi-adapter serving systems.
</Details>

<NotSaid>
Rank is not the only thing that matters. *Which* matrices carry adapters — attention projections
only, or the MLP too — changes results substantially and is not addressed by anything above. The
propositions here concern a single matrix in isolation; the composition of many adapted layers, and
the interaction with a frozen quantized base as in QLoRA, are outside what has been proved here.
</NotSaid>
