<Intuition>
Train on task B and the model gets better at B and worse at A. Gradient descent has no reason to
respect what A needed: it moves whichever weights reduce B's loss fastest, and some of those weights
were carrying A.

The fix suggested by Bayes is to remember not just *where* the old solution was, but *how sharply*
the old loss rose in each direction. Weights that A's loss was indifferent to may move freely; ones
it cared about should be held. The Fisher information is the object that measures "cared about", and
the derivation below shows why.
</Intuition>

## Fisher information is curvature

<Claim kind="theorem" id="info-matrix-equality" title="Information matrix equality">
Let $\lbrace p_\theta \rbrace$ be a family of densities, smooth in $\theta$, with support not
depending on $\theta$ and with differentiation under the integral sign permitted twice. Define the
Fisher information
$$
F(\theta) = \E_{x\sim p_\theta}\!\left[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)^\top\right].
$$
Then
$$
F(\theta) = -\,\E_{x \sim p_\theta}\!\left[\nabla^2_\theta \log p_\theta(x)\right].
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
The regularity conditions are doing real work: fixed support, and the exchange of $\nabla$ with
$\int$. Note also that **both expectations are under the model $p_\theta$**, not under the data
distribution. When the model is misspecified the two sides differ, and the discrepancy is exactly
what the sandwich (robust) covariance corrects for.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Since $p_\theta$ is a density, $\int p_\theta(x)\,dx = 1$ for all $\theta$. Differentiating twice
under the integral sign gives $\int \nabla^2_\theta p_\theta(x)\,dx = 0$.

Now express $\nabla^2 p_\theta$ via the log. From $\nabla p_\theta = p_\theta \nabla \log p_\theta$,
differentiate again by the product rule:
$$
\nabla^2 p_\theta
= \nabla\big(p_\theta \nabla\log p_\theta\big)
= p_\theta\,\nabla\log p_\theta\,\nabla\log p_\theta^\top + p_\theta\,\nabla^2\log p_\theta .
$$
Integrating and using $\int\nabla^2 p_\theta = 0$,
$$
0 = \E_{p_\theta}\!\left[\nabla\log p_\theta\,\nabla\log p_\theta^\top\right] + \E_{p_\theta}\!\left[\nabla^2\log p_\theta\right]
= F(\theta) + \E_{p_\theta}\!\left[\nabla^2\log p_\theta\right],
$$
which rearranges to the claim. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The Fisher is the expected curvature of the negative log-likelihood. So $F_{ii}$ large means the old
task's loss rises steeply when weight $i$ moves — the weight is load-bearing. $F_{ii}\approx 0$ means
the loss is flat in that direction and the weight can be repurposed at no cost. It is also positive
semidefinite by construction, being an expectation of outer products, which is what makes the penalty
below a sensible convex pull.
</KeyIdea>

## From Bayes to a quadratic penalty

<Claim kind="proposition" id="laplace-penalty" title="The elastic-weight-consolidation objective">
Let $\theta^\star_A$ be a mode of the posterior $p(\theta \mid \mathcal D_A)$. Approximating that
posterior by a Gaussian via a second-order expansion at $\theta^\star_A$, the maximum a posteriori
objective for the combined data $\mathcal D_A \cup \mathcal D_B$ becomes, up to additive constants,
$$
\min_{\theta}\; \mathcal L_B(\theta) \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\big(\theta-\theta^\star_A\big)^\top F\big(\theta-\theta^\star_A\big),
$$
where $\mathcal L_B$ is the negative log-likelihood of $\mathcal D_B$ and $F$ the Fisher information
at $\theta^\star_A$.
</Claim>

<Derivation>
<Step why="Conditional independence of the two datasets given the parameters.">
$$
\log p(\theta \mid \mathcal D_A, \mathcal D_B) = \log p(\mathcal D_B\mid\theta) + \log p(\theta\mid\mathcal D_A) + \text{const}.
$$
</Step>
<Step why="Second-order Taylor expansion of the old-task log posterior at its mode; the linear term vanishes there.">
$$
\log p(\theta\mid\mathcal D_A) \approx \log p(\theta^\star_A\mid\mathcal D_A)
- \tfrac12(\theta-\theta^\star_A)^\top H_A (\theta-\theta^\star_A),
$$
with $H_A = -\nabla^2 \log p(\theta\mid\mathcal D_A)\big|_{\theta^\star_A} \succeq 0$.
</Step>
<Step why="Identify the curvature with the Fisher using the information matrix equality.">
For a flat prior and $n_A$ observations, $H_A \approx n_A F(\theta^\star_A)$ by the theorem above.
Absorbing $n_A$ and the prior strength into a single hyperparameter $\lambda$ gives $H_A = \lambda F$.
</Step>
<Step why="Negate to turn maximisation into minimisation.">
$$
\min_\theta\ \mathcal L_B(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2}(\theta-\theta^\star_A)^\top F (\theta-\theta^\star_A). \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Step>
</Derivation>

In practice $F$ is replaced by its diagonal, giving the familiar per-weight form
$$
\mathcal L_B(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_{ii}\big(\theta_i - \theta^\star_{A,i}\big)^2 .
$$

<Warning>
Three approximations were made and all three are questionable for deep networks:

1. **The quadratic expansion is local.** Fine-tuning moves weights far enough that a second-order
   model of the old loss is not reliable, and the expansion has no error control here.
2. **$\theta^\star_A$ is not a mode.** The derivation expands at a posterior mode. A pretrained
   network sits at some point found by SGD, which is not a stationary point of the objective in any
   verified sense, so the vanishing linear term is assumed rather than established.
3. **Diagonal Fisher discards the couplings.** The off-diagonal entries encode exactly the correlated
   directions that matter most, and dropping them is done for tractability — $F$ is
   $P \times P$ — not for any reason of accuracy.

EWC is a principled *heuristic*: the Bayesian story motivates the form of the penalty, and the
penalty's usefulness on deep networks is an empirical finding, not a consequence of the derivation.
</Warning>

## What low rank buys

<Claim kind="proposition" id="lora-deviation-bound" title="Low rank bounds how far the function can move">
Let a layer compute $x \mapsto W_0 x$ and be adapted to $x\mapsto (W_0 + \tfrac\alpha r BA)x$. Then
for every input $x$,
$$
\big\|(W_0 + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BA)x - W_0x\big\|_2 \ \le\ \frac{\alpha}{r}\,\|B\|_2\,\|A\|_2\,\|x\|_2 ,
$$
so weight decay on $A$ and $B$ directly limits the layer's deviation from the pretrained function.
</Claim>

<Proof>
The difference is $\tfrac\alpha r BAx$. Submultiplicativity of the spectral norm gives
$\|BAx\|_2 \le \|B\|_2\|A\|_2\|x\|_2$, and $\alpha/r &gt; 0$ scales through. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Note>
This is a crude bound — it is tight only when $x$ aligns with the top right singular vector of $BA$ —
but it makes a structural point. Constraining the update to rank $r$ and penalising the factors gives
an explicit, tunable budget on how far the adapted model may move from the pretrained one, and
recovers the pretrained function exactly at $B=0$. Full fine-tuning has no comparable knob: its
deviation is limited only by the optimiser's trajectory.

This is one reason low-rank adaptation is often reported to forget less than full fine-tuning at
matched downstream performance. It is a plausible mechanism supported by the inequality, not a proof
that it does — the bound constrains one layer's output, and forgetting is a property of the composed
network on a distribution the bound says nothing about.
</Note>

<Claim kind="empirical" id="forgetting-empirical" title="Narrow fine-tuning degrades unrelated capability">
Fine-tuning a general model on a narrow distribution reliably reduces measured performance on
capabilities the base model previously had, with degradation increasing in the number of steps and
the learning rate, and decreasing when a fraction of general data is mixed into the fine-tuning set.
</Claim>

<Details summary="What is actually done about it">
- **Replay / data mixing.** Include a slice of the original or general-purpose distribution in the
  fine-tuning mixture. This is the most reliable method reported, and it sidesteps the approximations
  above entirely by simply not forgetting the data.
- **Smaller learning rates and fewer steps.** Blunt, effective, and consistent with the deviation
  bound.
- **Parameter-efficient methods.** Freeze most weights; constrain and penalise the rest, per the
  proposition.
- **Fisher-weighted penalties.** EWC and its descendants, with the caveats above.
- **Model merging.** Train separately and interpolate the weights afterwards, which avoids sequential
  interference by construction — though *why* weight interpolation preserves both capabilities is
  itself not understood.
</Details>

<OpenProblem>
There is no quantitative theory predicting how much a given fine-tuning run will forget. Inputs to
such a theory would include the divergence between the fine-tuning and pretraining distributions, the
optimisation trajectory, and the geometry of the pretrained solution — and no current account
combines them into a prediction that can be checked before training. Practitioners run the evaluation
suite before and after, which is the honest method available.
</OpenProblem>
