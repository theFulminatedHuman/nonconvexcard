<Intuition>
Almost every argument about a production model boils down to four numbers: how fast requests
arrive, how long one takes to serve, how many are in flight at once, and how hard the machine is
being pushed. Get those four straight and the rest of this field is arithmetic. Confuse any two of
them — and "throughput" and "capacity" are confused constantly — and you will size a cluster wrong
by a factor you cannot find afterwards.

The other half of the vocabulary is about *which* latency you mean. A system whose average response
is 200 ms can still fail most of its users, and the reason is not subtle: averages hide exactly the
part of the distribution that people notice.
</Intuition>

## The four quantities

<Definition id="serving-vocabulary" title="Offered load, throughput, concurrency, utilisation">
For a system in steady state over a long interval:

- **Offered load** $\lambda$ — the rate at which requests *arrive*, in requests per second. It is a
  property of the world, not of your system.
- **Service time** $S$ — the time one request occupies a server, excluding any time spent waiting.
  It is a random variable; write $\E[S]$ for its mean.
- **Throughput** $X$ — the rate at which requests *complete*.
- **Concurrency** $L$ — the number of requests inside the system at a given instant, waiting or
  being served.
- **Latency** $W$ — the time from arrival to completion, including queueing: $W \ge S$ always.
- **Capacity** $\mu$ — the maximum completion rate. With $c$ identical servers each able to
  complete $1/\E[S]$ requests per second, $\mu = c/\E[S]$.
- **Utilisation** $\rho = \lambda/\mu$ — the fraction of capacity the offered load asks for.
</Definition>

<Warning title="Throughput is not capacity">
Throughput is what you *measure*; capacity is what you *have*. A system serving 400 requests per
second might be at 40% utilisation or at 99%, and those two situations behave completely
differently — one has headroom, the other is about to fall over. A throughput graph alone cannot
tell you which you are in. This is why dashboards that show only requests per second are close to
useless for capacity decisions.
</Warning>

## Stability

<Claim kind="proposition" id="stability-condition" title="Stability requires utilisation below one">
Let $A(t)$ be the number of arrivals and $D(t)$ the number of departures by time $t$, with
$A(t)/t \to \lambda$ and departure capacity $D(t)/t \le \mu$. If $\lambda > \mu$ then the backlog
$L(t) = A(t) - D(t)$ satisfies $L(t) \to \infty$. Consequently throughput $X = \lambda$ is
achievable only when $\rho = \lambda/\mu \le 1$, and, as the next page shows, only $\rho < 1$ keeps
latency finite.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
- Arrivals and departures are counted over a common interval and no requests are dropped or
  abandoned. Dropping requests is precisely how a real system escapes this proposition — it
  converts an unbounded queue into a visible error rate, which is the point of admission control.
- Capacity $\mu$ is constant. Autoscaling makes $\mu$ a function of time and is treated separately.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
By definition $L(t) = A(t) - D(t) \ge A(t) - \mu t$. Divide by $t$:
$$
\frac{L(t)}{t} \ \ge\ \frac{A(t)}{t} - \mu \ \longrightarrow\ \lambda - \mu \ >\ 0 .
$$
So $L(t)/t$ is eventually bounded below by a positive constant $(\lambda-\mu)/2$, giving
$L(t) \ge (\lambda-\mu)t/2 \to \infty$. Since $L$ counts requests present in the system and each
present request has been waiting at least since its arrival, mean latency diverges as well.
</Proof>

<NotSaid>
This says nothing about what happens at $\rho$ slightly *below* one. It is tempting to read
"stable for $\rho < 1$" as "fine for $\rho < 1$", and that reading is wrong: the queue is finite at
$\rho = 0.99$ but its mean length is around a hundred requests. Stability is a statement about
$t \to \infty$, not about whether your latency target is met.
</NotSaid>

## Which latency you mean

A latency distribution is summarised by its quantiles. Write $F$ for the CDF of $W$ and
$$
q_p \ =\ \inf\{t : F(t) \ge p\}
$$
for the $p$-quantile — "p99" is $q_{0.99}$, the value 99% of requests come in under.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="mean-does-not-bound-tail" title="The mean constrains the tail only by a factor of 1/(1-p)">
Let $W \ge 0$ have mean $m$. Then for every $p \in (0,1)$,
$$
q_p(W) \ \le\ \frac{m}{1-p} ,
$$
and the bound is attained: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a distribution with mean $m$ whose
$p$-quantile exceeds $m/(1-p) - \varepsilon$. In particular a mean of 200 ms permits a p99 of just
under 20 s, and permits nothing worse.
</Claim>

<Proof>
**Upper bound.** By Markov's inequality, $\Pr[W \ge t] \le m/t$ for every $t > 0$. Put
$t_\delta = m/(1-p) + \delta$ with $\delta > 0$. Then $\Pr[W \ge t_\delta] \le m/t_\delta < 1-p$,
so $F(t_\delta) = \Pr[W \le t_\delta] > p$ and therefore $q_p(W) \le t_\delta$. Letting
$\delta \downarrow 0$ gives $q_p(W) \le m/(1-p)$.

**Tightness.** Fix $\alpha \in (1-p,\,1)$ and let $W$ take the value $v = m/\alpha$ with
probability $\alpha$ and $0$ otherwise. Its mean is $\alpha \cdot m/\alpha = m$. Since
$\Pr[W < v] = 1-\alpha < p$, no point below $v$ has $F \ge p$, so $q_p(W) = v = m/\alpha$. As
$\alpha \downarrow 1-p$ this increases to $m/(1-p)$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
A factor of $1/(1-p)$ is all the mean ever tells you. At $p = 0.99$ that factor is **100**. So
"average latency 200 ms" is consistent with a p99 anywhere from 200 ms to 20 s, and the two
situations are completely different products. This is the precise reason serving systems are
specified by quantiles rather than means.
</KeyIdea>

## Composing stages

Real requests pass through several stages in sequence: a load balancer, a tokeniser, a retrieval
call, the model itself. It is tempting to budget a latency target by adding up per-stage p99s.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="percentile-not-additive" title="Percentiles do not add">
Let $W = W_1 + \cdots + W_k$ with the $W_i$ non-negative. Then
$$
q_p(W) \ \le\ \sum_{i=1}^k q_{p_i}(W_i) \qquad \text{whenever} \quad \sum_{i=1}^k (1-p_i) \le 1-p .
$$
Independence is not needed; the bound holds for arbitrarily dependent stages.
</Claim>

<Proof>
If $W_i < q_{p_i}(W_i)$ for every $i$ then $W < \sum_i q_{p_i}(W_i)$. By a union bound,
$$
\Pr\Big[\exists i:\, W_i \ge q_{p_i}(W_i)\Big] \ \le\ \sum_{i=1}^k \Pr[W_i \ge q_{p_i}(W_i)]
\ \le\ \sum_{i=1}^k (1-p_i) \ \le\ 1-p .
$$
Hence $\Pr[W < \sum_i q_{p_i}(W_i)] \ge p$, which is the definition of
$q_p(W) \le \sum_i q_{p_i}(W_i)$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
To hit an end-to-end p99 across $k$ stages, the union bound says it is enough for each stage to hit
its $\,1 - 0.01/k\,$ quantile. With $k = 4$ stages that is each stage's **p99.75**, not its p99.
Budgeting with per-stage p99s is not conservative — it is optimistic, and the error grows with the
number of stages.
</KeyIdea>

<Note title="Why the bound is loose">
The union bound ignores the fact that stages are rarely slow simultaneously. When the $W_i$ are
independent and light-tailed, the sum concentrates and $q_{0.99}(W)$ sits much closer to
$\sum_i \E[W_i]$ than to $\sum_i q_{0.99}(W_i)$. The bound is the safe direction; the concentration
is the typical direction. Which one governs depends on whether the stages share a failure cause —
the same GPU, the same network, the same garbage collector — and shared causes are exactly what
independence assumes away.
</Note>

## What an SLO actually asserts

<Definition id="slo" title="Service-level objective">
A **service-level objective** is a triple: a metric, a quantile, and a window. "p99 latency under
500 ms over a rolling 28 days" asserts that, of the requests in any such window, at most 1% took
longer than 500 ms.
</Definition>

Three consequences follow immediately from the definitions and are worth stating because they are
routinely missed.

1. **An SLO is a budget, not a bound.** 1% of a billion requests is ten million slow requests.
   The SLO permits them.
2. **The window matters as much as the quantile.** A 28-day p99 permits a single catastrophic hour,
   because one hour is 0.15% of 28 days. A one-hour p99 does not.
3. **Quantiles of quantiles are not quantiles.** Averaging per-minute p99s over a day does not give
   the day's p99, and in general underestimates it, because the average of a set of quantiles is
   not the quantile of the pooled distribution.

<Warning title="You cannot average percentiles">
If minute one has p99 = 100 ms and minute two has p99 = 300 ms, the two-minute p99 is not 200 ms.
It is whatever value 1% of the *pooled* requests exceeded, which depends on the request counts and
the full shapes of both minutes. Percentile metrics must be computed from merged histograms, not
from per-window percentiles.
</Warning>
