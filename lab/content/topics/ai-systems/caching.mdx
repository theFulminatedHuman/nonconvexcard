<Intuition>
Caching feels like it should have a threshold: store enough and everything hits. Under realistic
popularity it does not. Request popularity is heavily skewed but has a long tail, and the
consequence is that hit rate grows like a *power* of cache size — you buy a fixed multiplicative
improvement per order of magnitude of memory, forever, without ever arriving.

The second surprise is that a cache's value is capped by something it does not control. If a miss
costs 100 ms and a hit costs 1 ms, then even a 99% hit rate leaves you at $0.99 \times 1 + 0.01
\times 100 = 2$ ms — twice the hit cost. The miss path, not the cache, sets the floor.
</Intuition>

## What a hit rate is worth

<Claim kind="proposition" id="cache-latency-identity" title="Effective latency under a cache">
Let $h$ be the probability a request hits, $t_{\text{hit}}$ the cost of a hit, and
$t_{\text{miss}}$ the cost of a miss (including the work to populate the cache). Then
$$
\E[T] \;=\; h\,t_{\text{hit}} + (1-h)\,t_{\text{miss}}
\;=\; t_{\text{miss}} - h\,(t_{\text{miss}} - t_{\text{hit}}) .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Condition on the hit event and apply the law of total expectation. The second form rearranges the
first.
</Proof>

<Claim kind="corollary" id="cache-amdahl" title="A cache cannot beat its own miss path">
The speedup relative to no cache is
$$
S(h) \;=\; \frac{t_{\text{miss}}}{\E[T]} \;=\; \frac{1}{(1-h) + h\,t_{\text{hit}}/t_{\text{miss}}}
\;\xrightarrow[h \to 1]{}\; \frac{t_{\text{miss}}}{t_{\text{hit}}} ,
$$
and for $h$ below $1$ the miss term $(1-h)$ dominates as soon as
$1-h \gtrsim t_{\text{hit}}/t_{\text{miss}}$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Divide $t_{\text{miss}}$ by the expression in the proposition. This is exactly Amdahl's law from
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/scaling-limits" /> with serial fraction $s = t_{\text{hit}}/t_{\text{miss}}$
in the limit, and with $(1-h)$ playing the role of the unparallelised remainder at finite $h$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
With $t_{\text{miss}}/t_{\text{hit}} = 100$, a hit rate of 90% gives speedup
$1/(0.1 + 0.009) \approx 9.2$, and 99% gives $1/(0.01+0.009) \approx 53$. The last percent of hit
rate is worth more than the first fifty. Cache work is therefore worth doing precisely when you are
already close to the ceiling — the opposite of most optimisation.
</KeyIdea>

## Where hit rates come from

<Definition id="zipf" title="Zipf popularity">
Items ranked $1,\dots,N$ by popularity have request probabilities
$$
p_i \;=\; \frac{i^{-a}}{H_N(a)}, \qquad H_N(a) = \sum_{j=1}^N j^{-a},
$$
with $a > 0$ the skew exponent. $H_N(a)$ is the generalised harmonic number. Larger $a$ means more
concentrated popularity.
</Definition>

<Claim kind="proposition" id="zipf-hit-rate" title="Static optimal hit rate under a Zipf popularity law">
A cache of capacity $C$ holding the $C$ most popular items — the best any eviction policy can do
against i.i.d. requests — achieves hit rate
$$
h(C) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{C} p_i \;=\; \frac{H_C(a)}{H_N(a)} .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Requests are independent draws from $\{p_i\}$, so the hit probability is the total mass of the
cached set. Among all sets of size $C$, the mass is maximised by taking the $C$ largest $p_i$,
which are $i = 1,\dots,C$ since $p_i$ is decreasing in $i$.
</Proof>

<Claim kind="corollary" id="zipf-asymptotic" title="The power-law form of the hit rate">
For $0 < a < 1$ and $1 \ll C \ll N$,
$$
h(C) \;\approx\; \left(\frac{C}{N}\right)^{1-a} ,
$$
for $a = 1$, $h(C) \approx \ln C / \ln N$, and for $a > 1$, $h(C) \to$ a constant close to $1$ even
for $C \ll N$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
For $a \ne 1$, comparing the sum with the integral $\int_1^M x^{-a}dx$ gives
$H_M(a) = \frac{M^{1-a}-1}{1-a} + O(1)$. For $a<1$ the $M^{1-a}$ term dominates, so
$H_C(a)/H_N(a) \to (C/N)^{1-a}$. For $a = 1$, $H_M(1) = \ln M + \gamma + o(1)$, giving the
logarithmic ratio. For $a>1$, $H_N(a)$ converges to $\zeta(a)$ as $N \to \infty$ while $H_C(a)$ is
already close to it for modest $C$, so the ratio approaches $1$.
</Proof>

Checking the approximation against exact sums with $N = 10^6$:

| $a$ | $C$ | exact $h$ | approximation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | $10^3$ | 0.031 | 0.032 |
| 0.5 | $10^4$ | 0.099 | 0.100 |
| 0.8 | $10^3$ | 0.207 | 0.251 |
| 1.0 | $10^3$ | 0.520 | 0.500 |
| 1.2 | $10^3$ | 0.822 | — |

<Warning title="The approximation degrades as a approaches 1">
At $a = 0.8$ the power law overstates the hit rate by about 20%, because the $O(1)$ terms dropped
from $H_M(a)$ are not negligible when $1-a$ is small. Use the exact harmonic ratio when the answer
matters; the power law is for reasoning about *how* the hit rate scales, not for sizing.
</Warning>

<KeyIdea>
For $a < 1$ the hit rate is a power law in cache size, so there is **no knee**. Going from 1 GB to
10 GB multiplies the hit rate by $10^{1-a}$ — at $a = 0.8$, a factor of $1.58$ — and going from 10
GB to 100 GB does exactly the same again. Cache sizing is therefore an economic question with no
natural stopping point, which is why it is usually settled by cost per byte rather than by finding
a threshold.
</KeyIdea>

## LRU rather than an oracle

Real caches do not know the popularity ranking. The standard analytic handle is Che's
approximation.

<Claim kind="heuristic" id="che" title="Che's approximation for LRU">
Define the **characteristic time** $t_C$ as the unique solution of
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(1 - e^{-p_i \lambda t_C}\Bigr) \;=\; C ,
$$
where $\lambda$ is the total request rate. Then item $i$'s hit probability is approximately
$1 - e^{-p_i \lambda t_C}$, and the overall LRU hit rate is
$\sum_i p_i\bigl(1-e^{-p_i\lambda t_C}\bigr)$.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
The approximation replaces the per-item eviction time, which genuinely depends on the whole request
sequence, by a single deterministic constant shared by all items. It is an approximation with no
general error bound, reported to be accurate for large caches under independent-reference
workloads, and it is offered here as a modelling tool rather than a theorem.
</Assumptions>

## Prefix caching is a different problem

Caching an LLM's KV state for a shared prompt prefix does not fit the model above, and the reason
is worth stating precisely: the unit of reuse is not an object but a *prefix*, and prefixes form a
tree rather than a set.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="prefix-savings" title="Prefill saved by a shared prefix">
Let a request have prompt length $S$ of which the first $k$ tokens are already cached. Using the
prefill cost $C_{\text{fwd}}(S) \approx 2NS + 4LdS^2$ from
<TopicRef id="llm-ops/inference-arithmetic" />, the work saved is
$$
C_{\text{fwd}}(S) - \bigl[C_{\text{fwd}}(S) - C_{\text{fwd}}(k)\bigr]_{\text{recomputed}}
\;=\; 2Nk + 4Ld\,k^2 ,
$$
so the saving is **superlinear** in the cached prefix length whenever the quadratic attention term
is not negligible.
</Claim>

<Proof>
With the first $k$ tokens' keys and values already materialised, only positions $k+1,\dots,S$ need
their projections computed, costing $2N(S-k)$, and their attention against all preceding positions
costs the difference of the quadratic terms. The tokens not recomputed carry exactly the cost
$C_{\text{fwd}}(k) = 2Nk + 4Ldk^2$ that would otherwise have been paid.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Object caches save a fixed cost per hit; prefix caches save a cost that grows *faster than
linearly* in how much of the prompt is shared. A system prompt shared across every request is
therefore worth far more than its token count suggests, and the correct eviction policy weights
entries by $2Nk + 4Ldk^2$ rather than by recency — a cost-aware policy, not an LRU one.
</KeyIdea>

<NotSaid>
Nothing here models cache *coherence*. A read-only cache of immutable KV blocks has no invalidation
problem; a cache of anything mutable does, and by the argument in
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/scaling-limits" /> cross-replica invalidation introduces exactly the
pairwise coherency term that produces a throughput maximum. Distributed caches with invalidation are
in the retrograde regime, and their capacity curve should be measured rather than assumed monotone.
</NotSaid>
