<Intuition>
Put ten one-second jobs behind one hundred-second job and the average wait is about 100 seconds.
Reverse the order and it is about 5. Nothing was made faster; the same work finished in the same
total time. Only the *order* changed, and mean latency moved by a factor of twenty.

That is the entire content of scheduling theory, and it has a clean optimum: always run whatever
will finish soonest. The reason production systems do not simply do that is the other half of the
story — the job that never finishes soonest never runs at all.
</Intuition>

## The optimum

<Claim kind="theorem" id="srpt-optimal" title="SRPT minimises mean response time">
Consider a single server, jobs arriving at arbitrary times with arbitrary known service
requirements, and preemption allowed at no cost. The **shortest-remaining-processing-time** rule —
always serve the job whose remaining work is least — minimises the mean response time
$\frac1n\sum_i (d_i - a_i)$ over all scheduling disciplines, on every arrival sequence.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
- Remaining work is known. For LLM decoding it is not: output length is not known until the model
  emits a stop token. Approximations — predicted length, elapsed tokens as a proxy — inherit the
  benefit only to the extent the prediction is accurate.
- Preemption is free. Iteration-level scheduling makes it nearly so for decoding, since a
  generation can be paused between tokens; it is not free if pausing means evicting a KV cache.
- One server. The multi-server case has no comparably clean optimum.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Fix any schedule $\sigma$ that is not SRPT and let $t$ be the earliest instant at which it serves a
job $j$ while some available job $i$ has strictly smaller remaining work, $r_i < r_j$.

Let $T$ be the set of time slots that $\sigma$ devotes to $i$ or $j$ at or after $t$; since
$\sigma$ completes both, $|T| = r_i + r_j$. Define $\sigma'$ to agree with $\sigma$ everywhere
except on $T$, where it runs $i$ to completion first and then $j$. Every other job's service is
untouched, so only $i$ and $j$ can change completion time.

Write $c_i, c_j$ for completions under $\sigma$ and $c'_i, c'_j$ under $\sigma'$. Two observations
finish it:

- $c'_j$ is the last instant of $T$. Under $\sigma$ the later of $i$ and $j$ also completes at the
  last instant of $T$, so $c'_j = \max(c_i, c_j)$.
- $c'_i$ is the instant at which $r_i$ units of $T$ have elapsed. Any schedule using only slots in
  $T$ needs at least $\min(r_i, r_j) = r_i$ units before either job can finish, so
  $c'_i \le \min(c_i, c_j)$.

Adding, $c'_i + c'_j \le \min(c_i,c_j) + \max(c_i,c_j) = c_i + c_j$: the sum of completion times
does not increase. Each exchange strictly reduces the number of pairs served out of
remaining-work order, so repeating terminates, and the only schedule with no such pair is SRPT.
Arrival times are unchanged throughout, so minimising the sum of completion times minimises the sum
of response times $\sum_i (d_i - a_i)$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Mean response time is minimised by a rule that uses *one* piece of information — remaining work —
and no information about arrival rates, distributions or the future. That is why length prediction
is such a high-leverage feature in an LLM scheduler: it is the single input that the optimal policy
needs.
</KeyIdea>

## What it costs

<Claim kind="proposition" id="srpt-starvation" title="What SRPT costs the largest jobs">
Under SRPT a job with remaining work $r$ is served only at instants when no job of smaller
remaining work is present. Writing
$$
\rho_{<r} \;=\; \lambda\,\E\bigl[S\,\one\{S<r\}\bigr]
$$
for the utilisation generated by work smaller than $r$, that job's expected response time is at
least $r/(1-\rho_{<r})$. For the largest jobs $\rho_{<r} \to \rho$, so their delay carries the
full $1/(1-\rho)$ factor even though SRPT is minimising the *mean* over all jobs.
</Claim>

<Proof>
SRPT gives absolute priority to any present job of smaller remaining work, so the tagged job
accumulates service only during instants when the smaller-work classes are absent. In steady state
the server spends a fraction $\rho_{<r}$ of its time on that work, so the tagged job receives
service at long-run rate at most $1-\rho_{<r}$. Accumulating $r$ units of service therefore takes
at least $r/(1-\rho_{<r})$ in expectation.
</Proof>

<NotSaid>
The lower bound above is not a claim that SRPT starves large jobs catastrophically. Measured
against the response time those jobs would get under a fair policy at the same load, SRPT's penalty
is far smaller than the folklore suggests for most realistic size distributions — the analysis is
in Bansal and Harchol-Balter's study of SRPT unfairness. What the bound does establish is that the
largest jobs pay the full utilisation-wall factor, so SRPT improves the mean without improving, and
sometimes while worsening, the quantile an SLO is written against.
</NotSaid>

<Warning title="Optimal on the mean, arbitrary on the tail">
SRPT minimises the *mean*. By the result in
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/what-a-serving-system-must-do" /> the mean constrains the p99 only by a
factor of $1/(1-p)$, and SRPT spends exactly that budget: it buys a better average by making the
largest requests much worse. A service whose SLO is a high quantile — as serving SLOs usually are —
can be made *worse* by switching to the mean-optimal policy.
</Warning>

## The cost of the fair policy

<Claim kind="proposition" id="hol-blocking" title="Head-of-line blocking under FCFS">
Under first-come-first-served on one server with Poisson arrivals, the mean wait is
$$
\E[W_q] \;=\; \frac{\lambda\E[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)}
\;=\; \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\cdot\frac{1+c_s^2}{2}\cdot\E[S] ,
$$
so a single very long request inflates $\E[S^2]$ and therefore the wait of *every* request behind
it, regardless of how short those are.
</Claim>

<Proof>
This is the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula proved in
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/queueing-and-the-utilisation-wall" />. The head-of-line reading is that
the residual-work term $\rho\E[S^2]/(2\E[S])$ is the expected remaining service of whatever is at
the head, and under FCFS every arrival pays it.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
FCFS is fair in the sense of *order* and unfair in the sense of *outcome*: a 20-token request
queued behind a 4000-token one waits for the whole generation. Continuous (iteration-level)
batching is the standard escape — it interleaves at token granularity, so a short request's wait is
bounded by one decode step rather than by a whole generation.
</KeyIdea>

## Admission control

Every policy so far assumes an unbounded queue. Unbounded queues are how a system converts a brief
overload into an hour of timeouts: requests accepted during the spike are still being served long
after the users behind them left.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="admission-tradeoff" title="A bounded queue converts latency failure into rejection">
Cap the queue at $K$ waiting requests, rejecting arrivals that find it full. Then every *accepted*
request waits at most $K\,S_{\max}$, and for M/M/1/K the rejection probability is
$$
P_{\text{block}} \;=\; \frac{(1-\rho)\rho^{K}}{1-\rho^{K+1}} .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
The bound on waiting is immediate: an accepted request has at most $K$ ahead of it, each taking at
most $S_{\max}$. For the blocking probability, the truncated birth–death chain on states
$0,\dots,K$ has $\pi_n \propto \rho^n$ by the same detailed-balance argument as M/M/1, normalising
to $\pi_n = \rho^n(1-\rho)/(1-\rho^{K+1})$. By PASTA an arrival is blocked exactly when the chain
is in state $K$, giving $\pi_K$ as displayed.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
A rejection is a *fast, honest* failure; a timeout is a slow one that consumed capacity first.
Bounding the queue is therefore not a compromise but the mechanism by which a system stays inside
its latency SLO under overload — it trades an availability number, which the formula above lets you
compute, for a latency guarantee. Load shedding is the same idea with a priority ordering attached.
</KeyIdea>

<NotSaid>
Nothing here addresses fairness *between tenants*. SRPT and FCFS both treat the queue as
undifferentiated; a multi-tenant service usually needs a guarantee that one customer's burst cannot
consume another's capacity, which calls for a weighted-fair-queueing or token-bucket structure
layered above whatever within-class policy is used. Those mechanisms are about isolation, and
isolation is precisely what the pooling argument in
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/queueing-and-the-utilisation-wall" /> says you pay for in delay.
</NotSaid>
