<Intuition>
Availability arithmetic has two moves and they pull in opposite directions. Putting things in
*series* — a request that needs the router and the retriever and the model — multiplies
availabilities, so nines erode. Putting things in *parallel* — three replicas, any one of which
will do — multiplies *un*availabilities, so nines accumulate fast.

The catch is that the parallel calculation assumes replicas fail independently, and they mostly do
not. They share a deployment, a configuration, a certificate expiry, a dependency version. Once a
fixed fraction of failures hit every replica at once, adding replicas stops helping, and the
mathematics says exactly where that floor sits.
</Intuition>

## Series: nines erode

<Definition id="availability" title="Availability">
The **availability** $A$ of a component is the long-run fraction of requests (or of time) in which
it is functioning. Its complement $U = 1-A$ is the **unavailability**. "Four nines" means
$A = 0.9999$, i.e. $U = 10^{-4}$, i.e. about 53 minutes per year.
</Definition>

<Claim kind="proposition" id="series-availability" title="Availability of a dependency chain">
If a request requires $k$ components with independent availabilities $A_1,\dots,A_k$, the
end-to-end availability is
$$
A_{\text{series}} \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^k A_i ,
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
U_{\text{series}} \;=\; 1-\prod_i (1-U_i) \;\le\; \sum_{i=1}^k U_i ,
$$
so unavailabilities add to first order.
</Claim>

<Proof>
The request succeeds exactly when all components are up; independence gives the product. The
inequality is the union bound, or equivalently Bernoulli's inequality
$\prod_i(1-U_i) \ge 1-\sum_i U_i$.
</Proof>

Ten services each at three nines give $0.999^{10} = 0.99005$ — **two** nines, and about 87 hours of
annual downtime. Depth in a call graph is expensive, and this is the quantitative case for
collapsing dependencies on the critical path.

## Parallel: nines accumulate

<Claim kind="proposition" id="parallel-availability" title="Availability of independent replicas">
If $n$ replicas each have availability $A$ and fail independently, and the system needs at least
one,
$$
A_{\text{parallel}} \;=\; 1-(1-A)^n \;=\; 1-U^n .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
The system is down only if every replica is down, which by independence has probability $U^n$.
</Proof>

Two replicas at three nines give $1-10^{-6}$ — six nines. That is the calculation that makes
redundancy look irresistible, and it is the one that is wrong in practice.

## Quorums

<Claim kind="proposition" id="k-of-n" title="Quorum availability">
If the system requires at least $k$ of $n$ independent replicas,
$$
A_{k/n} \;=\; \sum_{j=k}^{n}\binom nj A^j (1-A)^{n-j} .
$$
This is decreasing in $k$; $k=1$ recovers the parallel formula and $k=n$ recovers the series
formula $A^n$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
The number of functioning replicas is Binomial$(n, A)$; the system is available exactly when that
count is at least $k$. The endpoints are immediate: the $k=n$ term is $A^n$, and summing from
$j=1$ gives $1 - (1-A)^n$.
</Proof>

<Note title="Why majority quorums cost availability">
A three-replica system needing a majority ($k=2$) has availability
$3A^2(1-A) + A^3$, which at $A = 0.99$ is $0.99970$ — better than one replica but far short of the
$0.9999$ that $k=1$ would give. Consensus protocols buy consistency with exactly this: they move
$k$ from $1$ to $\lfloor n/2\rfloor + 1$. The CAP theorem is the formal statement that this trade
cannot be avoided when the network may partition.
</Note>

## The floor

<Claim kind="theorem" id="common-mode-floor" title="Correlated failure puts a floor under redundancy">
Model failures with a common-cause fraction $\beta \in [0,1]$: with probability $\beta$ a failure
event takes down *every* replica simultaneously, and with probability $1-\beta$ replicas fail
independently. Writing $U$ for a single replica's unavailability, the $n$-replica system has
$$
U_n \;=\; (1-\beta)\,U^{\,n} \;+\; \beta\,U ,
$$
which is decreasing in $n$ with
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty} U_n \;=\; \beta\,U \; > \;0 \quad\text{whenever } \beta > 0 .
$$
No amount of redundancy reduces unavailability below $\beta U$.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
The beta-factor model is the simplest correlated-failure model: it splits failure causes into
"independent" and "hits everything", with no intermediate structure. Real correlation is graded —
a rack, a zone, a region — and is better modelled by a hierarchy. The theorem's conclusion survives
any such refinement, because the coarsest shared level plays the role of $\beta$.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Condition on the failure mode. With probability $\beta$ the failure is common-cause, and the system
is down exactly when the (shared) cause fires, contributing $\beta U$. With probability $1-\beta$
the replicas fail independently, and all $n$ must be down, contributing $(1-\beta)U^n$. Adding
gives $U_n$. Each term is non-increasing in $n$ and the first tends to $0$, leaving $\beta U$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Take $A = 0.999$, so $U = 10^{-3}$, and suppose just **1%** of failures are common-mode. Two
replicas give
$$
U_2 \;=\; 0.99\times10^{-6} + 0.01\times10^{-3} \;=\; 1.1\times10^{-5},
$$
against $10^{-6}$ under the independence assumption — an order of magnitude worse — and the floor
at $n=\infty$ is $10^{-5}$. **A 1% correlation costs more than the second replica buys.** This is
why availability work is dominated by *decorrelation* — staggered rollouts, separate control
planes, different certificate expiries — rather than by adding replicas.
</KeyIdea>

<Warning title="Deployments are the largest common-mode term">
A new binary rolled to every replica at once is a $\beta = 1$ event: it makes the replicas
perfectly correlated for the duration. Staged rollout exists precisely to reduce $\beta$, and the
theorem says that reducing $\beta$ is the only lever that moves the floor. The corollary for model
serving is that shipping a new checkpoint to all replicas simultaneously discards the availability
that replication was supposed to provide.
</Warning>

<NotSaid>
Availability is not latency. A replica that is up but slow counts as available in every formula
here, while failing the SLO in
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/what-a-serving-system-must-do" />. Systems with generous timeouts convert
latency failures into availability successes on the dashboard and into abandoned requests in
reality. The honest measure is the fraction of requests served *within the objective*, which makes
availability a special case of the latency SLO rather than a separate quantity.
</NotSaid>
