<Intuition>
A road at 50% occupancy flows freely. The same road at 95% occupancy is a traffic jam, and the
difference is not that twice as many cars appeared — only 1.9 times as many did. What changed is
that at high occupancy, every small fluctuation has nowhere to go. A gap absorbed instantly at low
load propagates backwards for miles at high load.

Servers do the same thing, and the mathematics says exactly how: the waiting time carries a factor
$1/(1-\rho)$. Going from 50% to 90% load multiplies queueing delay by 9. Going from 90% to 99%
multiplies it by 10 again. This is the single most important curve in capacity planning, and it is
why "the CPUs are only at 80%" is not the reassurance it sounds like.
</Intuition>

## The simplest queue, solved exactly

<Definition id="kendall" title="Kendall notation">
A queue is written $A/B/c$: $A$ the arrival process, $B$ the service distribution, $c$ the number of
servers. $M$ means *memoryless* — Poisson arrivals, or exponential service; $D$ means
deterministic; $G$ means a general distribution. So M/M/1 is Poisson arrivals, exponential service,
one server; M/G/1 relaxes the service distribution.
</Definition>

<Claim kind="theorem" id="mm1-stationary" title="Stationary distribution of M/M/1">
Let arrivals be Poisson with rate $\lambda$ and service times be exponential with rate $\mu$, one
server, first-come-first-served, infinite buffer. Write $\rho = \lambda/\mu$. If $\rho < 1$ the
number in system $N$ has stationary distribution
$$
\pi_n \ =\ (1-\rho)\,\rho^{\,n}, \qquad n = 0,1,2,\dots
$$
so $N$ is geometric, and $\E[N] = \dfrac{\rho}{1-\rho}$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
The process $N(t)$ is a birth–death chain: from state $n$ it jumps to $n+1$ at rate $\lambda$ and,
for $n \ge 1$, to $n-1$ at rate $\mu$. A birth–death chain is reversible, so the stationary
distribution satisfies the *detailed balance* equations
$$
\pi_n \lambda \ =\ \pi_{n+1}\, \mu \qquad (n \ge 0),
$$
which say that transitions across the cut between $\{0,\dots,n\}$ and $\{n+1,\dots\}$ balance.
Solving the recursion gives $\pi_n = \pi_0 \rho^n$. Normalising,
$$
1 \ =\ \sum_{n\ge0}\pi_0\rho^n \ =\ \frac{\pi_0}{1-\rho}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_0 = 1-\rho ,
$$
which requires $\rho<1$ for the geometric series to converge — this is the stability condition
again. For the mean, differentiate the geometric series: with $\sum_n \rho^n = (1-\rho)^{-1}$,
$$
\E[N] \;=\; (1-\rho)\sum_{n\ge0} n\rho^n \;=\; (1-\rho)\cdot \frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}
\;=\; \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}. \qquad\square
$$
</Proof>

<Claim kind="corollary" id="mm1-latency" title="Mean latency of M/M/1">
$$
\E[W] \;=\; \frac{1}{\mu-\lambda} \;=\; \frac{\E[S]}{1-\rho},
\qquad
\E[W_q] \;=\; \E[W]-\E[S] \;=\; \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\,\E[S].
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Little's law applied to the whole system gives $\E[N] = \lambda\E[W]$, so
$\E[W] = \E[N]/\lambda = \rho/(\lambda(1-\rho)) = 1/(\mu-\lambda)$, using $\rho = \lambda/\mu$. The
waiting time is the total minus the service, and $\E[S] = 1/\mu$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Mean latency is the service time multiplied by $1/(1-\rho)$. That factor is the **utilisation
wall**:

| $\rho$ | 0.5 | 0.8 | 0.9 | 0.95 | 0.99 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\E[W]/\E[S]$ | 2 | 5 | 10 | 20 | 100 |

The last 9% of capacity costs a factor of ten in latency. This is why serving systems are
provisioned at 50–70% utilisation and why the remaining headroom is not waste — it is what the
latency target is *purchased with*.
</KeyIdea>

## Variance is a second axis of load

Exponential service times are convenient, not realistic. Real inference has service times set by
output length, which is far from exponential. The general answer separates load from variability.

<Claim kind="theorem" id="pollaczek-khinchine" title="The Pollaczek–Khinchine mean formula">
For an M/G/1 queue — Poisson arrivals at rate $\lambda$, i.i.d. service times $S$ with finite
second moment, one server, work-conserving and non-preemptive with a discipline that does not use
service times — the mean waiting time in queue is
$$
\E[W_q] \;=\; \frac{\lambda\,\E[S^2]}{2\,(1-\rho)}, \qquad \rho = \lambda\,\E[S] < 1 .
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
- Arrivals are Poisson. This is what licenses the PASTA property used below; the formula genuinely
  fails for non-Poisson arrivals, where Kingman's approximation is the usual substitute.
- $\E[S^2] < \infty$. Service distributions with infinite second moment have infinite mean wait at
  *every* positive utilisation, which is not a pathology — it is what heavy-tailed request sizes do.
- The scheduler may not look at service times. Shortest-remaining-processing-time beats this
  formula, at the cost discussed at the end.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Condition on a tagged arrival. By PASTA (Poisson Arrivals See Time Averages), the arriving request
finds the system in its stationary state. Its wait is the *unfinished work* $V$ in the system on
arrival, since service is work-conserving and first-come-first-served, so $\E[W_q] = \E[V]$.

Decompose the unfinished work at a stationary instant into two pieces:

**Work from requests waiting in queue.** There are $\E[N_q] = \lambda\E[W_q]$ of them on average by
Little's law applied to the queue alone, each carrying a full service time $\E[S]$, contributing
$\lambda\E[W_q]\E[S] = \rho\,\E[W_q]$.

**Residual work of the request in service.** The server is busy a fraction $\rho$ of the time, and
given that it is busy, the expected *remaining* service time is the mean residual life of $S$,
which for a stationary renewal-type observation is $\E[S^2]/(2\E[S])$. This is the inspection
paradox: a random instant is more likely to land inside a long service. Its contribution is
$\rho \cdot \E[S^2]/(2\E[S])$.

Adding,
$$
\E[W_q] \;=\; \rho\,\E[W_q] \;+\; \rho\,\frac{\E[S^2]}{2\E[S]} .
$$
Solve, using $\rho = \lambda\E[S]$:
$$
\E[W_q](1-\rho) \;=\; \lambda\E[S]\cdot\frac{\E[S^2]}{2\E[S]} \;=\; \frac{\lambda\E[S^2]}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\E[W_q] \;=\; \frac{\lambda\E[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)} . \qquad\square
$$
</Proof>

Writing $\E[S^2] = \E[S]^2(1+c_s^2)$ with $c_s = \sqrt{\Var(S)}/\E[S]$ the coefficient of
variation puts the formula in its most usable form:
$$
\boxed{\ \E[W_q] \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}}_{\text{load}}\ \cdot\
\underbrace{\frac{1+c_s^2}{2}}_{\text{variability}}\ \cdot\ \underbrace{\E[S]}_{\text{scale}}\ }
$$

<Claim kind="corollary" id="variance-costs-as-much-as-load" title="Halving service variability halves the queue">
Exponential service has $c_s = 1$, so the variability factor is $1$ and
$\E[W_q] = \rho\E[S]/(1-\rho)$, recovering the M/M/1 result. Deterministic service has $c_s = 0$,
giving variability factor $1/2$:
$$
\E[W_q]^{\,M/D/1} \;=\; \tfrac12\,\E[W_q]^{\,M/M/1} .
$$
Making service times perfectly predictable is worth exactly as much as halving the queueing term —
at $\rho = 0.9$, it is worth as much as dropping utilisation to $0.82$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Substitute $c_s^2 = 1$ and $c_s^2 = 0$ into the boxed form. For the utilisation comparison, solve
$\rho'/(1-\rho') = \tfrac12 \cdot 0.9/(1-0.9) = 4.5$ for $\rho'$, giving
$\rho' = 4.5/5.5 \approx 0.818$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
There are two ways to shorten a queue and they are interchangeable: reduce load, or reduce
variability. In LLM serving the second is often the cheaper lever, because output-length variance
is enormous — a mix of 20-token and 2000-token generations has a coefficient of variation well
above 1. Splitting that traffic into separate pools by expected length reduces $c_s$ within each
pool, and the boxed formula says the queueing term falls proportionally.
</KeyIdea>

## When arrivals are not Poisson

<Claim kind="heuristic" id="kingman" title="Kingman's approximation">
For a G/G/1 queue with arrival-interval coefficient of variation $c_a$ and service coefficient of
variation $c_s$,
$$
\E[W_q] \;\approx\; \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\cdot\frac{c_a^2+c_s^2}{2}\cdot\E[S] .
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
This is an approximation, not a theorem. Kingman proved it is asymptotically exact in the
**heavy-traffic limit** $\rho \to 1$; at moderate utilisation it is a rule of thumb that is
typically right to within tens of percent, and it can be materially wrong when either distribution
is heavy-tailed. It is stated here because it is the formula practitioners actually use, and it
should be labelled for what it is.
</Assumptions>

<Note title="Why the same shape keeps appearing">
Every formula on this page factorises as load $\times$ variability $\times$ scale, with the load
factor $\rho/(1-\rho)$ common to all of them. That is not a coincidence of the models: the
$1/(1-\rho)$ arises from solving $\E[W_q] = \rho\E[W_q] + (\text{something})$, and that fixed-point
structure — work arriving while you wait, at rate $\rho$ — is present in any work-conserving queue.
</Note>

## More servers beat bigger servers

<Claim kind="proposition" id="pooling" title="Pooling servers reduces delay">
Compare $c$ separate M/M/1 queues each receiving $\lambda/c$, against one M/M/c queue receiving
$\lambda$ with the same total capacity. The pooled system has strictly smaller mean waiting time at
every $\rho \in (0,1)$ and $c \ge 2$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
For the separated system each sub-queue has arrival rate $\lambda/c$ and service rate $\mu$, so
utilisation $\rho = \lambda/(c\mu)$ and, by the corollary above,
$\E[W_q]^{\text{sep}} = \rho\E[S]/(1-\rho)$.

For the pooled M/M/c system, a request waits only if all $c$ servers are busy. Let
$P_{\text{wait}}$ denote that stationary probability (the Erlang C formula). Given that it waits,
the system empties a server at rate $c\mu$, so the residual wait is exponential with mean
$1/(c\mu - \lambda)$, and
$$
\E[W_q]^{\text{pool}} \;=\; \frac{P_{\text{wait}}}{c\mu-\lambda}
\;=\; P_{\text{wait}}\cdot\frac{\E[S]}{c\,(1-\rho)} .
$$
Since $P_{\text{wait}} \le 1$ we get $\E[W_q]^{\text{pool}} \le \E[S]/(c(1-\rho))$, whereas the
separated system pays $\rho\E[S]/(1-\rho)$ — larger by a factor $c\rho/P_{\text{wait}} \ge c\rho$.
For $c \ge 2$ and $\rho$ not vanishing this is a strict improvement, and the gap grows with $c$.
</Proof>

Numerically, at $\rho = 0.8$: one server waits $4\E[S]$; four pooled servers wait $0.75\E[S]$;
sixteen pooled servers wait $0.095\E[S]$. Same utilisation, same total capacity, a
forty-fold difference in delay.

<Warning title="Pooling assumes interchangeable servers">
The pooling gain evaporates if requests cannot go to any server — which is exactly the situation
when each replica holds a different model, a different LoRA adapter, or a warm KV cache for a
particular conversation. Affinity requirements re-partition a pooled queue back into separate ones,
and the delay penalty above is the price. This is the central tension in multi-tenant model
serving: cache locality pulls towards affinity, queueing theory pulls towards pooling.
</Warning>

<NotSaid>
None of this bounds the *tail*. The formulas here are all statements about $\E[W_q]$. The
geometric stationary distribution of M/M/1 does give the full law in that one case — and it is
exponential, so its p99 is about $4.6$ times its mean — but M/G/1 tails depend on the service
distribution in ways the mean formula cannot see. Tail behaviour under fan-out is taken up in
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/tail-latency-and-fan-out" />.
</NotSaid>
