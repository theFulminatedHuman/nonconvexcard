<Intuition>
An autoscaler cannot react to a load spike until it has observed one, and cannot serve the spike
until the new capacity is ready. Between those two moments the system is under-provisioned by
construction, and the backlog it accrues is fixed by arithmetic, not by how clever the controller
is. Everything a scaling policy can influence is what happens *after* that.

The consequence is uncomfortable and unavoidable: a system that autoscales must still carry
capacity it is not using, sized to cover the delay. Autoscaling reduces the *average* cost of that
headroom; it does not remove the need for it.
</Intuition>

## What the delay costs

<Claim kind="proposition" id="backlog-during-delay" title="Backlog accumulated during the scaling delay">
Suppose the system has capacity $\mu$ and offered load steps from $\lambda_0 < \mu$ to
$\lambda_1 > \mu$ at time $0$, and that new capacity becomes available only at time $d$. Then the
backlog at time $d$ is
$$
Q(d) \;=\; (\lambda_1-\mu)\,d ,
$$
independent of the scaling policy.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Over $[0,d]$ arrivals total $\lambda_1 d$ and departures at most $\mu d$, with equality while the
server is saturated, which it is since $\lambda_1 > \mu$. The difference is the backlog. No action
taken during $[0,d]$ changes $\mu$, by the definition of the delay.
</Proof>

## How long the system stays behind

<Claim kind="theorem" id="recovery-time" title="Recovery time after a load step">
With capacity raised to $\mu' > \lambda_1$ at time $d$, the backlog clears at
$$
T_{\text{recover}} \;=\; d \;+\; \frac{Q(d)}{\mu'-\lambda_1}
\;=\; d\,\cdot\,\frac{\mu'-\mu}{\mu'-\lambda_1} .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
After $d$ the queue drains at net rate $\mu'-\lambda_1 > 0$, so clearing $Q(d) = (\lambda_1-\mu)d$
takes $(\lambda_1-\mu)d/(\mu'-\lambda_1)$. Adding $d$ and putting over a common denominator,
$$
d\left(1 + \frac{\lambda_1-\mu}{\mu'-\lambda_1}\right)
= d\,\frac{(\mu'-\lambda_1)+(\lambda_1-\mu)}{\mu'-\lambda_1}
= d\,\frac{\mu'-\mu}{\mu'-\lambda_1}. \qquad\square
$$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Read the formula as a warning about *timid* scaling. Scaling to $\mu'$ only just above
$\lambda_1$ makes the denominator small and the recovery time blow up: provisioning 10% above the
new load when the step was 50% gives
$T_{\text{recover}} = d\,(1.5\cdot 1.1 - 1)/(1.65-1.5)$ — with $\mu=1$, $\lambda_1 = 1.5$,
$\mu' = 1.65$ — which is $d \times 0.65/0.15 \approx 4.3\,d$. Overshooting to $\mu' = 3$ gives
$d \times 2/1.5 \approx 1.3\,d$. **Deliberate overshoot is how a scaler recovers quickly**, and it
is cheap because it is transient.
</KeyIdea>

## The headroom you must always carry

<Claim kind="corollary" id="headroom-requirement" title="Standing headroom needed to absorb a step">
To absorb an instantaneous load step by a factor $g > 1$ with no backlog at all, the pre-step
utilisation must satisfy
$$
\rho_0 \;=\; \frac{\lambda_0}{\mu} \;\le\; \frac 1g .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
No backlog requires $\lambda_1 = g\lambda_0 \le \mu$, i.e. $\lambda_0/\mu \le 1/g$.
</Proof>

So absorbing a doubling requires running at 50%; absorbing a 4× spike requires 25%. Combined with
the utilisation wall — which already forbids running near 1 — this is the quantitative case
against "scale to zero and react", and it explains why serving fleets look over-provisioned when
measured by average utilisation alone.

<Claim kind="heuristic" id="scale-on-queue-not-utilisation" title="Scale on queueing delay rather than utilisation">
Utilisation is bounded above by $1$ and saturates: at $\rho = 0.95$ and $\rho = 0.999$ it reads
almost the same, while latency differs by a factor of $20$. Queue depth and queueing delay are
unbounded and grow monotonically with excess load, so they distinguish the two. A controller that
targets queueing delay therefore has a usable error signal in exactly the regime where a
utilisation-based controller has none.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
This is an argument from the shape of the signals, not a theorem about a specific controller. Its
force depends on the delay measurement being timely: a queueing-delay estimate averaged over a
window longer than $d$ reintroduces the blindness it was meant to remove.
</Assumptions>

<Warning title="Delay plus feedback is how oscillation starts">
A controller that reacts to a signal delayed by $d$ with a gain high enough to correct the whole
observed error will overcorrect, because part of the error it is reacting to has already been
addressed by actions still in flight. The standard symptoms — capacity sawtoothing, alternating
over- and under-provisioning with period roughly $2d$ — are not tuning noise but the expected
behaviour of a delayed feedback loop with too much gain. The defences are the usual ones: reduce
the gain, add a cooldown longer than $d$, or make the controller predictive rather than reactive.
</Warning>

<NotSaid>
This page models capacity as a number that changes at time $d$. For model serving it is not: a new
replica must load tens of gigabytes of weights and warm its caches before it is useful, so there is
a further interval during which the replica exists, consumes quota, and serves nothing — and if the
load balancer routes to it on readiness rather than warmth, it will serve that traffic slowly.
Weight-loading time is the dominant term in $d$ for LLM serving, which is why snapshotting and
pre-warming pools matter more here than in a stateless web tier.
</NotSaid>
