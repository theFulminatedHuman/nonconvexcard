<Intuition>
There are only three things you can cut when a model will not fit on one device: the batch, the
individual matrices, or the stack of layers. Cutting the batch means every device holds the whole
model and they must agree on the gradient. Cutting the matrices means a single layer's arithmetic
is spread across devices, so they must exchange activations inside every layer. Cutting the layer
stack means each device owns a contiguous slice of depth, and activations flow between neighbours.

Each cut produces a different communication pattern, and — this is the useful part — each is
amortised by a *different* quantity. Data parallelism gets cheaper with more tokens per device.
Tensor parallelism gets cheaper with a wider model. Pipeline parallelism gets cheaper with more
microbatches. That is why large training runs use all three at once rather than picking a favourite.
</Intuition>

## Counting rules

Throughout, let $N$ be the parameter count, $d$ the model width, $L$ the layer count, $F$ the
achievable FLOP rate per device, $B$ the achievable interconnect bandwidth per device, and $b$ the
bytes per element communicated. For a decoder-only transformer we use the standard counts
established in <TopicRef id="llm-ops/inference-arithmetic" />:

- parameters per layer $\approx 12d^2$ (attention $4d^2$, MLP $8d^2$), so $N \approx 12Ld^2$;
- training cost $\approx 6N$ FLOPs per token (forward $2N$, backward $4N$).

## Data parallelism

Every device holds a full replica and processes a different slice of the batch. After the backward
pass the gradients must be summed across replicas — one all-reduce of $N$ elements per step.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="dp-ratio" title="Data-parallel communication does not depend on model size">
Let each device process $T$ tokens per optimiser step. Using a ring all-reduce, whose per-device
volume is at most $2Nb$ bytes,
$$
\frac{T_{\text{comm}}}{T_{\text{compute}}}
\;=\; \frac{2Nb/B}{6NT/F}
\;=\; \frac{b\,F}{3\,B\,T} .
$$
The parameter count cancels. The ratio depends only on the hardware ratio $F/B$, the gradient
precision $b$, and the tokens per device per step.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
- The all-reduce is bandwidth-bound, so its latency term $2(p-1)\alpha$ is negligible. For a
  multi-gigabyte gradient this holds; for small models it does not, and the latency term restores a
  dependence on $p$.
- Communication is not overlapped with computation. Overlapping changes the conclusion from "this
  is the cost" to "this is the amount that must be hidden", which is the same number.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Compute time per step is $6NT/F$ by the $6N$-FLOPs-per-token rule. Communication time is the
all-reduce volume $\frac{2(p-1)}{p}Nb \le 2Nb$ divided by $B$, using the ring cost from
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/collective-communication" />. Dividing,
$$
\frac{2Nb/B}{6NT/F} \;=\; \frac{2Nb}{B}\cdot\frac{F}{6NT} \;=\; \frac{bF}{3BT},
$$
and $N$ appears in both numerator and denominator.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Data parallelism is amortised by **tokens per device per step**. Doubling the per-device
microbatch halves the relative cost of gradient synchronisation. This is why data-parallel training
degrades at small per-device batch — and why gradient accumulation, which raises $T$ without
raising memory for activations proportionally, is the standard fix.
</KeyIdea>

## Tensor parallelism

Each weight matrix is sharded across $t$ devices, so a single layer's matmuls are computed jointly.
The Megatron decomposition requires one all-reduce of the activation tensor after the attention
block and one after the MLP block, in the forward pass, and the same in the backward pass.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="tp-ratio" title="Tensor-parallel communication does not depend on batch size">
With $T$ tokens in flight, a hidden size $d$, and $4$ all-reduces per layer per step (two forward,
two backward) each of $Td$ elements,
$$
\frac{T_{\text{comm}}}{T_{\text{compute}}}
\;=\; \frac{4L\cdot 2Tdb/B}{6\,(12Ld^2)\,T/F}
\;=\; \frac{8LTdb/B}{72Ld^2T/F}
\;=\; \frac{b\,F}{9\,d\,B} .
$$
Both $L$ and $T$ cancel. The ratio falls like $1/d$: wider models tolerate tensor parallelism
better.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Each all-reduce moves at most $2 \cdot Tdb$ bytes per device by the ring bound. With $4L$ of them
per step the communication time is $8LTdb/B$. Compute time is $6NT/F$ with $N = 12Ld^2$, giving
$72Ld^2T/F$. Dividing and cancelling $L$, $T$ and the factor $8/72 = 1/9$ yields the display.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Tensor parallelism is a within-node strategy">
Because the ratio is $bF/(9dB)$ and $d$ is only a few thousand, the required bandwidth $B$ is
enormous — this collective happens four times per layer per step rather than once per step. With
$F/B$ of order a hundred (a GPU's FLOP rate against a PCIe link) the ratio exceeds one and
communication dominates. Over NVLink, where $F/B$ is an order of magnitude smaller, it does not.
This is the quantitative reason tensor-parallel groups are kept inside a node and data-parallel
groups span nodes.
</Warning>

## Pipeline parallelism

The layer stack is cut into $p$ contiguous stages, one per device. A batch is split into $m$
microbatches which flow through the stages; only activations at stage boundaries are communicated,
which is a small point-to-point transfer rather than a collective. The cost is not bandwidth but
*idleness*: at the start, stages downstream have nothing to do, and at the end, upstream stages are
finished.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="pipeline-bubble" title="The pipeline bubble fraction">
With $p$ stages, $m$ microbatches, and equal per-stage time $\tau$ per microbatch, the schedule
completes in $(m+p-1)\tau$ against an ideal $m\tau$. The fraction of wall-clock time lost to idle
stages is
$$
\text{bubble} \;=\; \frac{p-1}{m+p-1},
$$
and the overhead relative to the ideal is $(p-1)/m$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Stage $j$ (indexed from $0$) cannot begin microbatch $i$ until stage $j-1$ has finished it, so it
starts at time $(i+j)\tau$. The last stage finishes the last microbatch at time
$(m-1+p-1)\tau + \tau = (m+p-1)\tau$. The ideal — all $p$ stages busy throughout — would be
$m\tau$. The lost fraction is
$$
\frac{(m+p-1)\tau - m\tau}{(m+p-1)\tau} \;=\; \frac{p-1}{m+p-1},
$$
and dividing the numerator by the ideal $m\tau$ instead gives $(p-1)/m$.
</Proof>

With $p = 4$ and $m = 8$ the bubble is $3/11 \approx 27\%$; raising $m$ to $32$ brings it to
$3/35 \approx 8.6\%$.

<KeyIdea>
Pipeline parallelism is amortised by **microbatch count**. But $m$ cannot grow freely: every
in-flight microbatch holds its activations for the backward pass, so memory grows with $m$. The
bubble and the activation memory pull in opposite directions, and that trade is what interleaved
and 1F1B schedules exist to improve.
</KeyIdea>

## Why all three

<Claim kind="corollary" id="strategy-selection" title="Why the three are combined rather than chosen between">
The three overheads are amortised by three different quantities:
$$
\text{data} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{T},
\qquad
\text{tensor} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{d},
\qquad
\text{pipeline} \;\propto\; \frac{p-1}{m} .
$$
Since $d$ is fixed by the model and $T$ and $m$ are bounded by memory, no single axis can be pushed
to absorb an arbitrary device count; minimising total overhead subject to a fixed device budget
$p_{\text{total}} = p_{\text{data}} \cdot p_{\text{tensor}} \cdot p_{\text{pipe}}$ therefore has an
interior optimum in general.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Each expression is the proposition above it. The budget constraint is multiplicative because the
three decompositions are orthogonal — a device is identified by its coordinates in the
(data, tensor, pipeline) grid. A pure strategy sets two of the three factors to $1$, forcing the
third to absorb all of $p_{\text{total}}$; since tensor overhead is independent of $p_{\text{tensor}}$
only until bandwidth saturates, and pipeline overhead grows like $p_{\text{pipe}}-1$, and data
overhead is flat in $p_{\text{data}}$ only while the all-reduce stays bandwidth-bound, each pure
strategy hits a wall that the mixed configuration avoids.
</Proof>

<Note title="What ZeRO changes">
Sharding optimiser state, gradients and parameters across data-parallel ranks removes the memory
redundancy of pure data parallelism without changing the arithmetic above: the gradient still has
to be reduced and the parameters still have to be available where they are used. The communication
volume rises from one all-reduce to a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather — the same
$2\frac{p-1}{p}N$ total — with parameters gathered on demand adding a further all-gather per step
in its most aggressive stage. The trade is memory for bandwidth, at a ratio the propositions above
let you price.
</Note>

<NotSaid>
This page counts bytes and FLOPs. It says nothing about *convergence*: a configuration that
minimises time per step can still be worse per unit of progress, because the global batch size it
implies may sit past the point where larger batches stop helping. Step time and steps-to-target are
separate quantities, and a parallelism plan that optimises only the first is incomplete.
</NotSaid>
