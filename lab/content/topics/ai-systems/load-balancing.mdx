<Intuition>
Send each request to a uniformly random server and the servers do *not* end up evenly loaded. With
$n$ requests and $n$ servers, the busiest server gets about $\log n / \log\log n$ of them — for
$n = 100{,}000$ that is roughly five, against an average of one. Randomness clumps.

Now change one thing: sample *two* servers at random and send the request to whichever has fewer
outstanding requests. The busiest server now gets about $\log\log n / \log 2$ — for the same
$n = 100{,}000$, roughly three and a half. One extra probe converts a logarithm into a
double logarithm. Almost nothing else in system design has that shape of return.
</Intuition>

## One choice

<Claim kind="theorem" id="random-max-load" title="Maximum load under uniform random assignment">
Throw $n$ balls independently and uniformly into $n$ bins. Then with probability tending to $1$,
the maximum load is
$$
\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\,\frac{\ln n}{\ln \ln n} .
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
The matching lower bound is a second-moment argument that we do not reproduce; it is the harder
half and is given in the cited literature. The upper bound is proved below, which is the direction
a system designer needs — it says the imbalance is no worse than this.
</Assumptions>

<Claim kind="proposition" id="upper-bound-one-choice" title="A log n / log log n upper bound for one choice">
With $n$ balls in $n$ bins, for $k \ge 1$,
$$
\Pr[\text{some bin has} \ge k \text{ balls}] \;\le\; \frac{n}{k!} .
$$
Consequently, taking $k = \lceil e\ln n/\ln\ln n\rceil$ makes this $o(1)$, so the maximum load is
$O(\ln n/\ln\ln n)$ with high probability.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Fix a bin and a set of $k$ balls. The probability that all $k$ land in that bin is $n^{-k}$. Union
bounding over the $\binom nk$ sets of balls and the $n$ bins,
$$
\Pr[\text{some bin has} \ge k] \;\le\; n\binom nk n^{-k} \;\le\; n\cdot\frac{n^k}{k!}\cdot n^{-k}
\;=\; \frac{n}{k!},
$$
using $\binom nk \le n^k/k!$. To make $n/k! = o(1)$ we need $k! \gg n$, i.e.
$\ln k! \gg \ln n$. By Stirling $\ln k! = k\ln k - k + O(\ln k)$, so it suffices that
$k \ln k \ge (1+\eps)\ln n$; taking $k = e\ln n/\ln\ln n$ gives
$k\ln k \approx e\ln n \cdot \frac{\ln\ln n - \ln\ln\ln n + 1}{\ln \ln n} \sim e \ln n > \ln n$,
which is more than enough.
</Proof>

## Two choices

<Claim kind="theorem" id="two-choices" title="The power of two choices">
Throw $n$ balls into $n$ bins, each ball choosing $d \ge 2$ bins uniformly at random and going into
the least loaded of them (ties broken arbitrarily). Then with probability tending to $1$ the
maximum load is
$$
\frac{\ln\ln n}{\ln d} \;+\; \Theta(1) .
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
Stated without proof; the argument is a layered-induction bound on the number of bins with load at
least $k$, given in full in the cited paper. What matters here is the *shape*: $\ln\ln n$ rather
than $\ln n/\ln\ln n$, and a further improvement only in the constant $1/\ln d$ as $d$ grows beyond
two.
</Assumptions>

Simulating $n$ balls into $n$ bins, averaged over five runs:

| $n$ | $d=1$ max load | $\ln n/\ln\ln n$ | $d=2$ max load | $\ln\ln n/\ln 2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10^3$ | 5.8 | 3.57 | 3.0 | 2.79 |
| $10^4$ | 6.6 | 4.15 | 3.0 | 3.20 |
| $10^5$ | 7.8 | 4.71 | 3.2 | 3.53 |

<KeyIdea>
The gain from $d=1$ to $d=2$ is exponential; the gain from $d=2$ to $d=3$ is a factor
$\ln 3/\ln 2 \approx 1.58$ in the leading term, and from $d=3$ onward it is diminishing. **Two
probes is the whole trick.** This is why production balancers sample a small constant number of
backends rather than maintaining a global ordering — the global information is worth almost
nothing beyond what two random samples already provide, and it costs a coherency term of the kind
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/scaling-limits" /> warns about.
</KeyIdea>

## What to measure when you probe

<Claim kind="proposition" id="round-robin-vs-loaded" title="Round robin ignores the information that matters">
Let servers be identical and let request $i$ have service time $S_i$. Round robin equalises the
*count* of requests routed to each server. Least-outstanding-requests equalises the number
currently in service. These coincide only if the $S_i$ are equal; in general, by Little's law
applied per server, the occupancy of server $j$ satisfies
$$
L_j \;=\; X_j\,\E[W_j],
$$
so equalising $L_j$ equalises the product of throughput and latency — the actual load — whereas
equalising the routed count equalises $X_j$ alone.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Little's law applied to server $j$ as a sub-system gives $L_j = X_j \E[W_j]$ with no distributional
assumptions (see <TopicRef id="ai-systems/littles-law" />). Round robin fixes the arrival counts,
hence $X_j$, leaving $\E[W_j]$ free to vary with the service-time draws that happened to land
there. Least-outstanding-requests fixes $L_j$ directly.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
For LLM serving the service-time coefficient of variation is large — generations differ in length
by orders of magnitude — so round robin and occupancy-based balancing are not close. By the
Pollaczek–Khinchine formula in
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/queueing-and-the-utilisation-wall" />, queueing delay scales with
$1+c_s^2$; routing on counts lets that variance land unevenly, while routing on occupancy absorbs
it.
</KeyIdea>

<Warning title="Stale load information is worse than none">
Choosing the least loaded of $d$ servers using *cached* load values causes herding: every balancer
sees the same stale minimum and sends everything there, creating the imbalance it was avoiding.
The power-of-two result assumes the sampled loads are current at decision time. When they cannot
be, randomisation is the defence — which is again an argument for sampling a couple of servers per
request rather than consulting a shared ranking.
</Warning>

<NotSaid>
The theorems here are about *static* balls into bins, where balls never leave. The queueing version
— the supermarket model, where jobs arrive and depart — is analysed in the second reference and has
the same qualitative conclusion, with the maximum queue length under $d = 2$ decaying doubly
exponentially in the load rather than singly. The static statement is quoted because it is the one
with a clean, checkable form; the dynamic statement is the one that actually applies to a serving
system.
</NotSaid>
