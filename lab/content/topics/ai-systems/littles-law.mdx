<Intuition>
Imagine photographing a queue at random moments and counting the people in the picture. If people
arrive twice a second and each stays five seconds, the photographs will average ten people. Nothing
about *how* the queue is served enters that calculation — not the order, not the number of
servers, not whether service times are wild or uniform. Arrivals times duration equals occupancy.

That triviality is the single most useful identity in capacity planning, because it is an identity:
it cannot be violated by a clever scheduler, and any measurement that appears to violate it is a
measurement error.
</Intuition>

## The law

<Claim kind="theorem" id="littles-law" title="Little's law">
Consider any system into which items arrive and from which they eventually depart. Let
$N(t)$ be the number present at time $t$, let $A(T)$ be the number of arrivals in $[0,T]$, and let
$W_i$ be the time item $i$ spends in the system. Suppose the limits
$$
\lambda = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{A(T)}{T},
\qquad
\overline{W} = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac 1n \sum_{i=1}^n W_i
$$
exist and are finite. Then the time-average occupancy exists and equals their product:
$$
\overline{L} \ =\ \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\int_0^T N(t)\,dt \ =\ \lambda \overline{W} .
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
- Every item that arrives eventually departs, and no item is present forever.
- The two limits above exist and are finite. This is the only substantive hypothesis.
- **Nothing else.** In particular: no assumption that arrivals are Poisson, that service times are
  exponential or independent, that service is first-come-first-served, that there is one server, or
  that the system is even work-conserving. The law is a sample-path identity, not a probabilistic
  one.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Write $a_i$ for the arrival time of item $i$ and $d_i = a_i + W_i$ for its departure. The indicator
that item $i$ is present at time $t$ is $\one\{a_i \le t < d_i\}$, so
$$
N(t) \ =\ \sum_{i \ge 1} \one\{a_i \le t < d_i\} .
$$
Integrate over $[0,T]$ and exchange the (non-negative) sum and integral by Tonelli:
$$
\int_0^T N(t)\,dt \ =\ \sum_{i\ge1} \bigl| [a_i, d_i) \cap [0,T] \bigr| .
$$
Both sides count the same area in the $(i,t)$ plane: the left by vertical slices at each time, the
right by horizontal bars for each item. That is the whole content of the argument.

Now bound the right-hand side. Every item that arrives *and departs* within $[0,T]$ contributes its
full $W_i$; items still present at $T$ contribute less; no item contributes more than $W_i$. Hence
$$
\sum_{i : d_i \le T} W_i \ \le\ \int_0^T N(t)\,dt \ \le\ \sum_{i : a_i \le T} W_i .
$$
Divide by $T$. For the upper bound, $a_i \le T$ means $i \le A(T)$, so
$$
\frac1T \int_0^T N(t)\,dt \ \le\ \frac{A(T)}{T}\cdot \frac{1}{A(T)}\sum_{i=1}^{A(T)} W_i
\ \longrightarrow\ \lambda \overline{W},
$$
using $A(T)\to\infty$ (which follows from $\lambda>0$; if $\lambda = 0$ both sides are $0$). The
lower bound is the same computation applied to the departure counting process $D(T)$, which
satisfies $D(T)/T \to \lambda$ because the number in system $N(T) = A(T)-D(T)$ is $o(T)$ — itself a
consequence of $\overline W < \infty$. The two bounds squeeze, giving the identity.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Little's law relates three quantities, so it determines any one from the other two. That is how it
is used in practice: measure the two you can measure, and deduce the third. The most common
application is the one you *cannot* measure directly — average latency inside a component that does
not emit per-request timings — from occupancy and arrival rate, both of which are easy counters.
</KeyIdea>

## Two immediate corollaries

Apply the law to a sub-system rather than the whole, and it gives something new each time. The
trick is choosing the boundary.

<Claim kind="corollary" id="utilisation-law" title="The utilisation law">
Draw the boundary around the servers only, excluding the queue. Items "arrive" at that sub-system
when service begins and "depart" when it ends, so the arrival rate is the throughput $X$ and the
time in the sub-system is the service time $S$. Little's law gives
$$
\overline{L}_{\text{busy}} \ =\ X\,\E[S],
$$
where $\overline{L}_{\text{busy}}$ is the average number of *busy* servers. Dividing by the number
of servers $c$,
$$
\rho \ =\ \frac{X\,\E[S]}{c} .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
The sub-system contains exactly the items currently in service, so $N_{\text{busy}}(t)$ is the
number of busy servers. In steady state every item that begins service finishes it, and items begin
service at the same long-run rate they complete, namely $X$. Applying the theorem with
$\lambda \mapsto X$ and $\overline W \mapsto \E[S]$ gives the first display; utilisation is by
definition the fraction of servers busy, $\overline L_{\text{busy}}/c$.
</Proof>

<Claim kind="corollary" id="concurrency-sizing" title="Concurrency needed for a throughput target">
To sustain throughput $X$ when each request takes $W$ seconds end to end, the system must hold
$$
L \ =\ X\,W
$$
requests concurrently. Conversely, a system whose concurrency is capped at $L_{\max}$ — by a
thread pool, a connection limit, or the KV-cache memory of a GPU — cannot exceed throughput
$L_{\max}/W$ no matter how much offered load it receives.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Immediate from the theorem with $\lambda = X$, which holds in steady state because arrivals and
departures balance. The converse follows by rearranging: $X = L/W \le L_{\max}/W$.
</Proof>

## Working the numbers

The corollary above is the sizing rule for an inference server, and it is worth doing concretely.

Suppose one replica must sustain $X = 20$ requests per second, and a request occupies the replica
for $W = 4$ seconds — a 500-token generation at 8 ms per token, say, plus prefill and queueing.
Then
$$
L \ =\ 20 \times 4 \ =\ 80
$$
requests must be resident simultaneously. Every one of them holds a KV cache. Using the KV-cache
arithmetic from <TopicRef id="llm-ops/kv-cache-and-batching" />, that resident count multiplied by
the per-sequence cache size is a hard memory requirement — and if the GPU cannot hold 80 sequences'
worth, the target throughput is unreachable on that replica at that latency, whatever the batching
implementation does.

<Note title="The three ways to read one equation">
$L = XW$ can be read as three different design statements, and each is used:
1. **Sizing** — "I want $X$ at latency $W$, so I must provision for $L$ concurrent requests."
2. **Diagnosis** — "Concurrency is pinned at $L_{\max}$ and latency is rising, so throughput has
   stopped growing" — the signature of a saturated system.
3. **Inference of the unmeasurable** — "Queue depth averages 80 and we complete 20 per second, so
   requests are spending 4 seconds inside," with no per-request instrumentation at all.
</Note>

<Warning title="Little's law does not predict latency">
The law is an identity among three averages: given any two it returns the third. It does **not**
tell you what $W$ will be at a given load — that requires knowing how the queue behaves, which is
the subject of <TopicRef id="ai-systems/queueing-and-the-utilisation-wall" />. A common error is to
increase the concurrency limit $L_{\max}$ expecting throughput to rise proportionally. If the
bottleneck is the server rather than the limit, raising $L_{\max}$ raises $W$ instead, and $X$ does
not move at all.
</Warning>

<NotSaid>
Little's law says nothing about variability. Two systems with identical $L$, $X$ and $W$ can have
completely different latency distributions — one tight around 4 seconds, the other mostly instant
with a heavy tail. Since <TopicRef id="ai-systems/what-a-serving-system-must-do" /> established that
the mean constrains the p99 only by a factor of $1/(1-p)$, an identity about means is exactly as
informative about the tail as that factor permits, and no more.
</NotSaid>
