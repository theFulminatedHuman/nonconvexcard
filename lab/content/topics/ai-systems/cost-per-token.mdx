<Intuition>
Hardware is billed by the hour whether it is working or not. So the cost of a token is the cost of
the hour divided by the number of tokens that hour actually produced — and the second factor, not
the first, is where the variance is. Two operators with identical GPUs at identical prices can
differ by an order of magnitude in unit cost, entirely through utilisation and batch size.

The uncomfortable part is that the biggest lever, utilisation, is capped by the latency promise.
Cost and latency are not independent axes to be optimised separately; they are two readings of the
same number, and the exchange rate between them is computable.
</Intuition>

## The unit cost

<Claim kind="proposition" id="unit-cost" title="Cost per token">
Let a replica cost $c$ per hour, have maximum sustainable output $X_{\max}$ tokens per second, and
run at utilisation $\rho$. Then
$$
\text{cost per token} \;=\; \frac{c}{3600\;\rho\,X_{\max}} .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
In one hour the replica is billed $c$ and delivers $3600\,\rho X_{\max}$ tokens, since $\rho$ is by
definition the fraction of capacity actually used. Dividing gives the display.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Three multiplicative factors, and only one of them is a procurement decision:

$$
\text{cost per token} \;=\; \underbrace{c}_{\text{what you pay}} \times
\underbrace{\frac{1}{X_{\max}}}_{\text{how fast it can go}} \times
\underbrace{\frac{1}{\rho}}_{\text{how much you use it}} \times \frac{1}{3600}
$$

A replica at 30% utilisation costs $3.3\times$ per token what the same replica costs at 100%.
Negotiating 20% off the hourly rate is worth less than moving utilisation from 50% to 65%.
</KeyIdea>

## What a latency target costs

Utilisation cannot be pushed freely, because latency rises with it. Making that explicit turns the
cost model into a statement about the latency target itself.

<Claim kind="theorem" id="cost-of-latency" title="The cost of a latency target">
Model a replica as a single-server queue with mean service time $S$ and mean latency
$W = S/(1-\rho)$. Then achieving a mean-latency target $W$ requires $\rho = 1 - S/W$, and
$$
\text{cost per token} \;=\; \frac{c}{3600\,X_{\max}}\cdot\frac{W}{W-S} .
$$
The factor $W/(W-S)$ is decreasing in $W$, equals $1$ in the limit of an unbounded latency budget,
and diverges as $W \downarrow S$.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
$W = S/(1-\rho)$ is the M/M/1 relation from
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/queueing-and-the-utilisation-wall" />. A different service distribution
changes the constant through the variability factor $(1+c_s^2)/2$ but not the shape: every
work-conserving queue has a $1/(1-\rho)$ term, so every such system has a cost that diverges as the
latency target approaches the bare service time.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Invert $W = S/(1-\rho)$ to get $1-\rho = S/W$, so $\rho = 1 - S/W = (W-S)/W$. Substituting into
the unit-cost proposition, $1/\rho = W/(W-S)$.
</Proof>

Reading off the exchange rate, with $S$ as the unit:

| latency target $W$ | $2S$ | $3S$ | $4S$ | $10S$ | $\infty$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| utilisation $\rho$ | 0.50 | 0.67 | 0.75 | 0.90 | 1 |
| relative cost | 2.00 | 1.50 | 1.33 | 1.11 | 1 |

<KeyIdea>
Tightening the mean-latency target from $4S$ to $2S$ raises unit cost by
$2.00/1.33 = 1.5$ — **50% more expensive**, for a $2\times$ latency improvement. That is the whole
trade, in one number, and it is why interactive and batch traffic belong in separate pools: batch
work has $W = \infty$ and should be run at the utilisation that implies, subsidising nothing and
being subsidised by nothing.
</KeyIdea>

## Where $X_{\max}$ comes from

<Claim kind="corollary" id="batching-cost" title="Why batching dominates the cost structure">
In weight-bound decoding, each decode step streams the weights once regardless of batch size, so a
step takes time $\approx N b / B_{\text{mem}}$ and emits $B$ tokens. Hence
$$
X_{\max} \;\approx\; \frac{B\,B_{\text{mem}}}{N\,b},
$$
linear in the batch $B$, and unit cost falls like $1/B$ until the arithmetic units saturate.
</Claim>

<Proof>
The decode-step time and arithmetic intensity are established in
<TopicRef id="llm-ops/inference-arithmetic" />: with $N$ parameters at $b$ bytes each and memory
bandwidth $B_{\text{mem}}$, a weight-limited step costs $Nb/B_{\text{mem}}$ seconds and produces
one token for each of the $B$ sequences in the batch. Dividing tokens by seconds gives $X_{\max}$;
substituting into the unit-cost proposition gives a cost proportional to $1/B$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Unit cost is inversely proportional to batch size, and by
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/capacity-planning" /> the achievable batch is
$L_{\max} = \eta M_{\text{kv}}/m$ — a *memory* quantity. So the dominant lever on cost per token is
KV-cache footprint, not FLOPs. Halving $m$ through grouped-query attention or KV quantisation
halves unit cost in the memory-bound regime, which no amount of arithmetic throughput would have
done.
</KeyIdea>

<Warning title="Prefill and decode have different unit costs">
The derivation above prices *decode* tokens. Prefill is compute-bound and processes all prompt
tokens in parallel, so its cost per token is far lower and scales differently — it is bounded by
$2N$ FLOPs per token against the compute roofline, not by weight streaming. A single blended
"cost per token" therefore misprices any workload whose prompt-to-output ratio differs from the one
it was calibrated on, which is why published pricing separates input and output tokens.
</Warning>

<NotSaid>
This is a model of marginal serving cost. It excludes training amortisation, which for a
frequently-retrained model can exceed serving cost entirely; it excludes the cost of the
over-provisioned headroom that
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/autoscaling" /> shows is mandatory; and it assumes the replica is billed
continuously, which is wrong for spot or serverless capacity where the pricing model interacts with
the scaling delay. Each of those is a real term, and each moves the answer in the same direction.
</NotSaid>
