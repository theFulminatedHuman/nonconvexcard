<Intuition>
A canary is a hypothesis test with a deployment attached. You route a slice of traffic to the new
version, watch a metric, and decide. Everything that makes statistical testing hard applies —
with two extra difficulties that experiments in a laboratory do not have: the slice is small, so
power is scarce, and you are watching continuously, which quietly destroys the error guarantee the
test was supposed to give.

Both problems have arithmetic answers. The first says how long you must wait. The second says that
if you look a hundred times at the 5% level, you will find "significance" in a healthy deployment
about half the time.
</Intuition>

## How much traffic you need

<Claim kind="proposition" id="sample-size" title="Sample size for a two-arm comparison">
To detect a true difference of $\Delta$ in the mean of a metric with per-observation variance
$\sigma^2$ in each arm, using a two-sided test at level $\alpha$ with power $1-\beta$, the required
sample size per arm is
$$
n \;=\; \frac{2\sigma^2\,\bigl(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}\bigr)^2}{\Delta^2} .
$$
For $\alpha = 0.05$ and $1-\beta = 0.8$ the constant is $2(1.96+0.84)^2 = 15.7$, so
$$
n \;\approx\; \frac{16\,\sigma^2}{\Delta^2} .
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
- Observations are independent within and across arms. Correlated units — repeated requests from
  the same user, or requests sharing a replica — inflate the effective variance, and the standard
  fix is to randomise and analyse at the unit of independence rather than per request.
- The difference-in-means estimator is approximately normal, which for the sample sizes below
  follows from the central limit theorem.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
The estimator $\hat\Delta = \bar X_A - \bar X_B$ has variance $2\sigma^2/n$ under equal allocation,
so its standard error is $\mathrm{se} = \sigma\sqrt{2/n}$. A two-sided level-$\alpha$ test rejects
when $|\hat\Delta| > z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\mathrm{se}$. Under the alternative $\E[\hat\Delta] = \Delta$,
so rejection has probability
$$
\Pr\left[\,\hat\Delta > z_{1-\alpha/2}\mathrm{se}\,\right]
= \Pr\left[\, Z > z_{1-\alpha/2} - \frac{\Delta}{\mathrm{se}} \,\right],
$$
ignoring the negligible far tail. Requiring this to be at least $1-\beta$ means
$z_{1-\alpha/2} - \Delta/\mathrm{se} \le -z_{1-\beta}$, i.e.
$\Delta/\mathrm{se} \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}$. Substituting
$\mathrm{se} = \sigma\sqrt{2/n}$ and solving for $n$ gives the display.
</Proof>

<Claim kind="corollary" id="canary-duration" title="How long a canary must run">
If total traffic is $\lambda$ requests per second and the canary receives a fraction $f$, then
both arms can be sized at $n$ only after
$$
T \;=\; \frac{n}{f\,\lambda} \;\approx\; \frac{16\,\sigma^2}{f\,\lambda\,\Delta^2}
$$
seconds, since the canary arm is the scarce one.
</Claim>

<Proof>
The canary accumulates observations at rate $f\lambda$; reaching $n$ takes $n/(f\lambda)$. The
control arm accumulates faster and is not binding.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Work an example. Suppose you want to detect a 1% relative regression in a latency metric whose
coefficient of variation is $1$ — so $\sigma \approx$ the mean $m$, and $\Delta = 0.01m$. Then
$$
n \;\approx\; \frac{16\,m^2}{(0.01m)^2} \;=\; 16\times10^4 \;=\; 160{,}000 \ \text{per arm}.
$$
At 1000 requests per second with a 1% canary, $f\lambda = 10$ per second, so
$T = 16{,}000$ s $\approx 4.4$ hours. **A 1% canary cannot detect a 1% regression quickly**, and
the quadratic dependence on $\Delta$ is unforgiving: detecting 0.5% instead takes four times as
long.
</KeyIdea>

## Why watching continuously breaks the test

<Claim kind="proposition" id="peeking-inflates-error" title="Repeated testing inflates the false-positive rate">
Suppose the null hypothesis holds and you run a fixed-sample test at level $\alpha$ at each of $k$
interim times, stopping and declaring significance the first time it rejects. The probability of
declaring significance at some point is
$$
\alpha_k \;=\; \Pr\left[\bigcup_{j=1}^k R_j\right] \;\ge\; \alpha,
$$
strictly increasing in $k$, and $\alpha_k \to 1$ as $k \to \infty$ with continuous monitoring.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Each $R_j$ has probability $\alpha$ under the null, so the union has probability at least $\alpha$
and strictly more as soon as some $R_j$ is not contained in the others. For the limit, note that
the test statistic is (asymptotically) a random walk whose rescaled path is a Brownian motion; the
event "the standardised difference ever exceeds a *fixed* threshold" has probability $1$ for
Brownian motion by the law of the iterated logarithm, since $\limsup_t B_t/\sqrt{2t\ln\ln t} = 1$
almost surely implies $B_t$ exceeds any fixed multiple of $\sqrt t$ infinitely often. Hence
continuous monitoring against a fixed-sample critical value rejects almost surely.
</Proof>

<Warning title="This is the most common way experiment results are wrong">
Checking a dashboard daily for a fortnight and shipping when $p < 0.05$ is not a 5% test. The
honest options are: fix the sample size in advance and look once; spend the error budget explicitly
across interim looks (a group-sequential design); or use a sequential procedure whose guarantee
holds at every time, such as those in the cited work. All three are fine. Looking repeatedly at a
fixed-sample threshold is not.
</Warning>

## What guardrails cost

A rollout is rarely judged on one metric. There is the target metric and then a suite of guardrails
— latency, error rate, cost, refusal rate — any of which can block the release.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="guardrail-cost" title="The sensitivity cost of a guardrail suite">
Testing $m$ metrics and controlling the family-wise error rate at $\alpha$ by the Bonferroni
correction means testing each at $\alpha/m$. The required sample size per metric becomes
$$
n_m \;=\; \frac{2\sigma^2\bigl(z_{1-\alpha/(2m)}+z_{1-\beta}\bigr)^2}{\Delta^2},
$$
and since $z_{1-u} \sim \sqrt{2\ln(1/u)}$ as $u \to 0$, $n_m$ grows like $\ln m$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Bonferroni: if each of $m$ tests is run at level $\alpha/m$, the union bound gives family-wise
error at most $\alpha$. Substituting $\alpha \mapsto \alpha/m$ in the sample-size proposition gives
$n_m$. For the growth rate, the Gaussian tail bound
$1-\Phi(z) \le e^{-z^2/2}$ inverts to $z_{1-u} \le \sqrt{2\ln(1/u)}$, and the matching lower bound
gives $z_{1-u} \sim \sqrt{2\ln(1/u)}$; with $u = \alpha/(2m)$ this is
$\sqrt{2\ln(2m/\alpha)} = \Theta(\sqrt{\ln m})$, whose square is $\Theta(\ln m)$.
</Proof>

Concretely, at $\alpha = 0.05$ and 80% power: one metric needs the constant $15.7$; ten metrics
need $2(z_{0.9975}+0.84)^2 = 2(2.807+0.842)^2 \approx 26.6$; a hundred need
$2(3.481+0.842)^2 \approx 37.4$. Going from one guardrail to a hundred costs about $2.4\times$ the
traffic — expensive, but logarithmic rather than linear, which is why large guardrail suites are
affordable at all.

<NotSaid>
Nothing here addresses whether the metric measures what you care about. A canary that is
statistically clean on latency, error rate and cost can still ship a model that is worse in ways
none of those capture — and for generative systems the important regressions are usually of that
kind. The statistics above bound the risk of being *fooled by noise*; they say nothing about the
risk of measuring the wrong thing, which is the larger risk in practice and is not a statistical
problem at all.
</NotSaid>
