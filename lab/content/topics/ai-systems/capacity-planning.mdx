<Intuition>
Everything up to here has been a separate constraint. A replica can only hold so many sequences
because KV cache is memory. It can only emit so many tokens per second because weights have to be
streamed. It cannot be run near 100% utilisation because latency explodes. And concurrency,
throughput and latency are welded together by an identity.

Sizing a cluster is the exercise of writing all four down at once and seeing which one is tight.
Almost always exactly one is, and almost always it is not the one people are optimising.
</Intuition>

## The three constraints

Fix a replica and a request profile. Write:

- $M_{\text{kv}}$ — bytes of device memory left for KV cache after weights and workspace;
- $m$ — KV bytes for one resident sequence at its expected length;
- $\eta \in (0,1]$ — the fraction of the cache actually usable, after fragmentation;
- $W$ — mean end-to-end request latency, the quantity the SLO constrains;
- $\lambda$ — offered load in requests per second for the whole service;
- $\rho^\star$ — the target utilisation, chosen from the utilisation wall.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="concurrency-ceiling" title="The memory ceiling on concurrency">
The number of requests a replica can hold concurrently is at most
$$
L_{\max} \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{\eta\,M_{\text{kv}}}{m} \right\rfloor .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Each resident sequence must have its keys and values materialised for every layer, occupying $m$
bytes; residency is required for the whole time the request is in the system, since decoding step
$t$ attends to all of positions $1..t-1$. Distinct sequences share nothing unless they share a
prefix. Hence $L \cdot m \le \eta M_{\text{kv}}$.
</Proof>

<Claim kind="proposition" id="replica-throughput" title="Maximum throughput of one replica">
$$
X_{\text{replica}} \;\le\; \frac{L_{\max}}{W} .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Little's law gives $L = X W$ for the replica as a sub-system
(<TopicRef id="ai-systems/littles-law" />). Since $L \le L_{\max}$ at all times, the time-average
satisfies $XW \le L_{\max}$.
</Proof>

<Claim kind="theorem" id="cluster-sizing" title="The sizing formula">
To serve offered load $\lambda$ at mean latency $W$ while keeping each replica at utilisation
$\rho^\star$, the number of replicas required is
$$
\boxed{\;R \;=\; \left\lceil \frac{\lambda\,W}{\rho^\star\,L_{\max}} \right\rceil
\;=\; \left\lceil \frac{\lambda\,W\,m}{\rho^\star\,\eta\,M_{\text{kv}}} \right\rceil \;}
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
Each replica is asked to serve $\lambda/R$ requests per second. Its utilisation is, by the
utilisation law, $\rho = (\lambda/R)/X_{\max}$ where $X_{\max} = L_{\max}/W$ is its capacity from
the previous proposition. Requiring $\rho \le \rho^\star$ gives
$$
\frac{\lambda W}{R\,L_{\max}} \;\le\; \rho^\star
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
R \;\ge\; \frac{\lambda W}{\rho^\star L_{\max}} ,
$$
and $R$ is an integer. Substituting $L_{\max}$ from the memory ceiling gives the second form.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Every term in that formula is measurable, and each one is a lever:

| Lever | Effect on $R$ | How |
|---|---|---|
| $m$ | linear | shorter contexts, grouped-query attention, KV quantisation |
| $\eta$ | inverse | paged allocation instead of contiguous reservation |
| $M_{\text{kv}}$ | inverse | smaller weights (quantisation), bigger device |
| $W$ | linear | faster decode, less queueing |
| $\rho^\star$ | inverse | accept a worse tail |

Note that $\rho^\star$ and $W$ are not independent: raising $\rho^\star$ raises $W$ through the
$1/(1-\rho)$ factor, so pushing utilisation up buys less than the formula suggests when read
naively.
</KeyIdea>

## Working an example

Take a replica with $M_{\text{kv}} = 40$ GB of spare memory, a request profile whose expected KV
footprint is $m = 500$ MB, and paged allocation giving $\eta = 0.9$. Then
$$
L_{\max} \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{0.9 \times 40\,\text{GB}}{0.5\,\text{GB}} \right\rfloor \;=\; 72 .
$$
With mean latency $W = 4$ s and target utilisation $\rho^\star = 0.7$, one replica sustains
$0.7 \times 72/4 = 12.6$ requests per second. Serving $\lambda = 500$ requests per second needs
$$
R \;=\; \left\lceil \frac{500 \times 4}{0.7 \times 72} \right\rceil
\;=\; \lceil 39.7 \rceil \;=\; 40 \ \text{replicas} .
$$

Now change one thing. Halving $m$ — by quantising the KV cache to 8 bits — doubles $L_{\max}$ to
$144$ and halves $R$ to $20$. Halving $W$ does the same. Raising $\rho^\star$ from $0.7$ to $0.9$
reduces $R$ to $31$ *if $W$ stayed fixed*, but $W$ does not stay fixed: by the utilisation wall the
queueing component grows by a factor $(0.9/0.1)/(0.7/0.3) = 3.86$, and unless queueing was a small
part of $W$ to begin with, the change is a net loss.

<Warning title="Do not size from peak throughput benchmarks">
A throughput benchmark reports $X$ at whatever latency the benchmark happened to produce, usually
by saturating the replica — that is, at $\rho \approx 1$, where $W$ is at its worst. Substituting
that $X$ into a sizing calculation silently assumes you will run in the same regime, which is
exactly the regime the SLO forbids. Size from $L_{\max}$ and the *target* $W$, and treat the
benchmark number as an upper bound you will never operate at.
</Warning>

## Which constraint binds

<Claim kind="corollary" id="binding-constraint" title="Which constraint binds">
There is a second ceiling on concurrency, from compute: the batch size at which decode stops being
weight-bound. Call it $L_{\text{compute}}$. The replica's effective cap is
$\min(L_{\max}, L_{\text{compute}})$, and:

- if $L_{\max} < L_{\text{compute}}$ the replica is **memory-bound** — buy KV capacity, not FLOPs;
- if $L_{\text{compute}} < L_{\max}$ it is **compute-bound** — a bigger cache buys nothing.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Both are upper bounds on the sustainable concurrency, so their minimum is binding. The
characterisation follows by asking which of the two the minimum is.
</Proof>

By the decode arithmetic in <TopicRef id="llm-ops/inference-arithmetic" />, arithmetic intensity in
weight-limited decoding is $2B/b$ for batch $B$ and $b$ bytes per parameter, so
$L_{\text{compute}}$ is roughly the batch at which $2B/b$ reaches the hardware's
compute-to-bandwidth ratio. For a machine with ratio $\approx 300$ and $b = 2$, that is
$B \approx 300$. A replica whose memory allows only $72$ concurrent sequences is therefore firmly
memory-bound, and its idle arithmetic units are not a bug to be tuned away — they are the direct
consequence of the KV budget.

<NotSaid>
This treats $m$ and $W$ as single numbers. Both are distributions, and by the
Pollaczek–Khinchine formula their *variance* affects queueing independently of their means. A
profile mixing 200-token and 20,000-token contexts has an $m$ whose mean badly misrepresents the
memory behaviour, because a handful of long sequences can consume the budget that the mean says
should hold dozens. Sizing on means and then operating on a mixed workload is a standard way to
discover the tail the hard way.
</NotSaid>
