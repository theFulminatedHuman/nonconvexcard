<Intuition>
Adding machines to a system does one of three things. In the best case throughput rises in
proportion. In the ordinary case it rises but flattens, because some part of the work cannot be
split. In the bad case it rises, peaks, and then *falls* — more machines, less work done — because
the machines spend their time telling each other what they did.

All three are captured by adding one term at a time to the same expression, and knowing which term
dominates tells you whether the fix is more hardware, a different decomposition, or less sharing.
</Intuition>

## The ceiling

<Claim kind="proposition" id="amdahl" title="Amdahl's law">
Let a fixed workload take unit time on one processor, of which a fraction $s \in [0,1]$ is serial
and $1-s$ is perfectly parallelisable across $p$ processors. Then
$$
T(p) \;=\; s + \frac{1-s}{p},
\qquad
S(p) \;=\; \frac{T(1)}{T(p)} \;=\; \frac{1}{\,s + (1-s)/p\,}
\;\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}\; \frac 1s .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
The serial portion takes time $s$ regardless of $p$; the parallel portion takes $(1-s)/p$. Their
sum is $T(p)$, and $T(1) = s + (1-s) = 1$. As $p \to \infty$ the second term vanishes and
$S(p) \uparrow 1/s$. Monotonicity is clear since $T$ is decreasing in $p$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
With 5% serial work the ceiling is $20\times$, and 64 processors already deliver $15.4\times$ —
77% of everything more hardware will ever give. The practical reading is not "parallelism is
futile" but **measure $s$ before buying**: the derivative $S'(p)$ falls off a cliff at
$p \approx 1/s$, and spending past that point is close to pure waste.
</KeyIdea>

<Warning title="s is not the fraction of code that is serial">
$s$ is the fraction of *time*, measured on one processor. Ten lines of lock-protected bookkeeping
in a thousand-line kernel can easily be 30% of the runtime. In serving systems the usual hidden
serial components are a shared tokeniser, a single scheduler thread, a metrics lock, and — most
often — logging.
</Warning>

## The escape

Amdahl assumes the *problem* is fixed. Often it is not: given more machines, people run bigger
batches, longer contexts, more shards.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="gustafson" title="Gustafson's scaled speedup">
Suppose that on $p$ processors the work is sized so that the run takes unit wall-clock time, of
which $\sigma$ is serial and $1-\sigma$ is parallel. The same work on one processor would take
$\sigma + (1-\sigma)p$. Hence
$$
S_{\text{scaled}}(p) \;=\; \sigma + (1-\sigma)\,p ,
$$
which is linear in $p$ with no ceiling.
</Claim>

<Proof>
By construction the parallel portion occupies $1-\sigma$ of the wall clock on $p$ processors, so it
represents $(1-\sigma)p$ units of single-processor work; the serial portion represents $\sigma$
either way. The ratio of single-processor time to parallel time is therefore
$(\sigma + (1-\sigma)p)/1$.
</Proof>

<Note title="The two laws do not disagree">
They answer different questions. Amdahl asks "how much faster does *this* job finish?" — strong
scaling. Gustafson asks "how much more work can I do in the same time?" — weak scaling. Training a
model is usually a weak-scaling problem: more GPUs means a larger global batch, not the same batch
finishing sooner. Serving a single user's request is a strong-scaling problem, and Amdahl's ceiling
is the one that binds.
</Note>

## The case where more machines are worse

Both laws above are monotone in $p$. Real distributed systems often are not: throughput peaks and
then declines. The universal scalability law adds a term for the cost of keeping $p$ participants
consistent, which grows like the number of *pairs*.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="usl-maximum" title="The universal scalability law has an interior maximum">
Define relative capacity
$$
C(p) \;=\; \frac{p}{1 + \alpha\,(p-1) + \beta\,p\,(p-1)} ,
$$
with $\alpha \ge 0$ the **contention** coefficient (serialisation, as in Amdahl) and $\beta \ge 0$
the **coherency** coefficient (pairwise consistency traffic). If $\beta > 0$ then $C$ is maximised
at
$$
p^\star \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta}} ,
$$
and $C(p) \to 0$ as $p \to \infty$.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
This is a two-parameter *model*, fitted to measurements, not a theorem about computation. Its
justification is that $\alpha$ captures a queueing-like serial term and $\beta$ a term proportional
to $\binom p2$ pairwise interactions; whether a given system obeys it is an empirical question. It
is stated here because the qualitative prediction — an interior maximum — is real and is
routinely observed.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Write $C(p) = p/D(p)$ with $D(p) = 1 + \alpha(p-1) + \beta p(p-1)$. Then
$$
C'(p) \;=\; \frac{D(p) - p\,D'(p)}{D(p)^2},
$$
so stationary points satisfy $D(p) = p D'(p)$. Compute
$D'(p) = \alpha + \beta(2p-1)$, hence
$$
1 + \alpha(p-1) + \beta p(p-1) \;=\; p\alpha + \beta p(2p-1) .
$$
Expanding both sides:
$$
1 + \alpha p - \alpha + \beta p^2 - \beta p \;=\; \alpha p + 2\beta p^2 - \beta p ,
$$
and cancelling $\alpha p$ and $-\beta p$ leaves $1 - \alpha + \beta p^2 = 2\beta p^2$, so
$\beta p^2 = 1-\alpha$ and $p^\star = \sqrt{(1-\alpha)/\beta}$. Since $D$ grows quadratically while
the numerator grows linearly, $C(p) \sim 1/(\beta p) \to 0$, confirming the stationary point is a
maximum.
</Proof>

With $\alpha = 0.03$ and $\beta = 10^{-4}$ the formula gives $p^\star \approx 98$, and a direct
search over integer $p$ agrees at $98$. Beyond that, adding machines reduces total throughput.

<KeyIdea>
The three regimes are distinguished by a single question: **what has to agree with what?**

- Nothing shared, no serial section: linear scaling.
- A serial section: Amdahl, flattening at $1/s$.
- Every participant must see every other's updates: coherency, peaking at
  $\sqrt{(1-\alpha)/\beta}$ and then falling.

A distributed KV cache with cross-replica invalidation is in the third regime. Independent stateless
replicas behind a load balancer are in the first. Which one you are in is an architectural choice,
not a tuning parameter.
</KeyIdea>

<NotSaid>
None of these models say anything about latency — they are all statements about throughput or
completion time of a fixed job. A system can be scaling perfectly by $C(p)$ while its p99 degrades,
because adding replicas increases fan-out, and
<TopicRef id="ai-systems/tail-latency-and-fan-out" /> showed what fan-out does to a tail. Capacity
and tail latency must be tracked separately; optimising one routinely damages the other.
</NotSaid>
