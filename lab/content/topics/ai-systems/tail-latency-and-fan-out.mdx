<Intuition>
Suppose your retrieval layer is fast: 99% of shard queries finish within 10 ms. Now a single user
request queries 100 shards and cannot answer until the last one returns. What fraction of user
requests finish within 10 ms?

Not 99%. About 37%. The chance that *all hundred* shards come in under 10 ms is
$0.99^{100} \approx 0.366$, so nearly two thirds of user requests are slower than the number on
your shard dashboard. Nothing is broken. Every shard is meeting its stated target. The architecture
itself converted a 1% event into a 63% event.
</Intuition>

## The maximum is the parent

<Claim kind="proposition" id="fanout-max" title="The distribution of the slowest of n calls">
Let a parent request issue $n$ sub-requests with latencies $X_1,\dots,X_n$, independent and
identically distributed with CDF $F$, and let the parent complete when the last child does. Then
the parent latency $M_n = \max_i X_i$ has
$$
\Pr[M_n \le t] \;=\; F(t)^n ,
\qquad
\Pr[M_n > t] \;=\; 1 - \bigl(1-\bar F(t)\bigr)^n \;\le\; n\,\bar F(t) ,
$$
where $\bar F = 1-F$ is the tail function.
</Claim>

<Proof>
$M_n \le t$ holds exactly when every $X_i \le t$; by independence that probability is
$\prod_i \Pr[X_i \le t] = F(t)^n$. The tail identity follows by complementation, and the
inequality $1-(1-u)^n \le nu$ for $u \in [0,1]$ is Bernoulli's inequality (or a union bound over
the $n$ events $\{X_i > t\}$).
</Proof>

<Claim kind="corollary" id="fanout-quantile" title="Fan-out shifts which quantile you must control">
To achieve $\Pr[M_n \le t] \ge p$ it is necessary and sufficient that each child satisfies
$F(t) \ge p^{1/n}$ — that is, $t$ must be the child's $p^{1/n}$ quantile. For $p = 0.99$:
$$
\begin{array}{r|cccc}
n & 1 & 10 & 100 & 1000\\ \hline
p^{1/n} & 0.99 & 0.99899 & 0.999899 & 0.9999899
\end{array}
$$
So a hundred-way fan-out turns a p99 target on the parent into a **p99.99** target on each child.
</Claim>

<Proof>
From the proposition, $\Pr[M_n \le t] = F(t)^n \ge p \iff F(t) \ge p^{1/n}$. The numerical row is
$0.99^{1/n}$ evaluated at each $n$; note $p^{1/n} = \exp(\ln p / n) \approx 1 - (1-p)/n$ for large
$n$, so the required child tail probability shrinks like $1/n$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Fan-out does not make any individual service slower. It changes **which quantile of that service
the user experiences**. A service optimised to a p99 target is, under 100-way fan-out, being
consumed at its p99.99 — a part of the distribution nobody measured, nobody tuned, and which is
usually dominated by effects invisible at p99: garbage collection, a cold cache, a retry, a kernel
scheduling hiccup.
</KeyIdea>

<Warning title="The independence assumption is doing real work">
$F(t)^n$ assumes the children fail to be fast independently. They frequently do not: they share a
network fabric, a power domain, a noisy neighbour, or a release. Correlation *helps* here — if all
shards are slow together, $\Pr[M_n > t]$ is closer to $\bar F(t)$ than to $n \bar F(t)$. So the
independent calculation is the pessimistic one for the tail of a single request, while being
optimistic about how many requests are affected at once. Both readings matter and they point in
opposite directions.
</Warning>

## What hedging buys

The standard countermeasure is to send a second copy of a sub-request if the first has not returned
by some deadline $d$, and take whichever answers first.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="hedging-bound" title="A hedged request truncates the tail at the hedge delay">
Let $X$ be the latency of one attempt, with tail $\bar F$. Issue a second, independent attempt at
time $d$ if the first is still outstanding, and let $Y$ be the time until the first response. Then
for $t > d$,
$$
\Pr[Y > t] \;=\; \bar F(t)\cdot \bar F(t-d) ,
$$
and for $t \le d$, $\Pr[Y>t] = \bar F(t)$.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
- The two attempts are independent — different replica, different host, no shared bottleneck. A
  hedge routed to the same overloaded machine buys nothing, which is the common failure mode.
- Issuing the hedge does not slow the original. At low hedge rates this is nearly true; at high
  rates it is false, and the cost proposition below is what bounds the damage.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
For $t \le d$ the hedge has not been issued, so $Y = X_1$ and the claim is immediate. For $t > d$,
the event $\{Y > t\}$ requires the first attempt to still be outstanding at $t$, which has
probability $\bar F(t)$, *and* the second attempt — which started at $d$ and has therefore been
running for $t-d$ — to still be outstanding, which by independence has probability
$\bar F(t-d)$. Multiplying gives the display.
</Proof>

Take $d$ to be the p95 of $X$. Then for $t$ beyond $d$ the tail is the product of two small
numbers rather than one. If $\bar F$ decays exponentially with scale $\tau$, so
$\bar F(t) = e^{-t/\tau}$, the hedged tail is $e^{-(2t-d)/\tau}$ — the decay rate has **doubled**.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="hedging-cost" title="The extra load a hedge creates">
If the hedge fires at delay $d = q_p(X)$, the expected number of attempts per logical request is
$$
\E[\#\text{attempts}] \;=\; 1 + \Pr[X > d] \;=\; 1 + (1-p) ,
$$
so hedging at the p95 costs at most 5% additional load, and at the p99 at most 1%.
</Claim>

<Proof>
The second attempt is issued exactly on the event $\{X > d\}$, whose probability is
$1 - F(d) = 1-p$ by definition of the quantile. Expectation of the count $1 + \one\{X>d\}$ is
$1 + (1-p)$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Hedging is unusually cheap because it is *conditional*: it only duplicates the requests that are
already slow, and by construction those are rare. Five percent extra capacity to square the tail is
one of the best trades available in a serving system — provided the hedge lands somewhere
genuinely independent, and provided the system has the 5% to spare, which by the utilisation wall
it will only have if it is not already near saturation.
</KeyIdea>

<Warning title="Hedging under overload is a positive feedback loop">
The cost bound $1+(1-p)$ assumes $d$ is a *fixed* quantile of the healthy distribution. Under
overload the whole distribution shifts right, so a fixed threshold $d$ starts firing on far more
than $1-p$ of requests. That extra load pushes utilisation up, which by $1/(1-\rho)$ pushes latency
up, which fires more hedges. Production hedging must therefore be rate-limited — typically to a few
percent of traffic — so that the mechanism cannot amplify the overload it was meant to mask.
</Warning>

## Reading the numbers

Two consequences are worth carrying around.

1. **Measure the quantile your architecture actually consumes.** If a request fans out $n$ ways,
   instrument the child at $1 - (1-p)/n$, not at $p$. For $n = 100$ and a p99 parent target that is
   p99.99 — which needs on the order of $10^5$ samples to estimate at all, a measurement problem in
   its own right.
2. **Reducing fan-out is a latency optimisation.** Halving the number of shards a query touches
   moves the consumed quantile from $p^{1/n}$ to $p^{2/n}$, which is a strictly easier target. This
   is a real argument for larger shards, and it trades against the memory and pooling arguments
   elsewhere in this field.

<NotSaid>
This page treats the children as independent draws from a fixed distribution. It does not model the
case where the slow child is slow *because* of the parent's own load — a self-inflicted straggler.
Nor does it cover the case where partial results are acceptable: if the parent can answer with 95
of 100 shards, the relevant order statistic is $X_{(95)}$ rather than the maximum, and the
fan-out penalty largely disappears. Designing for partial results is the strongest available
defence against tail amplification, and it is an application-level decision rather than an
infrastructure one.
</NotSaid>
