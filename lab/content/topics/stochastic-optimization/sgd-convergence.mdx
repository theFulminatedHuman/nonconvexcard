<Intuition>
Gradient descent needs $\nabla f(x_t)$, and for an empirical risk over $n$ examples that costs $n$
gradient evaluations per step. SGD replaces it with an unbiased estimate from one example, or a small
batch. The estimate is right *on average*, so the expected trajectory is the gradient-descent
trajectory — but the realised trajectory is a random walk around it, and everything on this page is
about what that walk costs.
</Intuition>

## Setup

<Definition id="sgd-setup" title="The stochastic gradient oracle">
$$
x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta_t g_t,
$$
where the oracle satisfies, conditional on the past $\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(x_0,\dots,x_t)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{(A1) unbiasedness:} && \E[g_t \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \nabla f(x_t);\\
&\textbf{(A2) bounded variance:} && \E\big[\norm{g_t - \nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_t\big] \le \sigma^2;\\
&\textbf{(A3) bounded second moment:} && \E\big[\norm{g_t}_2^2\mid\mathcal{F}_t\big] \le G^2 \quad\text{(used only for the non-smooth results)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
</Definition>

<Assumptions>
(A1) is what makes the method a *stochastic approximation* of gradient descent rather than a
different algorithm. It holds exactly for uniform sampling from a finite training set, and it fails
for importance sampling without reweighting, for asynchronous updates with stale gradients, and for
gradient clipping — which is worth knowing, since clipping is standard practice in LLM training and
the standard theorems do not cover it.
</Assumptions>

## Convex case

<Theorem id="sgd-convex" title="SGD on convex Lipschitz functions">
Let $f$ be convex with minimiser $x^\star$, $\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2\le R$, and assume (A1) and (A3).
Run SGD for $T$ steps with constant $\eta = \frac{R}{G\sqrt T}$ and output
$\bar x_T = \frac1T\sum_{t<T}x_t$. Then
$$
\E\big[f(\bar x_T)\big] - f^\star \le \frac{GR}{\sqrt T}.
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Expand the squared distance:
$$
\norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2 = \norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 - 2\eta\ip{g_t}{x_t-x^\star} + \eta^2\norm{g_t}_2^2 .
$$
Take conditional expectation given $\mathcal{F}_t$. Since $x_t - x^\star$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable,
(A1) and the "take out what is known" rule give
$$
\E\big[\ip{g_t}{x_t-x^\star}\mid\mathcal{F}_t\big] = \ip{\nabla f(x_t)}{x_t - x^\star} \ge f(x_t)-f^\star
$$
by convexity. Using (A3) on the last term and taking total expectations,
$$
\E\norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2 \le \E\norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 - 2\eta\,\E\big[f(x_t)-f^\star\big] + \eta^2G^2 .
$$
Summing over $t<T$ and telescoping,
$$
2\eta\sum_{t<T}\E\big[f(x_t)-f^\star\big] \le R^2 + T\eta^2G^2 .
$$
By convexity (Jensen) $f(\bar x_T)\le\frac1T\sum_{t<T}f(x_t)$, so
$$
\E f(\bar x_T)-f^\star \le \frac{R^2}{2\eta T}+\frac{\eta G^2}{2},
$$
minimised at $\eta = R/(G\sqrt T)$ with value $GR/\sqrt T$.
</Proof>

<Note title="Compare with the deterministic case">
This is *exactly* the <TopicRef id="optimization/gradient-descent-convergence">subgradient method</TopicRef>
bound, with the same proof. The only place stochasticity entered was in replacing an inequality by its
conditional expectation. So in the non-smooth convex regime, **SGD is as good as full gradient
descent per iteration** — and each iteration is $n$ times cheaper. That observation is the reason SGD
is the default in machine learning, and it is a statement about the *comparison*, not about SGD being
fast in absolute terms.
</Note>

## Strongly convex case: the noise floor

<Theorem id="sgd-strongly-convex" title="Constant-step SGD on strongly convex smooth functions">
Let $f$ be $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth, and assume (A1)–(A2). Run SGD with constant
$\eta \le \frac{1}{2L}$. Then
$$
\E\norm{x_T-x^\star}_2^2 \le (1-\eta\mu)^T\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2 + \frac{\eta\sigma^2}{\mu}.
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Let $e_t = \E\norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2$. Expand and condition as before:
$$
\E\big[\norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2\mid\mathcal{F}_t\big]
= \norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 - 2\eta\ip{\nabla f(x_t)}{x_t-x^\star} + \eta^2\E\big[\norm{g_t}_2^2\mid\mathcal{F}_t\big].
$$
Decompose the second moment using (A1)–(A2):
$\E[\norm{g_t}_2^2\mid\mathcal{F}_t] = \norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 + \E[\norm{g_t-\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2\mid\mathcal{F}_t] \le \norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 + \sigma^2$.

For the inner-product term, strong convexity gives the **co-coercivity-type** bound
$$
\ip{\nabla f(x_t)}{x_t-x^\star} \ge \mu\norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 ,
$$
which follows by adding the first-order strong convexity inequalities at $x_t$ and at $x^\star$ and
using $\nabla f(x^\star)=0$. Smoothness gives
$\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 \le 2L\big(f(x_t)-f^\star\big) \le L^2\norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2$; we use the
cruder second form. Putting these together,
$$
\E\big[\norm{x_{t+1}-x^\star}_2^2\mid\mathcal{F}_t\big]
\le \big(1 - 2\eta\mu + \eta^2L^2\big)\norm{x_t-x^\star}_2^2 + \eta^2\sigma^2 .
$$
For $\eta \le \mu/L^2$ (which holds under $\eta\le1/(2L)$ whenever $\mu \le L$, after adjusting the
constant) the bracket is at most $1 - \eta\mu$. Taking total expectations,
$$
e_{t+1}\le(1-\eta\mu)e_t + \eta^2\sigma^2 .
$$
This linear recursion has the explicit solution
$$
e_T \le (1-\eta\mu)^Te_0 + \eta^2\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}(1-\eta\mu)^j
\le (1-\eta\mu)^Te_0 + \frac{\eta^2\sigma^2}{\eta\mu} = (1-\eta\mu)^Te_0 + \frac{\eta\sigma^2}{\mu}.
$$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Two terms, two behaviours. The first decays geometrically — this is the deterministic
<TopicRef id="optimization/gradient-descent-convergence">linear rate</TopicRef>, unchanged. The
second, $\eta\sigma^2/\mu$, **does not decay at all**. Constant-step SGD does not converge to
$x^\star$; it converges to a ball of radius $\sqrt{\eta\sigma^2/\mu}$ around it, and the radius
shrinks only if you shrink $\eta$. The trade is explicit: a larger step reaches the ball faster and
the ball is bigger. This is the whole story behind learning-rate schedules, and it is visible
directly in the SGD experiment as the flattening of the error curve.
</KeyIdea>

<Theorem id="sgd-decaying" title="SGD with decaying step size">
Under the same assumptions, run SGD with $\eta_t = \frac{2}{\mu(t+\gamma)}$ for
$\gamma = 4L^2/\mu^2$. Then for all $T\ge1$,
$$
\E\norm{x_T-x^\star}_2^2 \le \frac{C\sigma^2}{\mu^2 T} + \frac{C'\norm{x_0-x^\star}_2^2}{T^2}
$$
for absolute constants $C,C'$ depending only on the ratio $L/\mu$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
From the recursion established above with a step-dependent $\eta_t$,
$$
e_{t+1}\le(1-\eta_t\mu)e_t + \eta_t^2\sigma^2 .
$$
Claim: $e_t \le \dfrac{A}{t+\gamma}$ with $A = \max\left(\dfrac{4\sigma^2}{\mu^2},\ \gamma e_0\right)$.
Induct. At $t=0$, $e_0 \le A/\gamma$ by the choice of $A$. Assuming it at $t$, and writing
$\eta_t\mu = 2/(t+\gamma)$,
$$
e_{t+1} \le \left(1 - \frac{2}{t+\gamma}\right)\frac{A}{t+\gamma} + \frac{4\sigma^2}{\mu^2(t+\gamma)^2}
= \frac{A(t+\gamma-2)}{(t+\gamma)^2} + \frac{4\sigma^2}{\mu^2(t+\gamma)^2}.
$$
Since $4\sigma^2/\mu^2 \le A$, the right-hand side is at most
$\dfrac{A(t+\gamma-1)}{(t+\gamma)^2}$, and
$(t+\gamma-1)(t+\gamma+1) = (t+\gamma)^2 - 1 \le (t+\gamma)^2$ gives
$\dfrac{A(t+\gamma-1)}{(t+\gamma)^2}\le\dfrac{A}{t+\gamma+1}$, completing the induction.

Unpacking $A$ gives the two stated terms.
</Proof>

<Note title="Rate comparison">
Strongly convex deterministic GD: $e^{-t/\kappa}$, i.e. $\log(1/\eps)$ steps. Strongly convex SGD:
$\sigma^2/(\mu^2 t)$, i.e. $1/\eps$ steps. The stochastic rate is **exponentially worse in the
accuracy**, and no step-size schedule fixes that — the $\Omega(\sigma^2/(\mu^2 T))$ rate is
information-theoretically optimal for this oracle. What SGD buys is a factor of $n$ per step, which
wins overwhelmingly when $n$ is large and the target accuracy is modest. That is precisely the
machine-learning regime: statistical error is $\Omega(1/\sqrt n)$ anyway, so optimising far below it
is wasted work.
</Note>

<Theorem id="robbins-monro" title="Robbins–Monro conditions">
In the stochastic approximation setting, if the step sizes satisfy
$$
\sum_{t=1}^\infty \eta_t = \infty \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{t=1}^\infty\eta_t^2 < \infty,
$$
then (under regularity conditions on $f$ and the noise) $x_t \to x^\star$ almost surely.
</Theorem>

Quoted from Robbins and Monro; modern proofs go through supermartingale convergence. The two
conditions have clean interpretations visible in the proofs above: $\sum\eta_t = \infty$ ensures the
geometric-decay factor $\prod_t(1-\eta_t\mu)$ goes to zero, so the initial condition is forgotten;
$\sum\eta_t^2<\infty$ ensures the accumulated noise $\sum_t\eta_t^2\sigma^2$ is finite, so the noise
floor vanishes. $\eta_t = 1/t$ satisfies both; $\eta_t = 1/\sqrt t$ satisfies the first only; any
constant $\eta$ satisfies the second only — and correspondingly has the floor computed above.

## Non-convex case

<Theorem id="sgd-nonconvex" title="SGD on smooth non-convex functions">
Let $f$ be $L$-smooth and bounded below by $f^\star$, and assume (A1)–(A2). Run SGD for $T$ steps
with constant $\eta = \min\left(\frac1L, \sqrt{\frac{2(f(x_0)-f^\star)}{L\sigma^2T}}\right)$. Then
$$
\frac1T\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\E\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 \le \frac{2\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big)}{\eta T} + \eta L\sigma^2
\;\lesssim\; \sqrt{\frac{L\sigma^2\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big)}{T}} .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Apply the descent lemma to the step $x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta g_t$:
$$
f(x_{t+1}) \le f(x_t) - \eta\ip{\nabla f(x_t)}{g_t} + \frac{L\eta^2}{2}\norm{g_t}_2^2 .
$$
Take conditional expectation. By (A1) the inner product becomes $\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2$, and by
(A1)–(A2) the second moment is at most $\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2+\sigma^2$:
$$
\E\big[f(x_{t+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_t\big] \le f(x_t) - \eta\left(1-\frac{L\eta}{2}\right)\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 + \frac{L\eta^2\sigma^2}{2}.
$$
With $\eta\le1/L$ the bracket is at least $1/2$, so
$$
\frac\eta2\,\E\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 \le \E f(x_t) - \E f(x_{t+1}) + \frac{L\eta^2\sigma^2}{2}.
$$
Sum over $t<T$, telescope, and divide by $\eta T/2$:
$$
\frac1T\sum_{t<T}\E\norm{\nabla f(x_t)}_2^2 \le \frac{2\big(f(x_0)-f^\star\big)}{\eta T} + L\eta\sigma^2 .
$$
Balancing the two terms in $\eta$ gives the stated choice and the $T^{-1/2}$ rate.
</Proof>

<NotSaid>
Compare with the deterministic non-convex rate $O(1/T)$ for $\min_s\norm{\nabla f(x_s)}_2^2$: noise
costs a full square root. And the conclusion is still only about **average squared gradient norm** —
it does not say the last iterate has small gradient, does not say any iterate is near a local
minimum, and says nothing at all about $f(x_T)-\inf f$. Every caveat from the deterministic
non-convex theorem still applies, plus the new one that the guarantee is in expectation.
</NotSaid>

## Mini-batching and the expectation/probability distinction

<Proposition id="minibatch-variance" title="Mini-batching reduces variance linearly">
If $g_t^{(B)} = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B g_t^{(b)}$ with the $g_t^{(b)}$ conditionally i.i.d. unbiased with
variance $\sigma^2$, then $g_t^{(B)}$ is unbiased with conditional variance $\sigma^2/B$.
Consequently the noise floor of constant-step SGD becomes $\eta\sigma^2/(\mu B)$ and the non-convex
rate becomes $\sqrt{L\sigma^2\Delta_0/(BT)}$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Unbiasedness is linearity. For the variance, the summands are conditionally independent and
identically distributed, so
$\E\norm{g^{(B)}-\nabla f}_2^2 = \frac{1}{B^2}\sum_b\E\norm{g^{(b)}-\nabla f}_2^2 = \sigma^2/B$,
the cross terms vanishing by conditional independence and zero conditional mean.
</Proof>

<Note title="What this says about batch size">
The rate improves as $1/\sqrt{BT}$, and the cost per step scales as $B$: for a **fixed compute
budget** $BT$, the bound is unchanged. So the theory says large batches buy parallelism, not sample
efficiency — consistent with the empirical finding that there is a "critical batch size" beyond which
scaling the batch stops helping. The theory does not predict where that threshold is.
</Note>

<Proposition id="expectation-vs-probability" title="Expected error does not bound failure probability">
A bound $\E[f(x_T)-f^\star]\le\epsilon$ implies only
$$
\P\big(f(x_T)-f^\star > s\big)\le \frac{\epsilon}{s},
$$
by Markov's inequality, and this is the best possible without further assumptions.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Markov applied to the non-negative variable $f(x_T)-f^\star$. Tightness: let $f(x_T)-f^\star$ equal
$s$ with probability $\epsilon/s$ and $0$ otherwise; the expectation is $\epsilon$ and the tail
probability is exactly $\epsilon/s$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
For a $99\%$ confidence statement one needs $\epsilon \le s/100$, i.e. a hundred-fold improvement in
the expected error — a $10^4$-fold increase in $T$ under the $1/\sqrt T$ rate. This is why
high-probability bounds for SGD are a separate research programme rather than a corollary: getting
$\P(\text{gap}>s)\le\delta$ with a $\log(1/\delta)$ dependence requires martingale concentration
(<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-inequalities">Azuma</TopicRef> or Freedman's
inequality) applied to the noise sequence, and additional assumptions such as sub-Gaussian gradient
noise. Reporting an in-expectation bound as though it were a high-confidence guarantee is a common
and consequential error.
</KeyIdea>

<Claim kind="heuristic" id="sde-approximation" title="The SDE / diffusion approximation">
As $\eta\to0$ with $t = \eta k$ held fixed, the SGD recursion resembles the Itô SDE
$$
dX_t = -\nabla f(X_t)\,dt + \sqrt{\eta\,\Sigma(X_t)}\;dW_t,
$$
where $\Sigma(x)$ is the gradient-noise covariance. Li, Tai and E make this precise as a weak
approximation of order one in $\eta$.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The approximation is genuinely useful for intuition — it explains why the stationary distribution
depends on the ratio $\eta/B$ rather than on either separately, and it motivates linear
learning-rate scaling rules. But its hypotheses are not satisfied in the regimes people invoke it
for:

- it requires $\eta$ small, and deep learning uses the largest stable $\eta$;
- it assumes the noise is approximately Gaussian, whereas mini-batch gradient noise is often heavy-tailed;
- it assumes $\Sigma$ varies slowly, whereas gradient noise covariance changes sharply across the loss landscape.

Statements of the form "SGD prefers flat minima because the SDE's stationary measure concentrates
there" are heuristic arguments resting on these hypotheses, not theorems about SGD. They belong in
the same category as the other plausible-but-unproven claims catalogued on the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">deep learning theory</TopicRef> page.
</NotSaid>
