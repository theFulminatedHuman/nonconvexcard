## Compute accounting

<Proposition id="flops-estimate" title="The 6ND FLOP estimate">
Training a dense transformer with $N$ non-embedding parameters on $D$ tokens costs approximately
$$
C \approx 6ND \text{ FLOPs}.
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Every parameter participates in one multiply–accumulate per token in the forward pass, which is
$2$ FLOPs (one multiply, one add). So the forward pass is $\approx2ND$.

The backward pass computes two quantities per weight matrix: the gradient with respect to the input
(one matrix product of the same shape as the forward) and the gradient with respect to the weights
(another). Each costs the same as the forward product, so the backward pass is $\approx4ND$.

Total $\approx6ND$. The estimate ignores attention's $O(n^2d)$ term, which is a correction of relative
size $n/(6d)$ per the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning/transformers">complexity table</TopicRef> — negligible when
$n\ll d$ and not negligible for long context.
</Proof>

## Compute-optimal scaling

<Proposition id="compute-optimal" title="Compute-optimal allocation under a power-law loss">
Suppose the fitted loss is
$$
\mathcal{L}(N,D) = \mathcal{L}_\infty + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta},
$$
and the compute budget is $C = 6ND$. Then the minimiser of $\mathcal{L}$ subject to fixed $C$
satisfies
$$
N^\star \propto C^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}},
\qquad
D^\star \propto C^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}} .
$$
In particular, if $\alpha = \beta$ then $N^\star$ and $D^\star$ both scale as $\sqrt C$ — that is,
**in fixed proportion to each other**.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Substitute $D = C/(6N)$ and minimise over $N$:
$$
g(N) = \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + B\left(\frac{6N}{C}\right)^{\beta} .
$$
Differentiate:
$$
g'(N) = -\frac{\alpha A}{N^{\alpha+1}} + \frac{\beta B\,6^\beta N^{\beta-1}}{C^\beta} = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
N^{\alpha+\beta} = \frac{\alpha A\,C^\beta}{\beta B\,6^\beta},
$$
so $N^\star\propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$. Then $D^\star = C/(6N^\star)\propto C^{1-\beta/(\alpha+\beta)} = C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}$.
Since $g$ is a sum of a decreasing and an increasing power of $N$, the stationary point is the unique
minimum.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The fitted exponents decide the allocation, and the empirical finding of Hoffmann et al. is that
$\alpha\approx\beta\approx0.34$, so $N^\star$ and $D^\star$ scale together as $C^{1/2}$ — roughly
**20 tokens per parameter**. The earlier Kaplan et al. analysis, fitting the same functional family
with different methodology (notably a fixed learning-rate schedule that disadvantaged the
longer-trained runs), inferred $N^\star \propto C^{0.73}$ and therefore recommended models much larger
and data much smaller.

The lesson is worth stating plainly: the *form* of the law is empirical, the *exponents* are fitted,
the allocation rule is a Lagrange computation on top of the fit, and the whole chain is only as
reliable as the fit. A methodological change to the fit moved the recommendation by more than an
order of magnitude. Compute-optimality is an engineering estimate, not a theorem.
</KeyIdea>

<NotSaid>
The computation optimises **training loss at fixed training compute**. It says nothing about
inference cost, and inference is where most lifetime compute is spent for a deployed model. For a
model that will serve many queries, a smaller model trained on more tokens than the compute-optimal
point is often the better system-level choice, even though it is suboptimal by this criterion. Recent
practice reflects this, and the divergence is not a contradiction of the analysis but a different
objective.
</NotSaid>

## Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

<Proposition id="lora-rank" title="LoRA parameter count and the rank argument">
Replace a frozen weight $W_0\in\R^{d\times k}$ by
$$
W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA, \qquad B\in\R^{d\times r},\ A\in\R^{r\times k},
$$
with $A$ initialised Gaussian and $B$ initialised to zero. Then

- the number of trainable parameters is $r(d+k)$ instead of $dk$, a factor $dk/(r(d+k))$ reduction, which for $d=k$ is $d/(2r)$;
- at initialisation $W = W_0$, so training starts from the pretrained function exactly;
- after training, $BA$ can be folded into $W_0$, so inference cost is unchanged.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The counts are immediate. $B = 0$ gives $BA=0$ hence $W = W_0$. And $W_0 + \frac\alpha rBA$ is a
single $d\times k$ matrix, so it may be precomputed.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Why should a *low-rank* update suffice? The
<TopicRef id="deep-learning/transformers">multi-head decomposition</TopicRef> gives a partial answer:
the operative objects in an attention layer are already the low-rank matrices
$W_Q(W_K)^\top$ and $W_VW_O$, each of rank at most $d/h$. An update that changes attention behaviour
therefore need not be full rank to be expressive in the relevant sense.

That is an argument, not a theorem. The empirical finding that $r$ as small as $4$–$16$ recovers most
of full fine-tuning's gain on many tasks is stronger evidence, and the accompanying observation that
the *gradient* of the loss with respect to $W$ is low rank early in fine-tuning (being a sum of a few
outer products per batch) is suggestive. There is no result establishing that the optimal fine-tuning
update is approximately low rank in general, and tasks requiring substantial new knowledge do show a
gap.
</KeyIdea>

<Proposition id="moe-flops" title="Mixture of experts decouples parameters from FLOPs">
Replace an MLP block of $P$ parameters by $E$ expert blocks of $P$ parameters each, with a router
sending every token to the top $k$ experts. Then the parameter count is $EP$ while the per-token
FLOPs are proportional to $kP$. The ratio $E/k$ is the **sparsity factor**.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Parameters: $E$ copies. FLOPs: only the $k$ selected experts are evaluated per token, plus the
router's negligible $O(dE)$. Hence FLOPs $\propto kP$ independent of $E$.
</Proof>

<NotSaid>
The decoupling is real but the costs move rather than vanish. All $EP$ parameters must be resident in
memory (or fetched), so memory bandwidth rather than FLOPs becomes the constraint. Routing is
discrete and therefore non-differentiable, so training needs auxiliary load-balancing losses to
prevent expert collapse — a single expert receiving all tokens is a stable and useless equilibrium.
And the <TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">scaling laws</TopicRef> fitted for dense
models do not transfer directly: a sparse model's loss depends on $E$, $k$ and $P$ separately, and the
appropriate scaling law has to be refit.
</NotSaid>

## In-context learning

<Claim kind="empirical" id="icl-status" title="In-context learning">
A pretrained language model, with no parameter updates, performs tasks specified only by examples in
its prompt — including tasks it was not explicitly trained on — with accuracy improving in the number
of in-context examples.
</Claim>

<Theorem id="icl-linear-regression" title="Transformers can implement gradient descent in context">
There exist explicit weight settings for a linear-attention transformer such that the forward pass on
a prompt containing $(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_m,y_m),x_{\text{query}}$ computes the prediction of $\ell$
steps of gradient descent on the least-squares objective over those examples, where $\ell$ is the
number of layers.
</Theorem>

Quoted from von Oswald et al.; Garg et al. independently showed that transformers *trained* on
in-context regression tasks match or exceed the performance of least squares and of specialised
estimators on those tasks. The construction is explicit and checkable, which makes it a genuine
theorem about expressivity.

<NotSaid>
What these results establish and what they do not:

- **Established.** A transformer *can* implement an optimisation algorithm in its forward pass, and transformers trained specifically on function-class regression tasks *do* behave like good estimators for those classes. In-context learning is therefore not mysterious in principle — there is a mechanism a transformer can implement.
- **Not established.** That this is what a language model trained on text is doing. The constructions use linear attention, specific weight patterns, and a prompt format that encodes a clean regression problem; a language model's in-context behaviour on, say, translation or arithmetic has not been shown to be gradient descent on anything.

A separate and complementary line of evidence is mechanistic. Olsson et al. identify **induction
heads** — attention heads that implement the rule "find the previous occurrence of the current token
and copy what followed it" — and show that their formation during training coincides with a sharp
improvement in in-context learning ability, across model scales. This is strong evidence about *a*
mechanism contributing to in-context learning, established by circuit-level analysis and ablation.

The honest summary: the phenomenon is real and robust, two distinct partial mechanisms are documented
(implicit optimisation in constructed settings, induction heads in real models), and there is no
account of the general case. This sits in the same category as the other entries in the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">ledger</TopicRef>.
</NotSaid>
