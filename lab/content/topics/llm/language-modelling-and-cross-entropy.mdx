## The factorisation

<Proposition id="chain-rule-prob" title="The autoregressive factorisation is exact">
For any distribution $p$ on sequences $x = (x_1,\dots,x_T)$ over a finite vocabulary,
$$
p(x_1,\dots,x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^T p\big(x_t \mid x_{<t}\big),
$$
where $x_{<t} = (x_1,\dots,x_{t-1})$ and the $t=1$ factor is the marginal $p(x_1)$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Induction on $T$ using the definition of conditional probability:
$p(x_{\le T}) = p(x_{<T})\,p(x_T\mid x_{<T})$, and expand $p(x_{<T})$ by the inductive hypothesis.
The identity holds whenever the conditioning events have positive probability, which is automatic on
a finite vocabulary with full support.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The factorisation is an identity, not a modelling assumption. So an autoregressive language model
loses **no generality** — it can represent any distribution over sequences. What *is* a modelling
choice is (i) the parameterisation of each conditional, (ii) the left-to-right ordering, and
(iii) the tokenisation. Only (i) limits expressivity in the usual sense; (ii) matters for what is
easy to learn and for what inference is cheap; (iii) changes the object being modelled entirely.
</KeyIdea>

## Maximum likelihood and cross entropy

Let $q_\theta(\cdot\mid x_{<t})$ be the model's conditional, produced by a softmax over logits
$z_t = W_U h_t$ where $h_t$ is the final-layer representation at position $t$ and
$W_U\in\R^{|\Vcal|\times d}$ is the unembedding.

<Definition id="ce-def" title="Cross-entropy loss">
$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{|\Dcal|}\sum_{x\in\Dcal}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q_\theta\big(x_t\mid x_{<t}\big) .
$$
</Definition>

<Theorem id="mle-is-kl" title="Maximum likelihood is empirical KL minimisation">
Let $\hat p$ be the empirical distribution of the data. Then
$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = H(\hat p) + \mathrm{KL}\big(\hat p \,\|\, q_\theta\big),
$$
where $H(\hat p) = -\sum_x\hat p(x)\log\hat p(x)$ does not depend on $\theta$. Hence minimising
cross entropy is exactly minimising $\mathrm{KL}(\hat p\|q_\theta)$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_x\hat p(x)\log q_\theta(x)
= -\sum_x\hat p(x)\log\hat p(x) + \sum_x\hat p(x)\log\frac{\hat p(x)}{q_\theta(x)}
= H(\hat p) + \mathrm{KL}(\hat p\|q_\theta),
$$
where the first equality uses that the sum of per-token log-probabilities is the log-probability of
the sequence, by the factorisation.
</Proof>

<Note title="The direction of the KL matters">
The objective is $\mathrm{KL}(\hat p\|q_\theta)$, the **forward** KL, which is
mode-covering: it assigns infinite penalty to any sequence the data contains and the model gives
probability near zero, but only finite penalty to sequences the model invents. Reverse KL
$\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta\|\hat p)$ would be mode-seeking and would penalise invention harshly. Maximum
likelihood training therefore has a structural bias towards *hedging* — spreading probability mass
broadly — and the fact that language models sometimes produce plausible-looking fabrications is
consistent with the objective they were trained on. This is also why
<TopicRef id="llm/preference-optimisation">RLHF and DPO</TopicRef>, which use reverse-KL-flavoured
objectives, change the behaviour qualitatively and not just quantitatively.
</Note>

<Proposition id="entropy-floor" title="The loss cannot go below the conditional entropy">
Suppose the data is drawn from a true distribution $p$. Then for any model $q$,
$$
\E_{x_{<t}\sim p}\Big[-\sum_{v}p(v\mid x_{<t})\log q(v\mid x_{<t})\Big]
\;\ge\; \E_{x_{<t}\sim p}\big[H\big(p(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\big)\big],
$$
with equality if and only if $q = p$ almost everywhere.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Pointwise in $x_{<t}$, the difference between the two sides is
$\mathrm{KL}(p(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\|q(\cdot\mid x_{<t}))\ge0$, with equality iff the distributions
agree. Non-negativity of KL is Jensen applied to $-\log$:
$$
\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = -\sum_vp(v)\log\frac{q(v)}{p(v)} \ge -\log\sum_vp(v)\frac{q(v)}{p(v)} = -\log 1 = 0 ,
$$
with equality iff $q/p$ is constant, i.e. $q=p$. Taking expectations over $x_{<t}$ preserves the
inequality.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
This is the origin of the $\mathcal{L}_\infty$ term in the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">scaling laws</TopicRef>: a fitted loss curve
$\mathcal{L}(N,D) = \mathcal{L}_\infty + AN^{-\alpha}+BD^{-\beta}$ has an irreducible floor, and the
floor has an interpretation — it is (an upper bound on) the conditional entropy of the true text
distribution at the given tokenisation. Language is genuinely stochastic; no model, however large,
reaches zero loss.
</KeyIdea>

## Perplexity, and why it is not portable

<Definition id="perplexity-def" title="Perplexity">
$$
\mathrm{PPL} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T -\log q_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})\right) = e^{\bar{\mathcal{L}}} .
$$
If the model were uniform over $k$ equally likely options at each step, the perplexity would be
exactly $k$ — hence the reading as an **effective branching factor**.
</Definition>

<Proposition id="tokenisation-perplexity" title="Perplexity is not comparable across tokenisers">
Let a corpus have $C$ characters and be tokenised two ways, into $T_1$ and $T_2$ tokens. Two models
assigning the *same probability* to the corpus have per-token losses
$$
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_i = \frac{-\log q(\text{corpus})}{T_i},
$$
so their perplexities differ by the factor $\exp\big(-\log q\cdot(1/T_1-1/T_2)\big)$. Only the
**per-character** (or per-byte) loss $-\log q(\text{corpus})/C$ is tokeniser-independent.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Immediate from the definitions: the numerator $-\log q(\text{corpus})$ is a property of the
distribution over the text, while the denominator is a property of the tokenisation.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Common mistake">
Comparing perplexities of models with different vocabularies or tokenisers is meaningless. A model
with a larger vocabulary uses fewer tokens per character and will report a *higher* per-token
perplexity for the same quality of text prediction. Always convert to bits-per-byte or
bits-per-character before comparing across tokenisers — and note that the
<TopicRef id="deep-learning-theory/what-is-proven">scaling law</TopicRef> exponents are also
tokeniser-dependent for the same reason.
</Warning>

## Gradients

<Proposition id="softmax-gradient" title="The cross-entropy gradient is the prediction error">
Let $z\in\R^{|\Vcal|}$ be the logits, $q = \operatorname{softmax}(z)$, and the loss
$\ell = -\log q_{v^\star}$ for the true token $v^\star$. Then
$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z} = q - e_{v^\star},
$$
and consequently $\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial W_U} = (q-e_{v^\star})h^\top$ and
$\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial h} = W_U^\top(q-e_{v^\star})$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
$\ell = -z_{v^\star} + \log\sum_ve^{z_v}$, so
$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z_v} = -\one\{v=v^\star\} + \frac{e^{z_v}}{\sum_{v'}e^{z_{v'}}} = q_v - \one\{v=v^\star\}.
$$
The parameter gradients follow from $z = W_Uh$ by the chain rule.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The gradient is the *prediction minus the target* — exactly as in
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/logistic-regression">logistic regression</TopicRef> and
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/linear-and-ridge-regression">linear regression</TopicRef>. The
composition of softmax and cross entropy is what makes this clean: the softmax Jacobian
$\diag(q)-qq^\top$, which
<TopicRef id="deep-learning/transformers">saturates and kills gradients</TopicRef> on its own,
combines with the $1/q_{v^\star}$ from the logarithm to cancel exactly. This is why the two are
always implemented as a single fused operation, and why using a softmax with a squared loss instead
reintroduces the saturation problem.
</KeyIdea>

## Sampling

<Proposition id="temperature-entropy" title="Temperature monotonically controls entropy">
Let $q^{(\tau)} = \operatorname{softmax}(z/\tau)$. Then $H(q^{(\tau)})$ is non-decreasing in
$\tau>0$, with $q^{(\tau)}\to$ uniform as $\tau\to\infty$ and $q^{(\tau)}\to$ the argmax one-hot as
$\tau\to0^+$ (assuming a unique maximum).
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Write $\beta = 1/\tau$ and $q^{(\beta)}_v \propto e^{\beta z_v}$, an exponential family in $\beta$
with sufficient statistic $z$. Its log-partition function $\psi(\beta) = \log\sum_ve^{\beta z_v}$
satisfies $\psi''(\beta) = \Var_{q^{(\beta)}}(z) \ge 0$, so $\psi$ is convex. The entropy is
$$
H(q^{(\beta)}) = \psi(\beta) - \beta\psi'(\beta),
$$
since $\E_{q^{(\beta)}}[z] = \psi'(\beta)$. Differentiating,
$$
\frac{dH}{d\beta} = \psi'(\beta) - \psi'(\beta) - \beta\psi''(\beta) = -\beta\,\Var_{q^{(\beta)}}(z) \le 0 .
$$
So $H$ decreases in $\beta = 1/\tau$, i.e. increases in $\tau$. The limits are direct: as
$\beta\to0$ all logits are equal after scaling; as $\beta\to\infty$ the largest logit dominates.
</Proof>

<Note title="Why truncated sampling exists">
Temperature reshapes the whole distribution, including the long tail of very low-probability tokens.
Over a $10^5$-token vocabulary, the tail can hold substantial total mass even when every individual
token is implausible, and sampling from it produces the degeneration Holtzman et al. document.
Top-$k$ and top-$p$ (nucleus) sampling truncate the tail before renormalising, which is a different
operation from lowering the temperature: temperature is a monotone reweighting of *all* tokens,
truncation removes support. Neither corresponds to a change in the model's beliefs — both are
decoding-time interventions, and a model's perplexity is unaffected by them.
</Note>
