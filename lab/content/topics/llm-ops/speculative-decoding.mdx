<Intuition>
Decoding is memory-bound: emitting one token costs a full sweep of the weights through memory, and
the arithmetic units idle through it. But verifying a *guess* at the next $\gamma$ tokens costs the
same single sweep, because the target model can score $\gamma+1$ positions in one parallel forward
pass — exactly the work that was going to waste.

So let a small, cheap model guess ahead, then have the big model check the guesses in one pass. When
the guesses are good you get several tokens for the price of one. The remarkable part is that the
output distribution is **not** an approximation of the big model's: with the right accept/reject rule
it is exactly the big model's, whatever the small model does.
</Intuition>

## The algorithm

<Algorithm title="Speculative sampling, one round">
1. Given context $x_{&lt;t}$, sample $\gamma$ draft tokens autoregressively from the draft model:
   $\tilde x_{t+i} \sim q(\cdot \mid x_{&lt;t}, \tilde x_{t:t+i-1})$ for $i = 0,\dots,\gamma-1$.
2. Run the target model **once**, in parallel over all $\gamma+1$ positions, to obtain
   $p(\cdot \mid x_{&lt;t}, \tilde x_{t:t+i-1})$ for every $i$.
3. For $i = 0,1,\dots,\gamma-1$ in order, draw $u \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ and accept
   $\tilde x_{t+i}$ if
   $$
   u \ \le\ \min\left(1,\ \frac{p(\tilde x_{t+i})}{q(\tilde x_{t+i})}\right).
   $$
   Stop at the first rejection.
4. If a rejection occurred at position $i$, emit one token drawn from the **residual distribution**
   $$
   p'(x) \ \propto\ \max\big(0,\ p(x) - q(x)\big),
   $$
   and discard the remaining drafts. If all $\gamma$ were accepted, emit one extra token from
   $p(\cdot \mid x_{&lt;t}, \tilde x_{t:t+\gamma-1})$, which step 2 already computed.
</Algorithm>

Every round costs $\gamma$ draft passes plus **one** target pass, and yields between $1$ and
$\gamma+1$ tokens.

## Exactness

<Claim kind="theorem" id="spec-correctness" title="Speculative sampling is exact">
Fix distributions $p$ and $q$ on a finite vocabulary $\mathcal V$ with $q(x) &gt; 0$ wherever
$p(x) &gt; 0$. Draw $X \sim q$, accept with probability $\min(1, p(X)/q(X))$, and on rejection draw
$Y \sim p'$ where $p'(x) = \max(0, p(x)-q(x))/\beta$ with
$\beta = \sum_{x}\max(0, p(x)-q(x))$. Then the emitted token is distributed exactly as $p$.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
Absolute continuity of $p$ with respect to $q$ on the support — if the draft assigns zero probability
to a token the target wants, that token can never be proposed, and the residual step is what repairs
it. Note the theorem assumes nothing about how *good* $q$ is: a uniform draft model gives an exact
sampler, just a slow one.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Write $Z$ for the emitted token and fix $x \in \mathcal V$. There are two disjoint routes to
emitting $x$.

**Accepted.** The proposal must be $x$ and must survive:
$$
\P(\text{propose } x \text{ and accept}) = q(x)\cdot\min\left(1, \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) = \min\big(q(x), p(x)\big).
$$

**Rejected, then resampled.** The total rejection probability is
$$
\P(\text{reject}) = 1 - \sum_{y}\min\big(q(y),p(y)\big) = \sum_y \big(p(y) - \min(p(y),q(y))\big) = \sum_y \max\big(0, p(y)-q(y)\big) = \beta,
$$
using $\sum_y p(y) = 1$ and the identity $p - \min(p,q) = \max(0, p-q)$. Conditional on rejection the
token is drawn from $p'$, so this route contributes
$$
\beta \cdot p'(x) = \beta \cdot \frac{\max(0, p(x)-q(x))}{\beta} = \max\big(0, p(x)-q(x)\big).
$$

Adding the two routes and using $\min(a,b) + \max(0, a-b) = a$ with $a = p(x)$, $b = q(x)$:
$$
\P(Z = x) = \min\big(p(x),q(x)\big) + \max\big(0, p(x)-q(x)\big) = p(x). \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The draft model affects **speed only, never quality**. This is what separates speculative decoding
from approximations like early exit or cascades: there is no quality/latency dial to tune, because
there is no quality loss to trade. A deployment can adopt it without re-running evaluations.
</KeyIdea>

<Details summary="Why the same argument extends to a whole accepted run">
Applying the single-token result at position $t$ shows the emitted token has the target's conditional
distribution given $x_{&lt;t}$. Conditional on that token, position $t+1$ is verified against
$p(\cdot \mid x_{&lt;t}, x_t)$ — the target's conditional given the *accepted* prefix, which step 2
computed. So by induction along the accepted run, the joint distribution of emitted tokens telescopes
into $\prod_i p(x_{t+i} \mid x_{&lt;t+i})$, which is exactly the target model's joint. The rejection
step truncates the run but, by the theorem, emits a correctly distributed token at the truncation
point.
</Details>

## How often do we accept?

<Claim kind="proposition" id="acceptance-tv" title="Acceptance probability equals one minus total variation distance">
The probability that a proposal from $q$ is accepted is
$$
\alpha = \sum_{x}\min\big(p(x), q(x)\big) = 1 - \mathrm{TV}(p,q),
\qquad
\mathrm{TV}(p,q) = \tfrac12\sum_x |p(x)-q(x)| .
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
The first equality was computed inside the proof above. For the second, use
$\min(a,b) = \tfrac12(a + b - |a-b|)$:
$$
\sum_x \min(p,q) = \tfrac12\left(\sum_x p(x) + \sum_x q(x) - \sum_x|p(x)-q(x)|\right)
= \tfrac12\big(1 + 1 - 2\,\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\big) = 1 - \mathrm{TV}(p,q). \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Proof>

This is a clean statement of what makes a good draft model: not low perplexity in the usual sense,
but **small total variation distance to the target's conditional, token by token.** A draft that is
confidently wrong is worse than one that is diffusely uncertain, because TV punishes mass placed
where the target puts none.

## Expected yield per round

<Claim kind="proposition" id="expected-tokens" title="Expected tokens per verification round">
Suppose each draft position is accepted independently with probability $\alpha \in [0,1)$. Then a
round with draft length $\gamma$ emits
$$
\E[\,\text{tokens}\,] = \frac{1 - \alpha^{\gamma+1}}{1-\alpha}
$$
tokens in expectation.
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
Independence and a common $\alpha$ across positions. Both are idealisations: acceptance at later
positions is conditioned on the earlier draft tokens being accepted, which correlates the events, and
$\alpha$ varies token by token — easy continuations accept far more often than hard ones. Treat this
as a first-order model, and see the caveat at the end.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
Let $n \in \lbrace 0,1,\dots,\gamma \rbrace$ be the number of accepted drafts. For $j &lt; \gamma$,
$n = j$ requires $j$ acceptances then a rejection, so $\P(n=j) = \alpha^j(1-\alpha)$; and
$\P(n=\gamma) = \alpha^\gamma$. Exactly one further token is emitted in either case — the residual
sample on rejection, or the target's own next token when all drafts survive — so the yield is $n+1$.

Rather than summing the series directly, use the tail formula for a non-negative integer variable:
$$
\E[n] = \sum_{j=1}^{\gamma} \P(n \ge j) = \sum_{j=1}^{\gamma} \alpha^{j},
$$
since $n \ge j$ exactly when the first $j$ drafts were all accepted, which has probability
$\alpha^j$. Hence
$$
\E[n+1] = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^{\gamma}\alpha^j = \sum_{j=0}^{\gamma}\alpha^j = \frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{1-\alpha}. \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Proof>

As $\gamma \to \infty$ this saturates at $1/(1-\alpha)$: **however long you draft, you cannot average
more than $1/(1-\alpha)$ tokens per target pass.** At $\alpha = 0.8$ the ceiling is 5 tokens per
round, and $\gamma = 8$ already achieves $4.6$ of them.

## Speedup, and how far to draft

<Claim kind="corollary" id="speculative-speedup" title="Speedup and the optimal draft length">
Let the draft model cost a fraction $c \in (0,1)$ of a target forward pass. Relative to ordinary
decoding, the wall-clock speedup is
$$
\mathcal S(\gamma) = \frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{(1-\alpha)\,(\gamma c + 1)} ,
$$
which for $c &gt; 0$ is maximised at a finite $\gamma$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
A round costs $\gamma$ draft passes and one target pass, i.e. $\gamma c + 1$ target-pass units, and
yields $(1-\alpha^{\gamma+1})/(1-\alpha)$ tokens by the previous proposition. Ordinary decoding
yields one token per target pass, so the ratio of tokens per unit time is $\mathcal S(\gamma)$.

For finiteness of the maximiser: the numerator is bounded above by $1/(1-\alpha)$ while the
denominator grows without bound in $\gamma$, so $\mathcal S(\gamma) \to 0$. Since
$\mathcal S(0) = 1 &gt; 0$ and $\mathcal S$ is positive, its supremum over the non-negative integers is
attained. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Details summary="Worked example: a 7B draft for a 70B target">
Take $c = 0.1$ (the draft is roughly ten times cheaper) and measure $\alpha = 0.8$.

| $\gamma$ | $\E[\text{tokens}]$ | cost | $\mathcal S$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1.80 | 1.1 | 1.64 |
| 2 | 2.44 | 1.2 | 2.03 |
| 4 | 3.36 | 1.4 | 2.40 |
| 6 | 3.95 | 1.6 | 2.47 |
| 8 | 4.33 | 1.8 | 2.41 |
| 12 | 4.72 | 2.2 | 2.15 |

The optimum sits near $\gamma = 6$ at about $2.5\times$. The curve is flat near its peak — anything
from 4 to 8 is within a few percent — which is fortunate, because $\alpha$ is not constant in
practice and a sharp optimum would be untunable.

Note also how sensitive this is to $\alpha$: at $\alpha = 0.5$ the ceiling $1/(1-\alpha)$ is only 2,
so no draft length gets past a $2\times$ speedup, and after paying $c$ the realised gain is closer to
$1.6\times$.
</Details>

<Warning>
The independence assumption behind $\E[\text{tokens}]$ is optimistic. Acceptance is strongly
correlated along a draft: once the draft model diverges from the target's intent it tends to keep
diverging, so runs of rejections cluster and the realised mean falls below the formula. Measure the
empirical distribution of accepted-run lengths rather than assuming a geometric one, then choose
$\gamma$ from the measurement.
</Warning>

<Empirical>
Reported end-to-end speedups for well-matched draft/target pairs cluster around $2$–$3\times$ on
standard generation workloads, with acceptance rates in the range $0.7$–$0.9$ — consistent with the
model above once correlation is accounted for. Gains are smaller on tasks where the target's
conditionals are high-entropy, which is exactly what $\alpha = 1 - \mathrm{TV}$ predicts.
</Empirical>

<NotSaid>
This analysis is about *latency for a single stream*. Speculative decoding spends extra FLOPs to buy
back idle memory-bandwidth time, so its advantage shrinks as batch size grows and the system moves
towards compute-bound — precisely the regime a throughput-oriented server tries to reach. It is a
latency technique, and on a saturated server it can reduce total throughput.
</NotSaid>
