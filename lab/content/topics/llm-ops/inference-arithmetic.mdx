<Intuition>
A GPU can do arithmetic far faster than it can fetch the numbers to do arithmetic *on*. An H100
does roughly 10<sup>15</sup> floating-point operations per second but moves only about
3.4 × 10<sup>12</sup> bytes per second from its own memory. That is a ratio of roughly 300 operations
per byte. If your computation does fewer than ~300 operations for every byte it loads, the arithmetic
units sit idle waiting for data, and buying a faster chip buys you nothing.

Generating text with a transformer has two phases with opposite characters. Reading the prompt does a
lot of arithmetic per byte loaded. Emitting one token at a time does almost none. Nearly every
surprising fact about LLM serving follows from that single asymmetry.
</Intuition>

## Counting the work

Fix a decoder-only transformer with $L$ layers, model width $d$, and $N$ non-embedding parameters.
We count multiply–accumulate as two FLOPs, the standard convention.

<Claim kind="proposition" id="forward-flops" title="Forward-pass cost of a transformer">
A forward pass over a sequence of $S$ tokens costs
$$
C_{\text{fwd}}(S) \ \approx\ \underbrace{2NS}_{\text{weights}} \;+\; \underbrace{4LdS^2}_{\text{attention}}
\quad\text{FLOPs}.
$$
</Claim>

<Assumptions>
- Every parameter participates in exactly one multiply–accumulate per token. This is exact for dense
  matrix multiplications and ignores layer norms, activations and residual adds, whose cost is
  $O(LdS)$ and therefore negligible against both terms.
- Attention is dense (every query attends to every key). Sparse, sliding-window or linear attention
  variants change the second term and nothing else.
- Embedding and unembedding parameters are excluded from $N$, per the usual convention.
</Assumptions>

<Proof>
**Weight term.** A matrix multiply $X W$ with $X \in \R^{S \times m}$ and $W \in \R^{m \times n}$
costs $2Smn$ FLOPs: each of the $Sn$ output entries is a length-$m$ dot product, which is $m$
multiplies and $m-1$ adds, i.e. $2m$ FLOPs to leading order. The parameter count of that layer is
$mn$, so its cost is $2S \times (\text{its parameter count})$. Summing over every weight matrix in
the network gives $2NS$.

**Attention term.** Within one layer, forming the score matrix $QK^\top$ multiplies
$\R^{S\times d}$ by $\R^{d \times S}$, costing $2S^2d$ FLOPs. Applying the normalised scores to $V$
multiplies $\R^{S \times S}$ by $\R^{S \times d}$, another $2S^2d$. That is $4S^2d$ per layer, and
$4LdS^2$ over the network. Note these two products involve no parameters, which is why they are not
already counted in $2NS$.

The softmax itself is $O(LS^2)$ elementwise work, smaller by a factor of $d$, and is dropped.
</Proof>

### When does attention actually dominate?

The quadratic term overtakes the linear one when $4LdS^2 > 2NS$, that is when
$$
S \;>\; S_\star \;:=\; \frac{N}{2Ld}.
$$

For a 7B-parameter model with $L = 32$ and $d = 4096$:
$$
S_\star = \frac{6.7\times 10^9}{2 \cdot 32 \cdot 4096} \approx 2.6 \times 10^4 .
$$

<KeyIdea>
Below roughly 25,000 tokens of context, attention is **not** where the FLOPs go — the feed-forward
and projection weights are. This is why "attention is quadratic" is a misleading summary of
short-context inference cost, and why it becomes the correct summary for long-context inference.
The crossover is a property of the model's shape, not of attention as such.
</KeyIdea>

## The roofline

<Claim kind="proposition" id="roofline-bound" title="The roofline bound">
Let a computation perform $F$ FLOPs while moving $Q$ bytes between memory and the processor, and
define its **arithmetic intensity** $I = F/Q$. On hardware with peak throughput $\pi$ FLOP/s and
memory bandwidth $\beta$ byte/s, the attainable throughput satisfies
$$
P \ \le\ \min\big(\pi,\ I\beta\big).
$$
</Claim>

<Proof>
The computation cannot finish faster than the arithmetic takes: $T \ge F/\pi$. It also cannot finish
faster than the data movement takes: $T \ge Q/\beta$. Hence
$$
T \ \ge\ \max\left(\frac{F}{\pi},\ \frac{Q}{\beta}\right),
\qquad
P = \frac{F}{T} \ \le\ \min\left(\pi,\ \frac{F\beta}{Q}\right) = \min(\pi, I\beta). \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Proof>

The two regimes meet at the **ridge point** $I^\star = \pi/\beta$. A kernel with $I < I^\star$ is
*memory-bound*; one with $I > I^\star$ is *compute-bound*.

<Note>
For an NVIDIA H100 SXM: $\pi \approx 990$ TFLOP/s in BF16 with dense tensor cores and
$\beta \approx 3.35$ TB/s of HBM3 bandwidth, giving
$$
I^\star = \frac{9.9\times10^{14}}{3.35\times10^{12}} \approx 296 \text{ FLOP/byte}.
$$
Treat these as order-of-magnitude figures: vendor peak numbers assume perfect utilisation that real
kernels do not reach, and the ratio is what matters here, not the absolute values.
</Note>

## The two phases have opposite intensity

### Prefill

Processing a prompt of $S$ tokens does $\approx 2NS$ FLOPs while loading the $N$ weights once, i.e.
$Nb$ bytes for $b$ bytes per parameter. Its intensity is
$$
I_{\text{prefill}} \approx \frac{2NS}{Nb} = \frac{2S}{b}.
$$
At $b = 2$ (BF16), $I_{\text{prefill}} \approx S$. Any prompt longer than a few hundred tokens clears
the ridge point comfortably: **prefill is compute-bound.**

### Decode

<Claim kind="proposition" id="decode-intensity" title="Decode arithmetic intensity is set by the batch size alone">
Generating one token for each of $B$ sequences, in the regime where weight traffic dominates,
has arithmetic intensity
$$
I_{\text{decode}} \approx \frac{2B}{b},
$$
independent of $N$, $L$ and $d$.
</Claim>

<Proof>
One decoding step processes a single new token per sequence, so it performs $2NB$ FLOPs. The weights
are read once for the whole batch — this is exactly what batching buys — costing $Nb$ bytes. Thus
$$
I_{\text{decode}} = \frac{2NB}{Nb} = \frac{2B}{b},
$$
and $N$ cancels. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<NotSaid>
This ignores KV-cache traffic, which also grows with $B$ and with context length and is the other
half of decode's memory bill. Including it lowers the intensity further, so the conclusion below —
that small-batch decode is memory-bound — only strengthens. The proposition is an upper bound on
intensity, not an estimate of it.
</NotSaid>

At $b = 2$, $I_{\text{decode}} \approx B$. To reach an H100's ridge point you need a batch of roughly
**300 concurrent sequences**. Below that, the tensor cores are idle most of the time and the only
thing that matters is how fast weights stream out of HBM.

<Claim kind="corollary" id="decode-latency-floor" title="A hard floor on single-stream decode latency">
With $N$ parameters at $b$ bytes each and memory bandwidth $\beta$, the time to emit one token
satisfies
$$
t_{\text{step}} \ \ge\ \frac{Nb}{\beta},
$$
whatever the implementation.
</Claim>

<Proof>
Producing the token requires every parameter to participate in the computation, so every parameter
must be read from memory at least once: $Q \ge Nb$. By the roofline argument
$t_{\text{step}} \ge Q/\beta \ge Nb/\beta$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

<Details summary="Worked example: the ceiling on single-stream generation">
A 7B model in BF16 occupies $Nb = 6.7\times10^9 \times 2 = 13.4$ GB. On one H100 at
$\beta = 3.35$ TB/s,
$$
t_{\text{step}} \ge \frac{1.34\times10^{10}}{3.35\times10^{12}} \approx 4.0\ \text{ms}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{at most } \approx 250 \text{ tokens/s}
$$
for a single sequence. A 70B model in BF16 is 140 GB — it does not fit on one 80 GB card at all, and
split across two H100s the same argument gives $t_{\text{step}} \gtrsim 21$ ms, i.e. under
50 tokens/s, before accounting for the interconnect.

Two consequences worth internalising:
- **Single-stream latency is a bandwidth problem, not a FLOPs problem.** A chip with twice the
  arithmetic and the same memory system would not move this number at all.
- **Quantisation is a latency technique.** Halving $b$ from 2 to 1 halves the floor directly. That,
  not memory capacity, is the main reason low-precision weights speed up decoding.
</Details>

## What this predicts, and where it fails

<Empirical>
Measured decode throughput on production stacks rises roughly linearly with batch size in the
small-batch regime and then flattens, with the knee in the low hundreds of sequences — the qualitative
shape this analysis predicts.
</Empirical>

<Warning>
The roofline is a *bound*, not a forecast. Real kernels fall short of it for reasons it does not
model: cache hierarchy and reuse, kernel launch overhead, tensor-core tile quantisation, imperfect
overlap of compute and transfer, and — in multi-GPU settings — interconnect bandwidth, which for
tensor-parallel decoding is frequently the true constraint rather than HBM. Use the roofline to rule
things out ("no kernel can beat 4 ms/token here"), not to predict what you will observe.
</Warning>

<OpenProblem>
There is no accepted analytical model that predicts *achieved* throughput of a full serving stack
from model shape and hardware specifications. Practitioners benchmark. The roofline gives a ceiling
and the gap to it is measured, not derived — a situation that is normal in performance engineering
and worth stating plainly rather than papering over.
</OpenProblem>
