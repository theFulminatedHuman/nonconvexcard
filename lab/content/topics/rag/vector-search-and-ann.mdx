## Exact search

<Proposition id="exact-cost" title="Cost of exact nearest-neighbour search">
For $N$ vectors in $\R^d$, brute-force search costs $O(Nd)$ time per query and $O(Nd)$ space, with no
preprocessing. Every known data structure achieving query time $o(N)$ for exact nearest neighbour in
the worst case uses space exponential in $d$ or is no better than brute force once
$d = \Omega(\log N)$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The brute-force bound is immediate: compute $N$ inner products of length $d$ and take the maximum.
Space and time are both $Nd$. The negative statement is the summary of the fine-grained-complexity
literature and is quoted rather than proved; the accessible half of the phenomenon is the tree-pruning
obstruction below.
</Proof>

<Proposition id="tree-failure" title="Why space-partitioning trees fail in high dimension">
Consider a $k$-d tree or any partition of $\R^d$ into cells, with pruning based on the rule
"skip a cell whose bounding box is farther than the current best distance $r$". If the data is
i.i.d. with independent coordinates, then by
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-of-the-norm">distance concentration</TopicRef>
all $N$ distances lie in a window of width $O(\sqrt{\log N})$ around $\sqrt{2d}$, so
$$
\frac{r}{\text{typical distance}} \to 1 ,
$$
and the ball of radius $r$ around the query intersects essentially every cell. Pruning eliminates
nothing and the search degenerates to brute force.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The concentration statement is the proposition on that page: with probability $1-\delta$, all
distances lie in $\sqrt{2d}\pm C\sqrt{\log(N/\delta)}$. The current best distance $r$ after examining
any subset is therefore at least $\sqrt{2d}-C\sqrt{\log(N/\delta)}$, while the distance from the
query to any point is at most $\sqrt{2d}+C\sqrt{\log(N/\delta)}$. A cell can be pruned only if every
point in it is farther than $r$, which requires the cell to be at distance more than
$r \approx \sqrt{2d}$ from the query — but no cell containing data is, since all data is at distance
$\approx\sqrt{2d}$. Hence no cell containing data is pruned.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
This is the sharpest form of the curse of dimensionality for search: it is not that trees are slow,
it is that the pruning *criterion* becomes vacuous. Once exact search is hopeless, the only way
forward is to relax the problem, and every method below relaxes it in one of two ways: return an
approximately-nearest neighbour ($c$-ANN), or return the true nearest neighbour with probability less
than one.
</KeyIdea>

## Locality-sensitive hashing

<Definition id="lsh-def" title="Locality-sensitive hash family">
A family $\mathcal{H}$ of hash functions is **$(r, cr, p_1, p_2)$-sensitive** for a distance $\rho$ if
for $h$ drawn uniformly from $\mathcal{H}$,
$$
\rho(u,v)\le r \implies \P\big(h(u)=h(v)\big)\ge p_1,
\qquad
\rho(u,v)\ge cr \implies \P\big(h(u)=h(v)\big)\le p_2,
$$
with $p_1>p_2$. The **exponent** is $\varrho = \dfrac{\log(1/p_1)}{\log(1/p_2)} \in (0,1)$.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="simhash-probability" title="Random-hyperplane LSH collision probability">
Let $h_w(u) = \sign\ip wu$ for $w\sim\Ncal(0,I_d)$. Then for any non-zero $u,v$,
$$
\P\big(h_w(u)\ne h_w(v)\big) = \frac{\theta(u,v)}{\pi},
\qquad \theta(u,v) = \arccos\frac{\ip uv}{\norm u\norm v} .
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
The event $\{h_w(u)\ne h_w(v)\}$ says the hyperplane $w^\perp$ separates $u$ and $v$. Only the
component of $w$ in the two-dimensional plane $P = \operatorname{span}(u,v)$ matters, and by
rotational invariance of the Gaussian, the projection of $w$ onto $P$ is a two-dimensional Gaussian,
hence uniform in direction.

Within $P$, the separating directions form the union of two arcs, each of angular width $\theta$ —
the directions lying strictly between $u^\perp$ and $v^\perp$ on either side. The total angular
measure of separating directions is $2\theta$ out of $2\pi$, so the probability is $\theta/\pi$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
The collision probability is $1 - \theta/\pi$, a monotone function of the angle. Combined with the
<TopicRef id="rag/embedding-geometry-and-similarity">cosine geometry</TopicRef>, that makes SimHash
the natural LSH family for cosine retrieval: it hashes by direction only, which is exactly what
cosine similarity measures. A single bit costs one inner product, and $b$ bits give a $b$-bit sketch
whose Hamming distance estimates the angle unbiasedly with variance $O(1/b)$.
</KeyIdea>

<Proposition id="lsh-amplification" title="AND/OR amplification and the LSH exponent">
Concatenate $k$ independent hashes into $g = (h_1,\dots,h_k)$ (an AND, requiring all to match) and
build $L$ independent tables (an OR, accepting a match in any). Then

- a near pair collides in a given table with probability $p_1^k$, hence in some table with probability $1-(1-p_1^k)^L$;
- a far pair collides in a given table with probability $p_2^k$, so the expected number of far candidates examined is $LNp_2^k$.

Choosing $k = \log_{1/p_2}N$ and $L = N^{\varrho}$ with $\varrho = \log(1/p_1)/\log(1/p_2)$ gives
query time $\tilde O(N^\varrho)$ and constant success probability, with space $\tilde O(N^{1+\varrho})$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
With $k = \log_{1/p_2}N$ we get $p_2^k = 1/N$, so the expected far candidates per table is
$Np_2^k = 1$, and $L$ tables give $O(L)$ candidates. The near-pair collision probability per table is
$$
p_1^k = p_1^{\log N/\log(1/p_2)} = N^{-\log(1/p_1)/\log(1/p_2)} = N^{-\varrho},
$$
so taking $L = N^\varrho$ makes $1-(1-p_1^k)^L \ge 1-e^{-1}$, a constant. Total query work is $L$ hash
computations plus $O(L)$ candidate distance evaluations, giving $\tilde O(N^\varrho)$; space is $L$
tables of $N$ entries.
</Proof>

<Note title="Reading the exponent">
$\varrho<1$ is genuinely sublinear, which was the breakthrough of Indyk and Motwani: the curse of
dimensionality is *removed* for the approximate problem. For SimHash at approximation factor $c$ the
exponent is roughly $1/c$, so a $2$-approximation costs $\tilde O(\sqrt N)$. Data-dependent hashing
(Andoni–Razenshteyn) achieves the optimal $\varrho = 1/(2c^2-1)$ for Euclidean distance, and there are
matching lower bounds — so the approximate problem's complexity is essentially settled, which is a
striking contrast with the exact one.
</Note>

## IVF and product quantisation

<Definition id="ivf-def" title="Inverted file index (IVF)">
Cluster the $N$ vectors into $n_{\text{list}}$ Voronoi cells by $k$-means. At query time, compute
distances to the $n_{\text{list}}$ centroids, select the $n_{\text{probe}}$ nearest cells, and search
only those. Expected cost per query:
$$
O\!\left(n_{\text{list}}d + \frac{n_{\text{probe}}}{n_{\text{list}}}Nd\right),
$$
minimised over $n_{\text{list}}$ at $n_{\text{list}}\asymp\sqrt{N n_{\text{probe}}}$, giving
$O(\sqrt{Nn_{\text{probe}}}\,d)$.
</Definition>

<Proposition id="pq-distortion" title="Product quantisation error and compression">
Split $x\in\R^d$ into $m$ contiguous sub-vectors of dimension $d/m$ and quantise each with its own
codebook of $2^b$ centroids. Then

- storage is $mb$ bits per vector, independent of $d$ — for $m=8$, $b=8$ that is $8$ bytes, a $384\times$ compression of a $768$-dimensional float32 vector;
- the squared error decomposes as $\norm{x-\hat x}_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^m\norm{x^{(j)} - \hat x^{(j)}}_2^2$;
- the codebook has $m2^b$ centroids representing $2^{mb}$ distinct quantised points.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Storage: $m$ indices of $b$ bits. The error decomposition is additivity of the squared Euclidean norm
across a partition of the coordinates, which is exactly why the sub-vectors are chosen to partition
(not overlap). The count: independent choices in each of $m$ blocks give $(2^b)^m$ combinations, while
only $m2^b$ centroids are stored — the **combinatorial** gain that makes the method work.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Product quantisation gets $2^{mb}$ representable points from $m2^b$ stored ones. At $m=8,b=8$ that is
$2^{64}$ points from $2048$ centroids. And asymmetric distance computation — quantising only the
database, keeping the query exact — lets the distance to every code be read from $m$ precomputed
lookup tables of $2^b$ entries, so a distance evaluation is $m$ table lookups and additions rather
than $d$ multiplications. That is the actual source of the speedup; the compression is what makes the
index fit in memory.
</KeyIdea>

## HNSW

<Definition id="hnsw-def" title="Hierarchical navigable small world graph">
Build a multi-layer proximity graph. Each vector is inserted at a random level
$\ell\sim\lfloor-\log U/\log M\rfloor$; at each level it is connected to its approximate $M$ nearest
neighbours among nodes present at that level. Search starts at the top (sparsest) layer, greedily
descends to the closest node, then drops a layer and repeats, finishing with a best-first search at
layer $0$ maintaining a candidate list of size $\mathrm{efSearch}$.
</Definition>

<Claim kind="empirical" id="hnsw-status" title="HNSW has no worst-case guarantee">
HNSW is the default index in most production vector databases and dominates ANN benchmarks at high
recall. It has no proof of its recall or query complexity on general data.
</Claim>

<NotSaid>
The heuristic justification is the small-world argument: a graph with short-range links (for accuracy)
and long-range links (for navigation) has $O(\log N)$ greedy routing, by analogy with Kleinberg's
navigable small-world results. That analogy is not a proof for HNSW: Kleinberg's theorems are about
specific lattice-plus-power-law-link constructions, and HNSW's links come from the data's own
geometry.

What is actually known:

- **No recall guarantee.** There is no bound of the form "HNSW with parameters $(M,\mathrm{efSearch})$ achieves recall $\ge1-\delta$", for any nontrivial data assumption.
- **Adversarial failure is possible and observed.** Greedy graph search can get trapped in a local minimum of the distance function; clustered data with a query between clusters is a realistic hard case.
- **Construction is $O(N\log N)$ empirically**, not provably, and is expensive in absolute terms; the index is also awkward to update and delete from.
- **LSH has guarantees but loses on benchmarks.** This is the honest tension: the method with theory is not the method people use, and the method people use has no theory.

So recall is measured, not proved, and measured on the specific corpus and query distribution. A
recall figure from a public benchmark does not transfer to a different corpus.
</NotSaid>

<Proposition id="recall-composition" title="Recall composes multiplicatively through a pipeline">
Let a retrieval pipeline consist of stages with recall $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_m$ — where
$\gamma_i$ is the probability that stage $i$ passes on the relevant document given that it received
it. Then the end-to-end recall is $\prod_i\gamma_i$, and in particular is at most
$\min_i\gamma_i$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The relevant document survives to the end only if every stage passes it. Conditioning stage by stage
and multiplying the conditional probabilities gives the product; each factor is at most $1$, so the
product is at most any single factor.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
This is the most consequential and most ignored fact about RAG system design. A cross-encoder
reranker cannot recover a document the ANN index did not return: if the index has recall $0.9$, the
system's ceiling is $0.9$ however good the reranker is. Tuning the reranker while the index silently
drops $10\%$ of relevant documents is effort spent on the wrong stage.

The same multiplicative structure propagates into generation, quantified on the
<TopicRef id="rag/retrieval-as-latent-variable-model">latent-variable</TopicRef> page: retrieval
failure is not a soft degradation but a hard ceiling on the information available to the model.
</KeyIdea>

## Comparison

| Method | Query time | Space | Guarantee | Metric |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brute force | $O(Nd)$ | $O(Nd)$ | exact | any |
| LSH (SimHash) | $\tilde O(N^{\varrho})$ | $\tilde O(N^{1+\varrho})$ | $c$-ANN with probability | cosine |
| IVF | $O(\sqrt{Nn_{\text{probe}}}d)$ | $O(Nd)$ | none | $\ell^2$, inner product |
| IVF + PQ | as IVF, smaller constant | $O(Nmb/8)$ bytes | none | $\ell^2$, inner product |
| HNSW | $O(\log N)$ empirical | $O(NM)$ links $+ O(Nd)$ | none | $\ell^2$, cosine, inner product |
