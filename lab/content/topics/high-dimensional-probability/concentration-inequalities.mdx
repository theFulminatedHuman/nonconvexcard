Every inequality on this page bounds the same kind of quantity — the probability that a random
variable is far from its mean — and each pays for its conclusion with a different hypothesis. The
table is the summary; the rest of the page is the proofs.

| Inequality | Hypothesis | Tail bound on $\P(|Z - \E Z| \ge t)$ | Decay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Markov | $Z \ge 0$ | $\E Z/t$ (one-sided, about 0) | $1/t$ |
| Chebyshev | $\Var Z < \infty$ | $\sigma^2/t^2$ | $1/t^2$ |
| Chernoff | MGF finite | $e^{-\sup_\lambda(\lambda t - \psi(\lambda))}$ | shape of $\psi^*$ |
| Hoeffding | $Z = \sum X_i$, independent, $X_i \in [a_i,b_i]$ | $2\exp\!\big(-2t^2/\sum(b_i-a_i)^2\big)$ | Gaussian |
| Bernstein | as Hoeffding, plus variance $\sigma^2$ | $2\exp\!\big(-t^2/(2\sigma^2 + 2Mt/3)\big)$ | Gaussian then exponential |
| Azuma | martingale, increments in $[-c_i,c_i]$ | $2\exp\!\big(-t^2/(2\sum c_i^2)\big)$ | Gaussian |
| McDiarmid | $f$ with bounded differences $c_i$ | $2\exp\!\big(-2t^2/\sum c_i^2\big)$ | Gaussian |
| Gaussian concentration | $f$ $K$-Lipschitz, $X\sim\Ncal(0,I_d)$ | $2\exp\!\big(-t^2/(2K^2)\big)$ | Gaussian, dimension-free |

## The two moment inequalities

<Theorem id="markov" title="Markov's inequality">
Let $Z \ge 0$ be a random variable and $t > 0$. Then
$$
\P(Z \ge t) \le \frac{\E Z}{t}.
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Pointwise, $Z \ge t\,\one\{Z \ge t\}$: on the event $\{Z \ge t\}$ the right side is $t \le Z$, and
off it the right side is $0 \le Z$ by non-negativity. Taking expectations and using monotonicity of
the integral, $\E Z \ge t\,\P(Z \ge t)$.
</Proof>

<Assumptions>
Non-negativity of $Z$, and that is all. No independence, no variance, no boundedness. This is why
Markov is the foundation on which every other inequality here is built: all of them are Markov
applied to a cleverly chosen non-negative function of $Z$.
</Assumptions>

<Corollary id="chebyshev" title="Chebyshev's inequality">
If $\E Z^2 < \infty$ then for all $t>0$,
$$
\P\big(|Z - \E Z| \ge t\big) \le \frac{\Var(Z)}{t^2}.
$$
</Corollary>

<Proof>
Apply Markov to the non-negative variable $(Z - \E Z)^2$ at level $t^2$:
$$
\P(|Z - \E Z| \ge t) = \P\big((Z-\E Z)^2 \ge t^2\big) \le \frac{\E (Z-\E Z)^2}{t^2} = \frac{\Var Z}{t^2}.
$$
</Proof>

More generally, applying Markov to $|Z - \E Z|^p$ gives $\P(|Z-\E Z| \ge t) \le \E|Z-\E Z|^p/t^p$, so
each finite moment buys one more power of $t$. Sub-Gaussianity is, in this light, exactly the
statement that *all* moments are available with the right growth, which is what upgrades polynomial
decay to Gaussian decay.

<Proposition id="chebyshev-optimal" title="Markov and Chebyshev are attained">
Both bounds are achieved with equality by explicit distributions, so neither can be improved without
strengthening its hypotheses.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
For Markov: fix $t > 0$ and $p \in (0,1]$, and let $Z = t$ with probability $p$ and $Z = 0$ otherwise.
Then $\E Z = pt$ and $\P(Z \ge t) = p = \E Z/t$ exactly.

For Chebyshev: let $Z = \pm t$ each with probability $p/2$ and $Z = 0$ with probability $1-p$, for
$p \in (0,1]$. Then $\E Z = 0$, $\Var Z = pt^2$, and
$\P(|Z| \ge t) = p = \Var(Z)/t^2$ exactly.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
These extremal distributions are worth staring at: they put all of their deviation mass at exactly
the threshold $t$. That is what a distribution with *only* a variance constraint is allowed to do,
and it is why Chebyshev cannot see the difference between a Gaussian and a two-point distribution.
Improving on Chebyshev requires a hypothesis that rules such distributions out — boundedness of many
independent summands does exactly that.
</KeyIdea>

## The Chernoff method

<Proposition id="chernoff-method" title="The Chernoff method">
Let $Z$ have cumulant generating function $\psi(\lambda) = \log \E e^{\lambda Z}$, finite on an
interval containing $0$. Then for every $t$,
$$
\P(Z \ge t) \le \exp\big(-\psi^*(t)\big),
\qquad \psi^*(t) = \sup_{\lambda > 0}\big(\lambda t - \psi(\lambda)\big),
$$
where $\psi^*$ is the Legendre transform of $\psi$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
For any $\lambda > 0$ the map $z \mapsto e^{\lambda z}$ is increasing, so $\{Z \ge t\}$ and
$\{e^{\lambda Z} \ge e^{\lambda t}\}$ are the same event. Markov applied to the non-negative variable
$e^{\lambda Z}$ gives
$$
\P(Z \ge t) \le e^{-\lambda t}\,\E e^{\lambda Z} = \exp\big(\psi(\lambda) - \lambda t\big).
$$
The left side does not depend on $\lambda$, so we may take the infimum of the right side over
$\lambda>0$, which is $\exp(-\sup_{\lambda>0}(\lambda t - \psi(\lambda)))$.
</Proof>

<Intuition>
The Chernoff recipe is: exponentiate, apply Markov, optimise. The optimisation is what turns a
one-parameter family of weak bounds into a sharp one, and the resulting exponent $\psi^*$ is exactly
the rate function of large deviations theory. Everything below is this recipe plus a bound on
$\psi$.
</Intuition>

## Hoeffding's inequality

<Theorem id="hoeffding" title="Hoeffding's inequality">
Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be independent with $X_i \in [a_i,b_i]$ almost surely, and let
$S = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$. Then for every $t \ge 0$,
$$
\P\big(S - \E S \ge t\big) \le \exp\left(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (b_i-a_i)^2}\right),
$$
and the two-sided bound holds with an extra factor $2$. In particular, for i.i.d. $X_i \in [0,1]$ and
$\bar X_n = S/n$,
$$
\P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge t\big) \le 2e^{-2nt^2}.
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Assume without loss of generality that each $X_i$ is centred (replace $X_i$ by $X_i - \E X_i$, which
shifts the interval but not its length). By independence the MGF factorises, and by
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/sub-gaussian-random-variables">Hoeffding's lemma</TopicRef>
each factor is bounded:
$$
\E e^{\lambda S} = \prod_{i=1}^n \E e^{\lambda X_i}
\le \prod_{i=1}^n \exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2(b_i-a_i)^2}{8}\right)
= \exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2 v}{8}\right),
\qquad v := \sum_i (b_i-a_i)^2 .
$$
So $\psi(\lambda) \le \lambda^2 v/8$. Apply the Chernoff method: for $\lambda > 0$,
$$
\P(S \ge t) \le \exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2 v}{8} - \lambda t\right).
$$
The exponent is a quadratic in $\lambda$ minimised at $\lambda_\star = 4t/v$, where its value is
$$
\frac{(4t/v)^2 v}{8} - \frac{4t^2}{v} = \frac{2t^2}{v} - \frac{4t^2}{v} = -\frac{2t^2}{v}.
$$
This gives the one-sided bound. Applying it to $-S$ (whose summands lie in $[-b_i,-a_i]$, of the same
length) and using the union bound gives the factor $2$.
</Proof>

<NotSaid>
Hoeffding's bound does not involve the variance of the $X_i$ at all — only the lengths of their
ranges. For a Bernoulli$(p)$ variable with small $p$, the range is $1$ while the variance is
$p(1-p) \ll 1$, and Hoeffding is correspondingly loose. It is also useless for unbounded variables:
there is no version of this theorem for Gaussian summands *via this route*, though of course the sum
of Gaussians is exactly Gaussian.
</NotSaid>

Inverting the bound is the form used in practice. Setting the right-hand side to $\delta$ and solving
for $t$: with probability at least $1-\delta$,
$$
|\bar X_n - \mu| \le \sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}\qquad\text{for } X_i \in [0,1].
$$
Note the shape: the dependence on the confidence level is $\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}$, which is extremely
mild — buying three more orders of magnitude of confidence costs a factor of about $1.6$ in the
bound. This is the practical content of exponential concentration.

## Bernstein's inequality

<Theorem id="bernstein" title="Bernstein's inequality">
Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be independent and centred with $|X_i| \le M$ almost surely, and set
$\sigma^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \E X_i^2$. Then for all $t \ge 0$,
$$
\P\left(\sum_i X_i \ge t\right) \le \exp\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\sigma^2 + \tfrac{2}{3}Mt}\right).
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Fix $\lambda > 0$ and bound the MGF of a single summand. Using the series for the exponential and
$\E X_i = 0$,
$$
\E e^{\lambda X_i} = 1 + \sum_{k \ge 2}\frac{\lambda^k \E X_i^k}{k!}
\le 1 + \E X_i^2 \sum_{k\ge2}\frac{\lambda^k M^{k-2}}{k!},
$$
where we used $|\E X_i^k| \le \E\big[X_i^2 |X_i|^{k-2}\big] \le M^{k-2}\E X_i^2$ for $k \ge 2$.

Now bound the numerical series. Since $k! \ge 2\cdot 3^{k-2}$ for $k \ge 2$,
$$
\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{\lambda^kM^{k-2}}{k!}
\le \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\sum_{k \ge 2}\left(\frac{\lambda M}{3}\right)^{k-2}
= \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - \lambda M/3}
\qquad\text{for } \lambda M < 3 .
$$
Hence, with $1 + u \le e^u$,
$$
\E e^{\lambda X_i} \le \exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2\,\E X_i^2}{2(1-\lambda M/3)}\right),
$$
and multiplying over $i$ (independence) gives
$$
\psi(\lambda) = \log\E e^{\lambda\sum_i X_i} \le \frac{\lambda^2\sigma^2}{2(1 - \lambda M/3)} .
$$
Chernoff then gives $\P(\sum_i X_i \ge t) \le \exp\big(\lambda^2\sigma^2/(2(1-\lambda M/3)) - \lambda t\big)$.
Choose
$$
\lambda_\star = \frac{t}{\sigma^2 + Mt/3} \in (0, 3/M),
$$
and substitute. Writing $D = \sigma^2 + Mt/3$, we have $1 - \lambda_\star M/3 = (D - Mt/3)/D = \sigma^2/D$,
so the exponent becomes
$$
\frac{(t/D)^2\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2/D} - \frac{t^2}{D} = \frac{t^2}{2D} - \frac{t^2}{D} = -\frac{t^2}{2D}
= -\frac{t^2}{2\sigma^2 + \tfrac23 Mt},
$$
which is the claim.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Read the denominator. For $t \lesssim \sigma^2/M$ the term $2\sigma^2$ dominates and the bound is
$e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$ — **Gaussian**, with the true variance, not the range. For $t \gtrsim \sigma^2/M$
the term $\tfrac23Mt$ dominates and the bound is $e^{-3t/2M}$ — **exponential**. Bernstein therefore
interpolates between the two behaviours, with the crossover at the scale set by the ratio of variance
to range. Hoeffding only ever gives the first shape, with $\sigma^2$ replaced by the worst case
compatible with the range.
</KeyIdea>

<Note title="Which to use">
Use Hoeffding when the variables are bounded and the variance is comparable to the square of the
range (fair coins, balanced labels). Use Bernstein when the variance is much smaller than the range
(rare events, sparse features, low-noise regression residuals). The
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-of-the-norm">norm concentration</TopicRef>
proof needs Bernstein, because squares of Gaussians are unbounded but sub-exponential, and there is a
version of the theorem above for sub-exponential summands with $M$ replaced by
$\max_i\norm{X_i}_{\psi_1}$.
</Note>

## Beyond independence: Azuma and McDiarmid

<Definition id="martingale" title="Martingale">
A sequence $(Z_0, Z_1, \dots, Z_n)$ adapted to a filtration
$\mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq \dots \subseteq \mathcal{F}_n$ is a **martingale** if each $Z_k$ is integrable
and $\E[Z_k \mid \mathcal{F}_{k-1}] = Z_{k-1}$ for all $k$. Its **increments** are
$D_k = Z_k - Z_{k-1}$, which satisfy $\E[D_k\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}] = 0$.
</Definition>

<Theorem id="azuma" title="Azuma–Hoeffding inequality">
Let $(Z_k)_{k\le n}$ be a martingale whose increments satisfy $|D_k| \le c_k$ almost surely. Then for
all $t\ge0$,
$$
\P\big(Z_n - Z_0 \ge t\big) \le \exp\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\sum_{k=1}^n c_k^2}\right).
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Condition and iterate. For $\lambda > 0$, the tower property gives
$$
\E e^{\lambda(Z_n - Z_0)}
= \E\Big[e^{\lambda(Z_{n-1}-Z_0)}\,\E\big[e^{\lambda D_n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\big]\Big],
$$
using that $e^{\lambda(Z_{n-1}-Z_0)}$ is $\mathcal{F}_{n-1}$-measurable and may be taken out.

Conditionally on $\mathcal{F}_{n-1}$, the increment $D_n$ has conditional mean $0$ and lies in the
interval $[-c_n, c_n]$ of length $2c_n$. Hoeffding's lemma applies verbatim to the conditional law —
its proof used only centring and boundedness — so
$$
\E\big[e^{\lambda D_n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\big] \le \exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2 (2c_n)^2}{8}\right)
= \exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2c_n^2}{2}\right).
$$
This factor is deterministic, so it comes out of the expectation, and iterating over
$k = n, n-1,\dots,1$ gives
$$
\E e^{\lambda(Z_n-Z_0)} \le \exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\sum_{k=1}^n c_k^2\right).
$$
Chernoff with $v = \sum_k c_k^2$: minimising $\lambda^2v/2 - \lambda t$ at $\lambda_\star = t/v$ gives
the exponent $-t^2/(2v)$.
</Proof>

<Note title="What was bought">
No independence is assumed anywhere — only that each increment is conditionally centred and bounded.
This is what makes Azuma the right tool for sequential processes: SGD iterates, online learning
regret, and the Doob martingale used in the next proof.
</Note>

<Theorem id="mcdiarmid" title="McDiarmid's bounded differences inequality">
Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be independent random variables with $X_i$ taking values in a set $\Xcal_i$, and
let $f : \prod_i \Xcal_i \to \R$ satisfy the **bounded differences** condition: for each $i$ there is
$c_i$ with
$$
\big|f(x_1,\dots,x_i,\dots,x_n) - f(x_1,\dots,x_i',\dots,x_n)\big| \le c_i
$$
for all $x \in \prod_j \Xcal_j$ and all $x_i' \in \Xcal_i$. Then for $Z = f(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ and every
$t \ge 0$,
$$
\P\big(Z - \E Z \ge t\big) \le \exp\left(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2}\right).
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Build the **Doob martingale**: let $\mathcal{F}_k = \sigma(X_1,\dots,X_k)$ and
$$
Z_k = \E[Z \mid \mathcal{F}_k], \qquad k = 0,1,\dots,n,
$$
so $Z_0 = \E Z$ and $Z_n = Z$ (the latter because $Z$ is $\mathcal{F}_n$-measurable). The tower
property makes $(Z_k)$ a martingale, with increments $D_k = Z_k - Z_{k-1}$.

It remains to show $D_k$ lies in an interval of length at most $c_k$, after which Azuma with
increments in an interval of length $c_k$ — that is, Hoeffding's lemma giving the factor
$e^{\lambda^2c_k^2/8}$ — yields the exponent $-2t^2/\sum_k c_k^2$.

Define, for $x_k \in \Xcal_k$,
$$
g_k(x_k) = \E\big[Z \mid X_1,\dots,X_{k-1}, X_k = x_k\big],
$$
which by independence is an average of $f$ over $X_{k+1},\dots,X_n$ with the first $k$ coordinates
fixed. Then $Z_k = g_k(X_k)$ and $Z_{k-1} = \E_{X_k}\big[g_k(X_k)\big]$, again using independence of
$X_k$ from $\mathcal{F}_{k-1}$. Set
$$
L_k = \inf_{x_k} g_k(x_k), \qquad U_k = \sup_{x_k}g_k(x_k).
$$
Both $Z_k$ and $Z_{k-1}$ lie in $[L_k, U_k]$ — the latter because an average lies between the
infimum and the supremum — so $D_k \in [L_k - U_k, U_k - L_k]$. Moreover, for any $x_k, x_k'$,
$$
\big|g_k(x_k) - g_k(x_k')\big|
= \Big|\E_{X_{k+1:n}}\big[f(\dots,x_k,\dots) - f(\dots,x_k',\dots)\big]\Big| \le c_k
$$
by the bounded-differences hypothesis applied pointwise inside the expectation. Hence
$U_k - L_k \le c_k$, so $D_k$ is a conditionally centred random variable supported on an interval of
length at most $c_k$, and Hoeffding's lemma gives
$\E[e^{\lambda D_k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}] \le e^{\lambda^2c_k^2/8}$. Iterating as in the Azuma proof
and optimising over $\lambda$ completes the argument.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
McDiarmid is the reason
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/erm-and-uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</TopicRef> arguments
work. The quantity of interest there is
$Z = \sup_{f\in\Fcal}\big(R(f) - \hat R_n(f)\big)$, which is a complicated function of the whole
sample — but changing one sample point changes each empirical risk by at most $1/n$, and the
supremum is $1$-Lipschitz in the uniform norm, so $Z$ has bounded differences $c_i = 1/n$. McDiarmid
then concentrates $Z$ around its mean at rate $e^{-2nt^2}$, and the remaining work — bounding
$\E Z$ — is what Rademacher complexity does.
</KeyIdea>

## Gaussian concentration

<Theorem id="gaussian-concentration" title="Gaussian concentration for Lipschitz functions">
Let $X \sim \Ncal(0, I_d)$ and let $f : \R^d \to \R$ be $K$-Lipschitz with respect to
$\norm{\cdot}_2$. Then
$$
\P\big(|f(X) - \E f(X)| \ge t\big) \le 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^2}{2K^2}\right)
\qquad\text{for all } t\ge0 ,
$$
equivalently $\norm{f(X) - \E f(X)}_{\psi_2} \le CK$ for an absolute constant $C$.
</Theorem>

<Proof sketch>
The standard proofs go through either the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality or the Gaussian
logarithmic Sobolev inequality; both are substantial and are not reproduced here. The
semigroup/log-Sobolev route in outline: the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroup
$P_s f(x) = \E f(e^{-s}x + \sqrt{1-e^{-2s}}\,G)$ interpolates between $f$ and $\E f$, one shows the
Gaussian log-Sobolev inequality
$\operatorname{Ent}(g^2) \le 2\,\E\norm{\nabla g}_2^2$ for the Gaussian measure, and then Herbst's
argument converts that entropy bound into the MGF bound $\E e^{\lambda(f - \E f)} \le e^{\lambda^2K^2/2}$,
from which Chernoff gives the stated tail. The references contain complete proofs.
</Proof>

<NotSaid>
The dimension $d$ appears **nowhere** in the conclusion. That is the whole point, and it is the
single most important structural fact in high-dimensional probability. But read the hypothesis
carefully: $f$ must be Lipschitz *in the Euclidean norm*, and many natural functions are not
Lipschitz with a dimension-free constant. $f(x) = \sum_i x_i$ is $\sqrt d$-Lipschitz, and the theorem
correctly returns a bound with a $d$ in it. The theorem is dimension-free; individual applications
are only as dimension-free as their Lipschitz constants.

The theorem also says nothing about $\E f(X)$, which is often the harder quantity. Concentration
tells you the value is near *some* number; identifying that number is a separate problem.
</NotSaid>

<Corollary id="gc-norm" title="Immediate consequence: the norm concentrates">
$f(x) = \norm{x}_2$ is $1$-Lipschitz, so for $X \sim \Ncal(0,I_d)$,
$$
\P\big(|\norm{X}_2 - \E\norm{X}_2| \ge t\big) \le 2e^{-t^2/2},
$$
with no dependence on $d$ whatsoever. Since $\E\norm{X}_2 = \sqrt d\,(1 + O(1/d))$, this is the
statement explored numerically on the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/concentration-of-the-norm">norm concentration</TopicRef>
page.
</Corollary>
