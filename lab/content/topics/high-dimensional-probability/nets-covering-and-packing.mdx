<Intuition>
Concentration inequalities control one random variable at a time. Almost every question in this
subject is really about a supremum: the operator norm is a supremum over the sphere, a generalisation
bound is a supremum over a function class, the worst-case distortion of a random projection is a
supremum over pairs. The union bound handles a *finite* supremum, at a cost of $\log(\text{number of
terms})$. Nets are how an infinite supremum is turned into a finite one.
</Intuition>

## Definitions

<Definition id="net-def" title="Nets, covering and packing numbers">
Let $(T,\rho)$ be a metric space and $\eps>0$.

- $\Ncal \subseteq T$ is an **$\eps$-net** of $T$ if every $x \in T$ has some $y \in \Ncal$ with $\rho(x,y)\le\eps$.
- The **covering number** $N(T,\rho,\eps)$ is the smallest cardinality of an $\eps$-net.
- $\mathcal{P}\subseteq T$ is **$\eps$-separated** if $\rho(x,y) > \eps$ for all distinct $x,y\in\mathcal{P}$; the **packing number** $P(T,\rho,\eps)$ is the largest cardinality of an $\eps$-separated set.
- $\log N(T,\rho,\eps)$ is the **metric entropy** of $T$ at scale $\eps$.
</Definition>

<Lemma id="packing-covering" title="Packing and covering numbers are equivalent">
For every $\eps>0$,
$$
P(T,\rho,2\eps) \le N(T,\rho,\eps) \le P(T,\rho,\eps).
$$
</Lemma>

<Proof>
**Right inequality.** Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a maximal $\eps$-separated set (maximal in the sense that
no point can be added). Maximality means every $x \in T$ is within $\eps$ of some point of
$\mathcal{P}$ — otherwise $x$ could be added — so $\mathcal{P}$ is an $\eps$-net, giving
$N \le |\mathcal{P}| = P(T,\rho,\eps)$.

**Left inequality.** Let $\Ncal$ be an $\eps$-net and $\mathcal{P}$ a $2\eps$-separated set. Map each
$x\in\mathcal{P}$ to some $y(x)\in\Ncal$ with $\rho(x,y(x))\le\eps$. This map is injective: if
$y(x_1)=y(x_2)$ for $x_1\ne x_2$ then $\rho(x_1,x_2) \le \rho(x_1,y)+\rho(y,x_2)\le 2\eps$,
contradicting $2\eps$-separation. Hence $|\mathcal{P}| \le |\Ncal|$.
</Proof>

So the two notions are interchangeable up to a factor of $2$ in the scale, and since every bound we
prove is stated up to constants and logarithms, they may be used interchangeably. The practical
consequence: to *upper bound* a covering number, exhibit a net; to *lower bound* it, exhibit a
separated set.

## The volumetric argument

<Theorem id="volumetric" title="Volumetric bound on covering numbers">
Let $B^d$ be the unit Euclidean ball in $\R^d$ and $\eps \in (0,1)$. Then
$$
\left(\frac1\eps\right)^d \;\le\; N(B^d, \norm{\cdot}_2, \eps) \;\le\; \left(\frac{3}{\eps}\right)^d .
$$
The same bounds, with the same proof, hold for the sphere $S^{d-1}$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
**Lower bound.** Let $\Ncal$ be an $\eps$-net of $B^d$. The balls $\{y + \eps B^d : y \in \Ncal\}$
cover $B^d$, so comparing volumes,
$$
\operatorname{vol}(B^d) \le |\Ncal|\cdot\operatorname{vol}(\eps B^d) = |\Ncal|\,\eps^d \operatorname{vol}(B^d),
$$
because volume in $\R^d$ scales as the $d$-th power of the dilation factor. Dividing gives
$|\Ncal| \ge \eps^{-d}$.

**Upper bound.** Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a maximal $\eps$-separated subset of $B^d$; by the previous
lemma it is an $\eps$-net, so it suffices to bound $|\mathcal{P}|$. The balls
$\{x + \tfrac\eps2 B^d : x \in \mathcal{P}\}$ are pairwise disjoint (their centres are more than
$\eps$ apart) and all contained in $(1+\tfrac\eps2)B^d$. Comparing volumes,
$$
|\mathcal{P}|\left(\frac\eps2\right)^d \operatorname{vol}(B^d) \le \left(1+\frac\eps2\right)^d\operatorname{vol}(B^d),
$$
so
$$
|\mathcal{P}| \le \left(\frac{2 + \eps}{\eps}\right)^d \le \left(\frac3\eps\right)^d
$$
using $\eps \le 1$.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
Both bounds are $\eps^{-d}$ up to the base of the exponent. So metric entropy of the ball is
$$
\log N(B^d,\norm{\cdot}_2,\eps) = d\log\frac1\eps + O(d),
$$
**linear in the dimension** and only logarithmic in the accuracy. This single fact is why net
arguments are useful: the union bound costs $\log(\text{net size}) \approx d\log(1/\eps)$, which is
paid against a concentration exponent that is typically of order $n$ or $t^2$ — so the argument
succeeds precisely when the sample size beats the dimension.
</KeyIdea>

## Using a net: the operator norm

<Lemma id="net-operator-norm" title="Operator norm from a net">
Let $A \in \R^{m\times d}$, let $\eps \in [0,1/2)$ and let $\Ncal$ be an $\eps$-net of $S^{d-1}$.
Then
$$
\sup_{x \in \Ncal}\norm{Ax}_2 \;\le\; \norm{A} \;\le\; \frac{1}{1-\eps}\,\sup_{x\in\Ncal}\norm{Ax}_2 .
$$
In particular a $\tfrac12$-net gives $\norm{A} \le 2\max_{x\in\Ncal}\norm{Ax}_2$.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
The left inequality is immediate since $\Ncal \subseteq S^{d-1}$.

For the right, let $x \in S^{d-1}$ attain $\norm{Ax}_2 = \norm{A}$ (the supremum is attained by
compactness). Choose $y\in\Ncal$ with $\norm{x-y}_2\le\eps$. Then
$$
\norm{A} = \norm{Ax}_2 \le \norm{Ay}_2 + \norm{A(x-y)}_2
\le \sup_{z\in\Ncal}\norm{Az}_2 + \norm{A}\norm{x-y}_2
\le \sup_{z\in\Ncal}\norm{Az}_2 + \eps\norm{A},
$$
where the middle step used the definition of the operator norm on the vector $x - y$. Rearranging,
$(1-\eps)\norm{A} \le \sup_{z\in\Ncal}\norm{Az}_2$.
</Proof>

<Note title="The recipe">
This lemma plus the volumetric bound plus a concentration inequality is the entire proof of the
spectral-norm bound for random matrices on the
<TopicRef id="random-matrix-theory/spectral-norm-of-random-matrices">next section</TopicRef>:
1. reduce $\norm{A}$ to a maximum over a $\tfrac12$-net of size $\le 6^d$;
2. bound $\P(\norm{Ax}_2 \ge t)$ for each *fixed* $x$ by concentration;
3. union bound over the net, paying $6^d = e^{d\log 6}$;
4. choose $t$ so that the concentration exponent beats $d\log 6$.
That is the whole argument, and it generalises to any set whose covering numbers you can bound.
</Note>

## Chaining and Dudley's integral

The net argument above is lossy. It approximates every point of $S^{d-1}$ at a single scale $\eps$,
paying the full union-bound price at that scale. **Chaining** approximates at a sequence of
geometrically decreasing scales, paying a smaller price at each.

<Definition id="subgaussian-process" title="Sub-Gaussian process">
A stochastic process $(Z_t)_{t\in T}$ indexed by a metric space $(T,\rho)$ is **sub-Gaussian** if
$$
\norm{Z_t - Z_s}_{\psi_2} \le C\rho(t,s) \qquad\text{for all } s,t \in T.
$$
</Definition>

<Theorem id="dudley" title="Dudley's entropy integral">
For a mean-zero sub-Gaussian process $(Z_t)_{t\in T}$,
$$
\E\sup_{t\in T} Z_t \;\le\; C\int_0^\infty \sqrt{\log N(T,\rho,\eps)}\;d\eps .
$$
</Theorem>

The proof is the chaining argument: write $Z_t - Z_{t_0}$ as a telescoping sum along a sequence of
nets at scales $2^{-k}$, apply a union bound at each scale, and sum the resulting geometric series.
It is a beautiful argument and it is given in full in the references; it is stated here because the
*shape* of the conclusion is what gets used.

<Definition id="gaussian-width" title="Gaussian width">
For a bounded set $T \subseteq \R^d$ and $g \sim \Ncal(0,I_d)$, the **Gaussian width** is
$$
w(T) = \E\sup_{x\in T}\ip{g}{x}.
$$
</Definition>

Gaussian width is the single scalar that governs a remarkable range of high-dimensional estimates:
the operator norm of a random matrix restricted to $T$, the number of measurements needed for
compressed sensing of signals in $T$, the error of an estimator constrained to $T$. Two reference
values worth memorising:

$$
w(S^{d-1}) = \E\norm{g}_2 \approx \sqrt d, \qquad
w\big(\{x : \norm{x}_2 \le 1, \norm{x}_0 \le s\}\big) \lesssim \sqrt{s\log(d/s)} .
$$

The second is the reason sparse recovery needs $O(s\log(d/s))$ measurements rather than $O(d)$: the
complexity of the set of $s$-sparse unit vectors, measured by Gaussian width, is
$\sqrt{s\log(d/s)}$, not $\sqrt d$. The <TopicRef id="high-dimensional-statistics/sparse-recovery-and-the-lasso">sparse recovery</TopicRef>
page makes this quantitative.

<NotSaid>
Dudley's bound is not tight in general. There are sets for which
$\E\sup_t Z_t \ll \int_0^\infty\sqrt{\log N(T,\rho,\eps)}\,d\eps$, and the gap can be a factor of
$\sqrt{\log}$ or worse. Talagrand's generic chaining replaces the entropy integral with the
$\gamma_2$ functional, which does characterise $\E\sup_t Z_t$ for Gaussian processes (the majorising
measure theorem). For most applications on this site Dudley suffices; when it does not, the
reference is the right place to go.
</NotSaid>
