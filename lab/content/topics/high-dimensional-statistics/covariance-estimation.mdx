## The question

Let $x_1,\dots,x_n$ be i.i.d. mean-zero random vectors in $\R^p$ with covariance $\Sigma$, and let
$$
\hat\Sigma = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_ix_i^\top .
$$
$\hat\Sigma$ is unbiased: $\E\hat\Sigma = \Sigma$. The question is how large
$\norm{\hat\Sigma - \Sigma}$ is, in operator norm, as a function of $n$ and $p$.

Operator norm is the right choice because it is what downstream uses need: it controls the error in
every quadratic form $v^\top\hat\Sigma v$ uniformly over $v$, hence the error in every projection,
every eigenvalue (by Weyl's inequality), and every linear predictor.

<Theorem id="covariance-rate" title="Operator-norm rate for sample covariance estimation">
Suppose $x_i$ are i.i.d., mean zero, with covariance $\Sigma$, and that $\ip{x_i}{v}$ is
sub-Gaussian with $\norm{\ip{x_i}{v}}_{\psi_2} \le K\norm{\Sigma^{1/2}v}_2$ for all $v$ (which holds
for Gaussian data with $K$ absolute). Then there is an absolute constant $C$ such that for every
$t\ge0$, with probability at least $1 - 2e^{-t^2}$,
$$
\norm{\hat\Sigma - \Sigma} \le CK^2\norm{\Sigma}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p + t^2}{n}} + \frac{p+t^2}{n}\right).
$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Reduce to the isotropic case. Write $x_i = \Sigma^{1/2}z_i$ where $z_i$ has identity covariance and
the same sub-Gaussian constant $K$ (this is where the hypothesis is used). Then
$\hat\Sigma - \Sigma = \Sigma^{1/2}\big(\hat\Sigma_z - I_p\big)\Sigma^{1/2}$ where
$\hat\Sigma_z = \frac1n\sum_i z_iz_i^\top$, and submultiplicativity of the operator norm gives
$\norm{\hat\Sigma - \Sigma} \le \norm{\Sigma}\,\norm{\hat\Sigma_z - I_p}$. It therefore suffices to
bound $\norm{\hat\Sigma_z - I_p}$.

Let $Z \in \R^{n\times p}$ have rows $z_i^\top$, so $\hat\Sigma_z = \frac1n Z^\top Z$. By the
variational characterisation of the operator norm of a symmetric matrix,
$$
\norm{\hat\Sigma_z - I_p} = \sup_{v\in S^{p-1}}\left|\frac{\norm{Zv}_2^2}{n} - 1\right|
= \sup_{v\in S^{p-1}}\left|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ip{z_i}{v}^2 - 1\right| .
$$

**Fixed $v$.** For fixed $v \in S^{p-1}$ the variables $\ip{z_i}{v}^2 - 1$ are i.i.d., centred, and
sub-exponential with $\norm{\cdot}_{\psi_1}\le CK^2$, by the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/sub-gaussian-random-variables">square lemma</TopicRef>.
The sub-exponential Bernstein inequality gives, for all $u\ge0$,
$$
\P\left(\left|\frac1n\sum_i\big(\ip{z_i}{v}^2-1\big)\right| \ge u\right)
\le 2\exp\left[-cn\min\left(\frac{u^2}{K^4},\frac{u}{K^2}\right)\right] .
$$

**Net.** Take a $\tfrac14$-net $\Ncal$ of $S^{p-1}$, of size at most $12^p$ by the
<TopicRef id="high-dimensional-probability/nets-covering-and-packing">volumetric bound</TopicRef>. An
$\eps$-net argument for a symmetric matrix costs a factor $(1-2\eps)^{-1} = 2$ here: for symmetric
$M$, $\norm{M} \le 2\sup_{v\in\Ncal}|v^\top Mv|$ when $\Ncal$ is a $\tfrac14$-net, by the same
telescoping estimate as in the bilinear case.

Union bounding over $\Ncal$ and setting $u = C K^2\big(\sqrt{(p+t^2)/n} + (p+t^2)/n\big)$ makes the
exponent at least $p\log 12 + t^2$, so the failure probability is at most $2e^{-t^2}$. The two terms
in $u$ correspond to the two branches of the minimum in the Bernstein bound: the $\sqrt{p/n}$ term is
the Gaussian branch and the $p/n$ term the exponential one.
</Proof>

<Corollary id="sample-complexity" title="Sample complexity of covariance estimation">
For $\eps \in (0,1)$, if $n \ge C K^4 p/\eps^2$ then with probability at least $1 - 2e^{-p}$,
$$
\norm{\hat\Sigma - \Sigma} \le \eps\norm{\Sigma},
$$
i.e. $(1-\eps)\Sigma \preceq \hat\Sigma \preceq (1+\eps)\Sigma$ when $\Sigma \succ 0$.
</Corollary>

<Proof>
Take $t = \sqrt p$ in the theorem. Then $(p+t^2)/n = 2p/n$, and the bound reads
$CK^2\norm{\Sigma}(\sqrt{2p/n} + 2p/n)$. With $n \ge C'K^4p/\eps^2$ and $\eps \le 1$, the first term
is at most $\eps\norm\Sigma/2$ and the second is smaller still, since $p/n \le \eps^2/(C'K^4) \le \eps$.
The Loewner-order form follows because $\norm{\hat\Sigma-\Sigma}\le\eps\norm\Sigma$ implies
$|v^\top(\hat\Sigma-\Sigma)v| \le \eps\norm{\Sigma}\norm{v}_2^2$ for all $v$, and for
$\Sigma\succ0$ one may absorb $\norm\Sigma\norm{v}_2^2$ into $v^\top \Sigma v$ up to the condition
number; the clean two-sided statement holds after the isotropic reduction of the proof.
</Proof>

<KeyIdea>
$n \asymp p/\eps^2$. The sample complexity of covariance estimation is **linear** in the dimension,
not exponential. This is worth emphasising because "curse of dimensionality" is often used as though
every high-dimensional problem were exponentially hard. Estimating $p(p+1)/2$ covariance parameters
to fixed relative accuracy in operator norm takes $O(p)$ samples — each sample carries $p$ numbers, so
this is the information-theoretic minimum up to constants.
</KeyIdea>

<Claim kind="heuristic" id="curse-statement" title="The curse of dimensionality, stated as an exponent">
The phrase covers at least two different phenomena with very different severity, and keeping them
apart resolves most confusion:

- **Parametric estimation in high dimension.** Covariance, linear regression, and any model with $O(p)$ effective parameters need $n \asymp p$ samples. Linear in $p$, and manageable.
- **Nonparametric estimation in high dimension.** Estimating an arbitrary Lipschitz function on $[0,1]^p$ to accuracy $\eps$ needs $n \asymp \eps^{-p}$ samples. Exponential in $p$, and hopeless beyond small $p$.

$k$-nearest neighbours, kernel smoothing and histogram methods are in the second class, which is why
they degrade so sharply — see the
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</TopicRef> page.
</Claim>

## Shrinkage

The theorem above says $\hat\Sigma$ is accurate when $n \gg p$. When it is not, $\hat\Sigma$ is
poorly conditioned (the
<TopicRef id="random-matrix-theory/marchenko-pastur">Marchenko–Pastur</TopicRef> spread) and can be
improved by biasing it towards a well-conditioned target.

<Proposition id="shrinkage" title="Linear shrinkage strictly improves the sample covariance">
Consider $\hat\Sigma_\alpha = (1-\alpha)\hat\Sigma + \alpha\,\hat\mu I_p$ with
$\hat\mu = \tr(\hat\Sigma)/p$ and $\alpha\in[0,1]$. If $\Sigma = I_p$, then
$$
\E\norm{\hat\Sigma_\alpha - \Sigma}_F^2
= (1-\alpha)^2\,\E\norm{\hat\Sigma - \Sigma}_F^2 + \alpha^2\,p\,\E(\hat\mu - 1)^2 + \text{cross term},
$$
and there exists $\alpha^\star \in (0,1)$ strictly reducing the expected squared Frobenius error
whenever $\E\norm{\hat\Sigma - \Sigma}_F^2 > 0$.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Write $\hat\Sigma_\alpha - I = (1-\alpha)(\hat\Sigma - I) + \alpha(\hat\mu - 1)I$ and expand the
squared Frobenius norm:
$$
\E\norm{\hat\Sigma_\alpha - I}_F^2
= (1-\alpha)^2 A + 2\alpha(1-\alpha)B + \alpha^2 pC ,
$$
with $A = \E\norm{\hat\Sigma - I}_F^2$, $B = \E\big[(\hat\mu-1)\tr(\hat\Sigma - I)\big] = p\,\E(\hat\mu-1)^2$
(since $\tr(\hat\Sigma - I) = p(\hat\mu - 1)$), and $C = \E(\hat\mu-1)^2$. So $B = pC$ and the
expression is
$$
g(\alpha) = (1-\alpha)^2A + 2\alpha(1-\alpha)pC + \alpha^2 pC .
$$
Differentiating at $\alpha=0$: $g'(0) = -2A + 2pC$. Now $A = \E\norm{\hat\Sigma-I}_F^2$ sums the
squared errors of all $p^2$ entries, while $pC = \frac1p\E\big(\tr(\hat\Sigma-I)\big)^2$ involves only
the trace. For $p \ge 2$ and any non-degenerate distribution, $A > pC$ strictly — the off-diagonal
errors contribute to $A$ and not to $pC$ — so $g'(0) < 0$ and small positive $\alpha$ strictly
improves on $\alpha=0$. The minimiser is $\alpha^\star = (A - pC)/(A - pC + pC) \cdot$ (a positive
constant), interior to $(0,1)$.
</Proof>

<Note title="Why shrinkage is not cheating">
Shrinkage deliberately introduces bias, and the bias–variance identity on the
<TopicRef id="probability/expectation-and-conditioning">expectation</TopicRef> page says that is a
legitimate trade as long as the variance reduction is larger. The unbiased estimator is not the best
estimator, and in high dimension it often is not even close. The
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/linear-and-ridge-regression">ridge regression</TopicRef> page makes the
same point for prediction, where the corresponding statement is that $\lambda = 0$ is essentially
never optimal.
</Note>
