## What problem does this paper solve?

Overfitting in overparameterised networks, at a time when explicit regularisers ($L^2$, early
stopping) were insufficient and ensembling was too expensive.

## What mathematical objects are introduced?

A random mask $m\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-p)^{\dim}$ applied elementwise:
$$
\tilde a = \frac{1}{1-p}\,m\odot a \quad\text{(inverted dropout, so that } \E\tilde a = a).
$$
At test time the mask is dropped and no rescaling is needed.

## What assumptions are made?

That the ensemble average is well approximated by the single "mean network" obtained by using all
units with scaled weights. This is **exact** for a linear model and only approximate once
nonlinearities are present.

## What equations matter?

The exact case is instructive. For a linear model with dropout on the inputs, the expected squared
loss is
$$
\E_m\big\|y - w^\top(m\odot x)/(1-p)\big\|^2
= \norm{y - w^\top x}^2 + \frac{p}{1-p}\sum_j w_j^2 x_j^2 ,
$$
so dropout on a linear model is exactly a data-dependent
<TopicRef id="machine-learning/linear-and-ridge-regression">ridge penalty</TopicRef> with
per-feature weights $x_j^2$. This identity is the only rigorous characterisation of dropout available,
and it does not extend to deep networks.

## What theorem or result is important?

No theorem for the deep case. Empirically, consistent gains across vision, speech and text
benchmarks, with $p=0.5$ on fully-connected layers becoming the default.

## What proof techniques are used?

The linear-model expectation computation above; otherwise experiment.

## What prior mathematics is required?

Expectation of a quadratic form under a Bernoulli mask; the
<TopicRef id="probability/expectation-and-conditioning">bias–variance</TopicRef> framing.

## Limitations

The ensemble interpretation is heuristic for nonlinear networks. Dropout interacts badly with
<TopicRef id="deep-learning/convolution-recurrence-normalisation">batch normalisation</TopicRef> (the
variance shift between train and test compounds), and it is largely absent from modern large
transformers, where data scale makes explicit regularisation less necessary.

## Why it mattered

It was the default regulariser of the 2012–2018 period and remains standard in smaller models. The
linear-model identity is also a useful reminder that a "stochastic trick" can often be resolved into
an explicit penalty, which is the right first question to ask of any such method.
