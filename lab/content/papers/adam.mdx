## What problem does this paper solve?

Practical optimisation of non-stationary stochastic objectives with sparse or badly-scaled gradients,
without per-layer learning-rate tuning.

## What mathematical objects are introduced?

First and second moment estimates with bias correction:
$$
m_t = \beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t, \quad
v_t = \beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^2, \quad
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\frac{m_t/(1-\beta_1^t)}{\sqrt{v_t/(1-\beta_2^t)}+\eps}.
$$

## What assumptions are made?

The convergence analysis in the paper assumes convex $f_t$, bounded gradients, and a condition on
$\beta_1,\beta_2$. Those hypotheses turn out not to be sufficient.

## What equations matter?

The <TopicRef id="stochastic-optimization/adaptive-methods">bias-correction derivation</TopicRef>:
$\E m_t = (1-\beta_1^t)\E g$, so the division by $1-\beta_1^t$ is exactly unbiasing. And the
scale invariance: replacing $g$ by $cg$ leaves $m_t/\sqrt{v_t}$ unchanged, which is why Adam is
insensitive to loss scaling.

## What theorem or result is important?

The paper states an $O(\sqrt T)$ regret bound. **That theorem is false as stated**: Reddi, Kale and
Kumar give a convex counterexample on which Adam's average regret does not vanish, and the error in
the original proof is in the handling of the changing preconditioner. Their AMSGrad variant, using a
running maximum of $v_t$, restores a correct proof.

## What proof techniques are used?

An online-convex-optimisation regret argument in the style of AdaGrad's analysis.

## What prior mathematics is required?

Exponential moving averages and their bias; the
<TopicRef id="stochastic-optimization/adaptive-methods">AdaGrad regret argument</TopicRef>; the
distinction between expectation and high-probability guarantees.

## Limitations

The published convergence proof is incorrect. $L^2$ regularisation and weight decay are not
equivalent under Adam's preconditioner, which AdamW later fixed. Adam sometimes generalises worse
than tuned SGD with momentum on vision benchmarks, an effect that is documented and not explained.

## Why it mattered

It is the default optimiser for essentially all language-model training. It is also the clearest
example in the field of a method whose empirical value is established and whose theoretical
justification was wrong — and which nobody stopped using when that was discovered, for entirely
defensible reasons.
