## What problem does this paper solve?

Generative modelling without a tractable density. A GAN's generator is a deterministic map
$G:\R^k\to\Xcal$ pushing a simple noise distribution forward; its density is not available in closed
form, and maximum likelihood is therefore unusable.

## What mathematical objects are introduced?

The minimax objective
$$
\min_G\max_D\ \E_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(x)\big] + \E_{z\sim p_z}\big[\log\big(1-D(G(z))\big)\big].
$$

## What assumptions are made?

That $D$ and $G$ have unlimited capacity and that the inner maximisation is solved exactly — both
required for the equilibrium analysis and both false in practice.

## What equations matter?

For fixed $G$ with induced distribution $p_G$, the optimal discriminator is
$$
D^\star(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x)+p_G(x)},
$$
obtained by maximising the integrand pointwise. Substituting back gives the generator's objective as
$$
2\,\mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_G) - \log4 ,
$$
minimised exactly when $p_G = p_{\text{data}}$.

## What theorem or result is important?

The equilibrium characterisation above: the unique global optimum of the game is $p_G = p_{\text{data}}$
with $D^\star\equiv\tfrac12$. This is a genuine theorem, and its proof is the pointwise maximisation
plus the definition of Jensen–Shannon divergence.

## What proof techniques are used?

Pointwise optimisation of an integral; convexity of the objective in $p_G$ for the convergence
argument.

## What prior mathematics is required?

KL and Jensen–Shannon divergences; minimax problems and the gap between $\min\max$ and $\max\min$;
pushforward measures.

## Limitations

The theorem is about the equilibrium of the idealised game. Gradient descent–ascent on a non-convex
non-concave objective need not converge to it, and does not: mode collapse, oscillation and
discriminator overpowering are the standard failure modes. When $p_G$ and $p_{\text{data}}$ have
disjoint support the JSD is constant and the gradient vanishes — the observation that motivated
Wasserstein GANs.

## Why it mattered

It introduced adversarial training, dominated image generation for five years, and remains the
canonical example of a learning objective defined by a game rather than a likelihood. Its
displacement by <TopicRef id="deep-learning/approximation-and-backpropagation">diffusion
models</TopicRef> was driven precisely by the training instability the theory did not control.
