## What problem does this paper solve?

It establishes, at scale and across three axes, that test error is not U-shaped in capacity for deep
networks — resolving a persistent mismatch between classical theory and practice into a reproducible
phenomenon with a described shape.

## What mathematical objects are introduced?

**Effective model complexity** (EMC): the largest sample size on which the training procedure
achieves approximately zero training error. The paper's organising claim is that the peak in test
error occurs when EMC $\approx n$, whichever parameter is varied to get there.

## What assumptions are made?

That the interpolation threshold is the relevant control variable, and that the same mechanism
explains model-wise, sample-wise and epoch-wise double descent. The last is the boldest claim and the
least directly supported.

## What equations matter?

None derived. The paper is experimental. The relevant analysis is the linear model treated on the
<TopicRef id="learning-theory/bias-variance-and-double-descent">double-descent page</TopicRef>, where
the variance term is $\sigma^2\tr((X^\top X)^{-1}\Sigma)$ and diverges as
$\lambda_{\min}(X^\top X)\to0$ at $p=n$.

## What theorem or result is important?

No theorem. The empirical results: (i) test error peaks at the interpolation threshold and then falls;
(ii) *more data can hurt* at fixed model size, when the extra data moves the model towards the peak;
(iii) the peak grows with label noise and shrinks with explicit regularisation.

## What proof techniques are used?

None; controlled experiments with careful isolation of the varied quantity.

## What prior mathematics is required?

The bias–variance identity, the conditioning of random design matrices
(<TopicRef id="random-matrix-theory/marchenko-pastur">Marchenko–Pastur</TopicRef>), and the
minimum-norm interpolator.

## Limitations

The mechanism is not established for deep networks — EMC is a descriptive variable, not an
explanation. The results are sensitive to label noise, and adding optimal regularisation removes the
peak, which suggests the phenomenon is partly an artefact of insufficiently regularised training
rather than a fundamental feature. And the paper describes a risk curve; it does not explain why the
interpolating solution generalises.

## Why it mattered

It made "bigger is sometimes worse, and then better again" a fact to be explained rather than an
anomaly to be dismissed, and it provided the experimental grounding that the linear-model theory of
Hastie et al. and Bartlett et al. then matched.
